# Interview MWI-001

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Well, let me just give you a sample of my daily routine. At this moment, I am a housewife, and I have three children. My daily routine starts with Fajr prayer, being a Muslim, and some dhikr afterward, and then getting the kids ready for school — waking them up, helping them get ready, packing their lunch. That's the morning routine, and I drop them off at school; they have different timings. Then I come home, and I'm currently studying — I'm working on a certification, so I work on that. And obviously it's a lot of housework — nothing really important going on, just what a woman is expected to do: the cleaning, the house chores, I would say. Then prayer comes, and after prayer I definitely have time for reading the Quran and tafsir, learning about my faith. It's in my daily routine. Alhamdulillah, I've been maintaining that for quite a while now, and I'm kind of proud of that, before the kids come home. I work on that, and after the kids arrive we actually do have after-school programs for them — Quran classes at the mosque, two to four times a week, sometimes four times, and activities, sports they do go to. I try to keep them busy, keep myself busy, and at the same time maintain — being a woman — taking care of my own health and hygiene, working out, sports, anything that I can do for my health. Yeah, it's not a bad idea.

It's a lot — well, it sounds like a lot, but I mean, it is a lot. And then at the end of the day, sometimes I get a comment like, "What did you do today?" I'm like, sure, let me just start counting. Sometimes I do feel like a woman's work is 24-7, especially being a mom with three kids, and it's not as valuable — society doesn't really accept it as you doing something, you know what I mean? I get that from my own family members — they say, "What did you do today? You've been home all day long." It's like, oh, okay, so I did nothing. It's amazing, but I'm still grateful. I have love for everything; I enjoy being able to provide for the kids, not just financially but spiritually — I can put them in classes; they have a morning Quran and an afternoon Quran. My goal is for them to start with the morning Quran and finish with Quran in the evening — the least I can give them. My goal is to teach them the Quran so the Quran can teach them everything in life, inshallah.

Ameen, for all of us, but for all the Muslims, especially living in the West — a lot of the influences out here, the environment is not as ideal, right?

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

You know, I was actually very lucky. My community is full of Muslims, alhamdulillah, and I like to surround myself with Muslims, even though my husband's side of the family are not Muslims — I like to keep myself close to our community more than any other, because I noticed in the past — before marriage and even after marriage, I'll be honest — I surrounded myself with non-Muslims, and without me wanting it, I saw their lifestyle and culture and was kind of trying to fit in, or maybe adapting to it somehow without knowing it. Like I said before, I wasn't a hijabi — I was very, I guess, modern, and everything you'd see in any other woman outside, non-Muslim or non-practicing, you could see I was one of them. I don't want to put anybody down, but what I mean is you wouldn't know that I was a practicing woman, even though I was praying and doing all the other pillars I'm supposed to be doing — fasting, giving zakat, everything — but I wasn't properly showing myself as a Muslim woman. It's not because I was embarrassed or anything, but that's just where I grew up, this is where we come from — being modest wasn't part of our culture, I guess. It was like the opposite.

I grew up in Uzbekistan, and I grew up in Russia — I grew up in parts of Europe, because my father was around the world, so we moved around with him a lot. I never actually got exposed to modest Muslim women; everybody around me was Muslim, but at the same time everybody was like me, so I didn't really have modest Muslim women around me growing up. I wish it would have been different, but at the same time I'm really grateful it wasn't that way, because I don't take it for granted — I feel like I respect it more now than if I had been forced into it while growing up. I came to start wearing hijab and wearing more modest clothes by my own will; nobody forced me into it. I learned about it, and Allah guided me to do it — he gave me that option of choosing it. It's not like my parents or the society I grew up in forced me into it.

I do appreciate that a lot, and I'm grateful for that as well. I just want to stress that enough. I've seen a lot of young women who unfortunately were forced into hijab, but then later on they took it off. I've met so many of them, but wallahu a'lam — it's not for me to talk about other people's reasons; people have their own decisions. But my experience of coming into being more modest, becoming a Muslim woman, came with my own decision through Allah's guidance.

How would I describe it? I would say that I think Muslim women are the freest women on earth. That's what I would say, because unfortunately non-Muslims don't have as much freedom as Muslim women do — at least in my case, in the case of my own family, the people that I know — because they think we're oppressed, they judge us by...

Well, let me take that back — I would probably explain to non-Muslims that Muslim women have much more freedom than they think. Muslim women have much more choice than non-Muslims in terms of going to work or not going to work, cooking food or not cooking food, working and giving the money to the husband or not — you can own it. Even with breastfeeding — a lot of that is the woman's choice. I didn't know that a woman can even charge her husband for it. I grew a lot in the past five years, I would say. Before that I was, mashallah, in my la-la land — I was not a really good example for anybody. I'm still not a great example for a lot of the non-Muslim women out there, but I try. If they were to ask me one thing about Muslim women and if I had to describe them, I would describe them as faithful. They are pious, and at the same time they're very caring and responsible women.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

No, I didn't have it. I started seeing myself doing what I saw in my mom. Mashallah, she's very well educated, she's a teacher, and once she started having kids she gave up her career for them — she raised five kids. I remember growing up sometimes we'd say, "Why do you do that? I don't want to do that," but I remember how she was handling us — it wasn't like, "You've got to do it, I'm your mom." It wasn't like that. She was more understanding, giving us choices, and guiding us through it in a calm way.

I see that in my mom — she was able to take care of everything without going to work. My father was the provider; she never had to work. All she did was take care of the family, the kids, the household — she even had to take care of the in-laws — giving her best to her kids. That's what I saw in my mom. And after having a kid myself, I started doing what she was doing — I don't know, maybe it's in the blood — and I saw, "This is what I'm supposed to be doing." Nobody told me; at that point my husband wasn't even Muslim, but he supported me in a way — he didn't push me to go to work. I didn't hear that from him. Or maybe I trained him naturally — "Hey, I'm a mom now, I have to stay home and take care of my kids, that's my priority." I remember those kinds of conversations too — my kids are my priority.

He supported it, so I just naturally did what I saw. I didn't realize it until very recently, looking back — "Oh my gosh, this is exactly what my mom did, and this is what I'm doing too." Then I started thinking about whether I wanted to go all the way through like she did, because she never went back to work. Allah knows best — I'm trying to be a little different from her, because the timing is different, the environment is different, the place I'm living is different.

I've been living here maybe close to thirty years — I've been back and forth between different countries, but yeah, it's been close to thirty years.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes, I do. If you asked me maybe three years ago, I didn't even know the Arabic letters — I didn't know how to read Quran. I'd never read Quran. I'm not proud of it, but I'm not ashamed of it either, because I was never exposed to it. It's not an excuse, but I never thought, "We should be reading Quran daily" — that came later, once I started listening to sheikhs online, on YouTube. You can increase your knowledge that way. I started learning about the religion because my husband started questioning me on a few things and I couldn't give him an answer — I said, "I don't know, we were just told we should do it," but he didn't accept that as an answer. He said, "That's not really an answer — that means you've been brainwashed." And I was like, "Yeah, you're right, maybe so — I'm going to start learning." I told him I'm obviously not the right person for his questions, that I could connect him with the mosque imams or find some information so we could both read.

He would say things like, "We don't do that, you should never let the kids—" well, I guess I can share this too — we were telling the kids they should start praying, they should go to Sunday school, and my husband said, "You cannot force your religion on the kids."

And I said, "Yes, I can, because they were born Muslim — this is what we intended, this is who they are, and they're going to have to start practicing and learning, just like we saw our parents doing it and started doing it naturally." But with them, they know they have a choice — they know they can celebrate Christmas and at the same time they can celebrate Eid. When they were asking, I said, well, because those aren't our holidays, we don't celebrate them — so we're going to have to learn our religion, our beliefs, what we can celebrate and why, why we don't celebrate other things. Those are things I started taking them to Sunday school for, and my husband would say, "Why don't you guys celebrate this, what's the problem, why can't the kids do that?" He'd ask the questions and I'd say, "Well, that's a really great question, and I'm not the right person to answer it."

But I started seeking knowledge, because I could see he was right — I don't have the knowledge, I can't defend myself. When you have little to no knowledge about something, you're speechless. So I started educating myself online, learning, reading when I could. I started listening a lot to — I think he was really the one who saved me — Omar Suleiman; that's why I have such deep respect for that man. I started listening to him a lot, and to people good for beginners, like Mufti Menk — he's really easy to start with.

I started seeking knowledge, I guess, and learning — "Oh, that's why we don't do this, that's why—" Once I started learning it, I started teaching it to my husband, sharing the knowledge with him, so he could learn for himself and so he'd never again have to tell me my kids should just choose their own religion. It started making sense for both of us. Before, I knew I shouldn't be doing certain things, but I didn't really know why — I would just obey because I knew we weren't supposed to do it, not because I understood it deeply. That's how I started — just doing it all. I still listen a lot online; that's where my resources for knowledge come from, I would say.

Basically, I led my entire family toward becoming more intentional about why we do what we do. But honestly, I give all the credit to Allah, because he's the one who guided me — if he hadn't put that situation in front of my family, if my husband hadn't pushed me the way he did, I probably wouldn't have stayed firm on that point.

If I hadn't, I wouldn't be sitting here talking to you right now — I would have lost it right there. Something inside me told me, "No, you can't do that, you need to start educating yourself." It's the situations that guide you to the light. It wasn't a happy moment at the time, but now I look back and think, I'm glad that happened, because I wouldn't know a lot of things — my kids wouldn't know a lot of things, my kids wouldn't even be memorizing Quran. Small things led to bigger and bigger things.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

A lot of things changed after hijab. Before, like I said, for most of my life I was without the scarf, but I felt more, being in a Western country, like you feel connected, you feel like one of them — it's such an awkward feeling, but that's how you feel. Even though I didn't wear hijab, I never dressed too openly — I was still fairly modest in my clothing, wearing loose clothes, but I just wouldn't wear a scarf.

How did I come to the scarf? I always knew the time was going to come, because I started seeing a lot of Muslim women at the mosque and thought, maybe I should start putting that on. I honestly thought — and this shows how little knowledge I had — that the scarf was a cultural thing. I'd see it on women from Pakistan, women from Afghanistan, and what I saw in the media was that their husbands made them wear it, that the family, the brothers, forced them — that's how I thought, which is very strange coming from a Muslim woman, but that's how I saw it: that these women wore hijab because their husbands made them, maybe out of jealousy or control. That's what I thought, until I started learning and reading the Quran, and I started seeing the ayat — "Oh, so I'm supposed to be wearing it too." It's not something somebody is telling me to do, it's my Creator telling me to wear it. That's how I came to it.

It was really a big decision for me, because I knew a lot of things were going to change around me — people were going to change around me. I couldn't do the same things I was doing before — you basically have to get rid of most of your closet, renew everything, adapt to a new look and to the people around you. It took me some time to work up to it, but once I decided, it didn't take long to actually put the hijab on — it was just one morning I woke up and said, "That's it, I'm starting today," and I've been wearing it ever since. It was just the decision I made once I found out it wasn't a cultural thing.

Oh my gosh, tell me about it — I've had a lot of ups and downs in my life. I still think I'm discovering myself; I don't know about other women, but I still try to find myself, who I am and why I'm here. Those are the things I always try to discover — why things around me happen, what the reason is. I always have that in the back of my head.

I don't regret putting the scarf on — I actually believe I earned more respect. Even before, in the Western countries, when you'd see a man, you'd shake his hand or give him a hug — it was very normal for me because I didn't know I wasn't supposed to do that. I didn't know I shouldn't be hugging a man I wasn't related to — I thought that's just how you greet people, and it was very common where I come from too. Then, once I started learning, it was like, "Okay, this isn't allowed, and here's why." I noticed that men in Western countries actually knew more than I did — when they see a woman in a scarf, they don't reach their hand out or try to hug her, they distance themselves respectfully, which I think is a great thing. They were more educated on that than I was. I think it's a matter of respect, because people I used to know stopped hugging me once I started wearing hijab — even family friends, people I know, they stopped doing that kind of thing.

It was great — I don't regret putting the scarf on, and I've seen it have a huge influence on my daughter. She's very young and goes to public school, and sometimes she comes to me and says she wants to wear a scarf to school. I say, "Are you sure?" and she says yes, so I say, "Okay, go try it, experience it, why not." She comes back and says everybody told her it looked so cute. I don't want to force her, but I want her to do something out of her own will — I want my kids to have a better education about this than I had myself, I guess.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s05 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

I don't know — what do you mean by that, the portrayal of Muslim women?

The feeling is one thing, of course — I know it's wrong, what they're saying is wrong, at least I understand that now. But how would I explain it to them? I try to connect with them, relate it to their own religion or belief — like, why are Muslim women held to a much higher standard, much more piety, than they are — relating it back to their own religion. If you look at Maryam, the pictures and scriptures of her, or the pious women in the church, the nuns — just look at your own belief, your own religion, how you were commanded, before you even judge me. People have judged me a lot, and I say, "You go to church and put a scarf on too."

My own mother-in-law goes to church and has to have some kind of head covering on to pray, and I think, why is it okay for you to do that but not okay for me, a Muslim woman, to have my scarf on all the time? What's wrong with that? The Quran says that when you pray you should be covering — not just your head, but down to your arms, your form should be covered too. When you read that, and then — I don't want to sound like a hypocrite, because I was one of them, I wasn't wearing one either — I started relating it back to them, and then they lose the argument, because they know what they're saying is wrong.

Yes, everybody who goes to church wears something — it's not like us wearing a hijab, but they put a light veil on their head. It's a little tradition — I don't know if you've seen Jewish women, they just cover the back of the head. My mother-in-law wears a scarf like that, most of the time without it, but for church she wears it. So I say a more spiritual, more religious person would do it, because somebody who really knows their religion, who's well educated, would do it — you see that more in the older generation than the younger one. I don't know how the younger ones dress for church now, but they probably still have something like a uniform — they don't just go in whatever, they wear proper clothes because they know that's a place to pray, to glorify God, whatever they believe. They respect the prayer and the outfit, so — I respect my religion, I respect my faith, I respect my Creator, because he asked me to put it on. If that bothers you, that's your problem.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I don't think it's that they misunderstand — it's judging, like, "Oh, she wears a scarf." A lot of people don't know my background — when they say things like, "Why are you wearing a scarf," assuming it's my husband — my husband isn't even Muslim originally, he's a revert, and he would never in his life tell me to put a scarf on. He was actually the opposite — he believes women are beautiful and should show that beauty, that's how he grew up. So that whole idea — that it's your husband or a cultural thing — doesn't apply to me, because culturally I'm not from there, and I'm definitely not forced by anyone.

It's probably her own decision — if somebody judges another woman for wearing hijab, assuming her husband is behind it. If somebody has the nerve to say something to me about my scarf, I don't bother engaging with them much, but I make sure I defend women — not just myself. If I'm being judged, I know other women are getting judged too, so I don't just defend myself, I defend all women in hijab.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s10 | question_id: followup__how_did_your_family_respond_to_you_wearing -->

It was good — after I started wearing it, my mother started wearing it, and then my sister-in-law, my brother's wife, started wearing it too. She thought, if you live in America, you're educated, you have everything you want, nobody's forcing you, and you started wearing it, then I should too — since she lives among Muslim countries, she felt she could start too.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s13 | question_id: role_family_community -->

In my household I'm a mom, and I know I should be responsible — I know my actions speak louder to my kids than what I'm saying. What I do in front of them is much more effective, so I take that responsibility very seriously, being a mom, a role model, and a good Muslim — I'm their first example. They see that in me and try to imitate it — my daughter, my kids, they know I pray at certain times, and they say, "Okay, Mom, I'm going to go pray," without me even telling them. It might not sound like a big responsibility, but it is — that's how we pass our religion on to them, so they can continue the faith we believe in. I believe that's my responsibility. On the community level, unfortunately I'm not too involved in community events, just smaller-scale things — I encourage my neighbors. There's a lady across from me, from Pakistan, who was always very open about everything, and lately I've been encouraging her to start putting her scarf on, maybe twice a week to start, slowly, and I feel it's been working — though it's not because of me, exactly. Her mother recently passed away, and I sent her a video of a woman talking about why she started wearing hijab, after she saw, for the first time, a woman's body being washed and shrouded — she saw the woman's body covered in extra layers, her hair covered just like a hijab. Hearing that story, I sent it to my neighbor, and she came back to me and said, "Oh my gosh, I saw that with my mom too — when they were shrouding her, only her face was open, but her head was covered just like hijab."

I haven't seen how it's done for men, but the woman's experience was that after her body was gone, they still put a scarf on her. Sending that to her was a reminder for me too, and for her — she was actually more modest than me before, and it was the opposite by the time she moved here. I was the one wearing shorts and things I'm not supposed to be wearing, and then I turned around 180 degrees, and I started seeing her change too.

I've been there — I know. It's not something somebody told me to do, I chose it, because I was on that side and it wasn't good — even though I was wearing certain things, I wasn't feeling good about it. I tell her she should be more modest. I see that as community-level work — sharing whatever knowledge I have with the people around me, on a smaller scale, but I want to slowly make a bigger impact in the community, inshallah. May Allah guide me.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s17 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

If she wants to go on that path, of course, I wish she does, but I want her to have deep knowledge, so that when she faces any challenge in her life — just being a Muslim woman and standing up for her faith — she's ready. ## HOW DID YOU BECOME MORE INTENTIONAL PRACTICING ISLAM OUTSIDE A MUSLIM-MAJORITY COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-001_s21 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_become_more_intentional_practicing_islam -->

Right, yeah. You see, something has to come with the foundation — our foundations, at least the seeds, were planted, so we knew who we were, we knew our identity, we knew we should pray, we knew we were Muslim. Even though we weren't practicing everything fully, we were doing the things we knew — we knew we should fast in Ramadan, we knew we should pay our zakat, we knew we shouldn't be bad to people, we knew we should respect our mom and dad. We'd been introduced properly, and that's super important for kids from a young age, I believe, because if kids are introduced to their faith early — they're already born Muslim, they're already born modest, in a way.

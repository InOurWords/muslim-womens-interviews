# Interview MWI-002

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Well, I think how faith shows up every day would be through praying five times a day. Most importantly, starting my day with the Fajr prayer, before the sun rises — I get up, make an intention, do the wash that we do as Muslims, and pray to Allah, our Lord, our Creator. It's a time to talk to our Creator, to meditate, and it really starts the day in the Islamic faith. That's the start of the day, and then throughout the day, praying — I go to work and I pray at my job. There's a place where I can pray at my employment. At first I was doing it without anyone knowing, but then people would see me — I'm around people who aren't Muslim, so I had a little dialogue with everyone and explained how my day goes, that Muslims pray five times a day, and they would see me doing this. I got permission that it was okay for me to have a space to do so, and they were fine with it.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I'm sorry, repeat that again?

Okay, let's see — I'm thinking if I've had an incident with anyone who hasn't met a Muslim. Not to bombard a person, but if they ask me, or I'm just approaching them and letting them know — I think it's different if someone comes to you and asks; then it's more welcoming to freely talk in that type of situation.

Okay, yeah, so I've had questions — someone would come up to me and say, "Oh, I like your hijab," or maybe they don't know anything and say, "I like what you have on, I like your scarf," and I tell them, yes, it's part of my Muslim identity as a woman — we cover our hair, we dress very modestly, we cover parts of our body, so it's an everyday look. Then I've gotten the question, "So do you wear it at home too?" and I say no, at home I don't have to wear it — I'm dressed very comfortably in my house clothes, and I don't have to cover around my husband, my son, uncles. So I think it depends on the questions, and that's how I would explain it. And then, especially around the holidays, people will tell me "Merry Christmas," and I tell them I don't celebrate Christmas, and they say, "Oh, okay, so what do you celebrate?" So I can explain that we have two holidays throughout the year, and I give the details on the Eids we celebrate, and I tell people we don't celebrate Christmas because we don't believe Jesus is the son of God — we believe Jesus is a prophet. In Arabic his name is Isa. So it's a nice educational piece to share with people who aren't aware that some people don't celebrate Christmas.

They're very beneficial, because for people that don't know anything about Islam, or very little, it gives them some more in-depth insight that they wouldn't get otherwise. It breaks down assumptions and misconceptions when they stop and ask me instead of just seeing me and assuming. I really appreciate someone asking instead of assuming, so then I can clarify and break down whatever assumptions people may have, and then they can go into their community or their church and say, "I met a Muslim lady, this is what I asked her and what she explained to me." It spreads education about Muslims, because I feel like day to day we interact with a lot of non-Muslims just in passing, but we don't have much dialogue. There are a lot of Muslims here in [STATE 1], and from everyday interactions — passing in grocery stores and other common places we meet — it's important to have dialogue with people.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s02 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

I haven't personally gotten any negative feedback — I can't think of a specific time. My immediate family is Muslim, but that's it; my cousins, uncles, aunts are not Muslim. I have family members down south — when you go deeper down south, into [STATE 2], [STATE 3], I have family that practice Christianity, in church every day, having Bible study. Some of them said, years ago, "What is this you're wearing on your head?" I said, "We call it a hijab, it's a covering, a form of modesty." And they said, "Well, how are you going to get married if you're not showing your hair?" I said, "Well, we usually marry someone of our same faith." They understand that this is a passage of righteousness, to cover and to be modest — it's one of the marks of being Muslim. I told them, as a Christian, you also have a look of modesty that you follow — if you look back at the Old Testament, at old photos, Christian women used to cover, used to wear a type of hijab, long sleeves, a long gown. Over time it's gotten shorter and shorter, and now you don't even cover your hair at all, but at one time you also, as a Christian woman, covered.

Then they said, well, if you don't take Jesus as your Lord, you're going to hellfire. I said, we believe we pray straight to the Creator — why don't you pray straight to the Creator? Why do you feel the need to go through a man to get to the Creator? It was a very long, complicated conversation, confusing coming from a Muslim, someone who has always practiced — I was never Christian, my parents didn't raise us Christian, so we never really studied it, but just knowing about the Trinity, three different people in one, is confusing to me. I didn't really get a good explanation from my family about what it was — it sounded as if they just practice it because it's stated to them, and certain things they couldn't clarify, why they do it, just that they do it because it's written. I know from the Quran and Hadith there are reasons why, as Muslims, we do things — the Quran states what is done, and the Hadith explains why it's done. It's very clear, in black and white, so for something not to be explained or clarified is confusing.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s03 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

That's a good question, because I didn't always wear hijab growing up. I grew up in the Midwest — there weren't too many Muslims at my school. In high school, I was the only Muslim, I think. We did have a few people from Palestine, Pakistan, India, maybe a few West Africans, but not too many, not like here on the East Coast. My parents didn't encourage us to wear hijab — we couldn't wear shorts or short-sleeve shirts or tight pants, but we didn't have to cover. So I didn't really have a Muslim identity — you couldn't tell I was Muslim. I didn't get any questions in school about my faith unless I wanted to tell someone about it.

When I started wearing hijab as an adult, I noticed I got approached differently — men didn't approach me anymore. I feel like I get a level of respect I didn't get before. Especially in the African American community — if I go into [CITY 1], people who aren't Muslim will say "Salaam Alaikum" to me, and even Muslims will say it, because in urban cities there's a predominantly large amount of African American Muslims. A lot of African Americans become Muslim — they weren't raised Muslim, but they convert — and they're already integrated in the community, so they have easy dialogue with each other. I get a lot of salaam alaikum, and people don't look at me a certain way because I'm not wearing tight clothes, not revealing my body — I get a more respectful look. It's a protection for me, wearing my hijab, for that reason — I'm in a safe space, traveling on my journey outside my home in a safe space.

I actually started wearing hijab when I got married. My husband was an influence on me, a gentle one — he didn't tell me, "You have to do this, you have to do that," it was more, "You should try," a level of modesty that's part of our religion. When I started covering, I didn't cover my neck — I just took a scarf and tied it behind my head, and my neck was showing. Over time, as I studied more and learned more about the religion, it deepened my understanding of the importance of why Muslim women cover, so I felt the importance to cover more, and I started covering my neck and draping it down over my chest, as it's stated in our Holy Book.

How do we learn that? Through prayer — because through prayer you have to be patient, you have to focus, you don't rush. That teaches you a level of patience to deal with any type of situation. Alhamdulillah, I thank Allah for being Muslim when I say that.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s06 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

Who do I feel most responsible to? My kids, my husband. It was my mother, when she was here, but she's not here anymore. And I feel a responsibility to my siblings as well, because everyone is spread out, living in different places — my sister's in [STATE 4], I have a brother here, and another brother in [STATE 5]. As the oldest of my family, I feel responsible for keeping the connection going — calling them, checking on them, seeing how they're doing. This is important because they're the only family I have that's Muslim.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s07 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I understand it to be very important for well-being — it connects our spiritual, our mental, and our physical health, to stay connected. We have to keep the community, we have to keep connected to one another, encourage each other, check on each other, see how we're doing. It's very important — it's part of our deen, but it's also part of our mental health, our physical well-being. That's why, as a Muslim, if someone's sick, you go to visit them. It's important to care for one another — it's part of our religion.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s08 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

No, I don't feel my religion is a threat, to anybody or to my community. No.

It's hard to say, because I'm also African American. In a country that's so racist, and the world is so racist at times, depending where you go, sometimes I don't know if it's because of the color of my skin or because I'm wearing a hijab — some of the tension I'll feel, or the looks I'll get. But then, because I'm so optimistic, I try to give people — I think Prophet Muhammad (peace be upon him) said to give people fifty or seventy excuses — so it may not even be the color of my skin or my hijab; someone may just be having a bad day and take it out on me, not caring about my skin color or my hijab. Sometimes people have had a death in the family that morning, and they still have to go to work and function. Giving people those kinds of excuses, and not taking it personally, kind of breaks barriers between people, because then you're able to deal with someone who's maybe having a difficult day or acting in a difficult manner.

I never know what kind of response I'm going to get unless someone says something verbally abusive or aggressive — then I'd know how to deal with that. But I don't get backlash from people, alhamdulillah — I haven't had anyone come up to me and call me offensive names, I haven't had anyone pull on my hijab, like I've heard of certain incidents where some Muslim women are attacked or called certain names. I haven't personally had that, and I pray Allah continues to protect me in that way.

That hasn't been my experience, but I know of a situation with my sister — she used to wear niqab, and she lived in Canada for thirteen years, in [CITY 2]. She got a lot of harassment from white men because she was wearing the niqab, and she would go out with her kids without her husband, and men would approach her aggressively and say demeaning things to her — "take that off, go back to your country" — very offensive, negative words that they shouldn't be saying to a woman with children. So even though it's not my experience, I personally know my sister has experienced that.

Alhamdulillah for that, and may it be a ticket to Jannah for all the women who wear hijab, because you have women who cover, who wear hijab, but you can't tell if their husband is Muslim or not — so a lot falls on the Muslim woman, I feel like, but in a good way.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s13 | question_id: followup__where_does_your_approach_to_speaking_up_and -->

Yeah, because I teach — I'm an art educator at a farm, with the homeschooling community, a co-op that runs for homeschoolers. I taught the middle schoolers this year. During the middle school years is a time when children are trying to find themselves, making friends, learning about themselves, wanting to socialize, but some of them aren't doing it in a positive direction, or aren't sure how. I try to teach the children that.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-002_s14 | question_id: liberation -->

Where in my life do I feel most liberated — where, meaning a particular place?

These are good questions, I never thought about this one. I feel like when I'm — actually, the work that I do — I feel most liberated when I'm teaching, because...

I feel like I have a presence, as the only Muslim, and the way I teach, I can incorporate a little bit of Islam into it, which makes me feel liberated, because I feel like I'm giving a community something they wouldn't get anywhere else — the people I work with are all non-Muslim, and I don't think they'd have the opportunity to approach any other Muslim and ask the kinds of questions they ask me, since they know me on a personal level. I feel most liberated when I can teach, and teach the youth too — I've taught them about some Islamic values that I wouldn't otherwise have the opportunity to share. Every morning, during our morning circle, I have seventeen kids, and I do a lot of social-emotional teaching, because I feel there's a need there — if they're lacking things based on how they act and what they say. I give a general concept, and then I'll say, "As a Muslim, we do this." So I'm able to educate the young generation, and my hope is that they'll embrace Islam, or at least they can say, "I had a Muslim teacher, and she taught us about Islam" — that I didn't just come and teach academics or art, but gave them something of myself. I feel like that's important and needed with the young generation coming up — I see that they're very open, they're not stuck holding tightly to any particular values, their minds are very free. This is a good time to educate them about Islam.

It's a good place to be, to reflect and see how I can best serve the children as a Muslim woman and as an African American woman.

Yes, and I feel liberated doing that, because I'm giving them a piece of information and teaching them a little about myself and how I practice Islam. Otherwise they wouldn't know — like when we had the Eid celebration, I brought goodies for everybody and let them know we celebrate — we don't celebrate Christmas, but we have our own celebration, and I welcome and share it with everyone. I don't think they'd otherwise see that — even my colleagues didn't know what Eid was. I came in one day — it wasn't the actual day of Eid, because I never work on Eid day, but it was a few days after — and I brought some cookies, and they asked, "What are the cookies for?" I said, "We're celebrating Eid," and they said, "Oh, what is that?" — people just don't know.

I feel good to be able to teach people about Islam. That's my job.

# Interview MWI-003

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

I'm a practicing Muslim, as long as I can remember, so faith is a big part of my day. I'm a mom — my kids are older now, so I decided to go back to school to finish my graduate studies. My day normally starts with prayer in the morning, before Fajr — that's been my time since I can remember, because I always had little kids; I got married early, so that used to be my time, waking up early before Fajr and spending some time praying. I do my Fajr, get my coffee, and then get my only daughter, who's younger, ready for school. I send her to school and then head to my own school — I have classes, I work in the lab, and then come back, pick her up, go back home. During the day, as a Muslim, I have to pray five times a day, so I do my prayers at school. I come back home with my daughter, get dinner ready, sometimes go to the gym, come back. I'm normally out of energy by around seven p.m., so I just wait until I do my last prayer, and the day ends for me.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

Let's start with the self. Muslims in general, practicing Muslims, are very disciplined. First of all, knowing that God is overseeing everything gives a lot of discipline to your life — you know that not just every movement or action, but every thought, is monitored, so you work a lot on yourself to make sure you're doing everything pleasing to God, because we believe this life is temporary, that it's just a path to the next life, and it decides your life forever in the hereafter. So we're very disciplined people, in terms of our commitment to worship, to family, to community — we're responsible in every position we're in, not toward people but toward God, so you try your best to please God in every way.

In terms of community, this is something a lot of people don't know — since I've lived in the West for a long time, many people don't know that the Muslim community is very accepting, open-minded, and loving. Their discipline, their main goal, is to serve humanity and serve people locally. This is something I feel because of the media, because of the politics around us — a lot of people don't know that unless they actually engage with a Muslim or a Muslim community or community centers around them.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I came here in 2000, so I've been here for twenty-six years. When I first came, it was actually a hard time, because September 11th happened a year after I arrived, so there was a huge campaign against Muslims. I came wearing my hijab, my full covering — I came young, I was twenty when I came, so I wanted to represent my faith in a good way to people here. It wasn't a good time when we came, because there was a lot of hate toward Muslims, a lot of misconceptions in the media, so it was a challenge, but I was pretty open about my faith, about my values. I remember a lot of times people would stop their car when I was outside and yell, "Go back to your country, go back to the camels," and even in the mall people would stop me and yell things. I always took every opportunity to help people understand — I would explain to them what my faith is, how the media misrepresents Muslims. It was a hard time, but it was also a good time — a good opportunity for me personally to open dialogue with other people.

I think age has a role. I had my kids here, after I came. I don't know if it's age, but I'm almost in my forties now, and I feel I'm not as strong as I used to be — I think it's because of age. I'm still open to answering questions, but I'm not fighting as hard as I used to, showing others the way I used to. I'm still trying to do my best, but I think I'm leaving that to the new generation now.

Fighting means fighting the mainstream idea that Muslims are violent, that Muslims are bad. I'm fighting those ideas, trying to show people what we're actually about, what Muslims are about, and how Muslims are misrepresented in the media. That's the fight I meant.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yeah, this is a blessing I always had. Wherever I moved — I've moved to many states here — I always had a good Muslim community, and I tried to always connect to other knowledge-seeking, Islamic-knowledge-seeking people. Nowadays I use YouTube a lot, always listening to new videos about Muslims, about Islam, more details on faith, explanations of the Quran. We also have a sisters' halaqa to study Quran — we've been doing it almost weekly for a long time now, something me and my friends established and worked on together.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

Hijab, back in our culture, in my country, is of course an Islamic thing, but a lot of it is mostly culture — when you get to a specific age, you're kind of expected to wear it. I started my connection with my faith early, around sixth or seventh grade — we had a really good Islamic teacher, a woman who explained Islam in a way you could relate to, and even though I was younger than the age you'd typically wear hijab, I decided to fully wear it — I wore hijab and what we call a jilbab, a long dress. My parents at that time weren't very practicing — they were more into the culture than the religion — so they were actually against it, but I stood my ground and wore it, and I still do now. It was a challenge at that time, because back home there was also a lot of misconception against Islam and practicing Muslims, so it wasn't very acceptable in the family either. But I decided to wear it, me and my older sister, and I've always been seeking to learn more about my faith, because with Islam you always have to revisit certain concepts to reinforce them in your daily life, and I've been doing that for a long time — alhamdulillah, that helped me a lot with wearing the hijab and practicing everything.

I remember when I first came here, a lot of friends would tell me, "Oh, you're going to the US, that means you're going to take it off," and it was confusing — why would I take it off, if I wear it for God, when I come here? So I came here, and it was a big part of my identity — I always looked different from other people around me. I joined school a couple of years after I came here, and I was almost the only person wearing hijab in my classes. It was good, though, because I always achieved in my classes, so it was a good representation for Muslim women with hijab. Since then, it's been a big part of my life.

When I grew up back in Palestine, Islam wasn't something you just grew up following — unfortunately, even in Muslim countries, there were a lot of campaigns to move people away from practicing the right Islam. Islam basically encourages you to think for yourself, to ask questions, and because of the dictatorships in many of these countries, they don't want people to think, they just want them to follow what the government wants. If you're a Muslim, a practicing Muslim, a Muslim who seeks knowledge, that's something they don't want, because to follow Islam properly you actually have to think about whether something is right or wrong, whether you're following what God wants or what people want, and they don't want that — they want you following what the government or the rulers want, because that's not always what's best for the people. There's no free will, no democracy, and the easy way to control people is by making them submit — especially when what the rulers want isn't what's best for the people, but what's best for themselves.

We weren't allowed — mosques now are open to young people, they're encouraged to ask questions, encouraged to think for themselves; that wasn't the case back then. You'd wear hijab just because it was the culture — you'd grow up, start going to college, and that's when you'd wear it. If you actually wanted to wear it because Islam wants you to, that wasn't encouraged — a lot of people viewed girls who wore hijab early as girls who were forced into it, not doing it for themselves. In my case, it was the opposite — I actually wanted to do it, but my parents didn't want people to think they were forcing me.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s05 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Are you talking about here, or in the West, or back home?

I still don't understand — what kind of woman?

Yeah.

I actually love when I'm in a situation like this. I've been in many situations where people would tell me, "Oh, we feel bad for you, you're forced to wear hijab, you're forced to listen to your husband, you're forced to—" and I love these moments, because that's a good opportunity to explain, especially for people who know me or interact with me — I'm very kind and open-minded, easy to talk to. There's this idea that Muslim women always get married through arranged marriage, and I've been told many times, "I feel bad for you, being forced to marry someone you don't know," and that's not the case at all. I love these questions, because it's a good opportunity to explain, since people don't know otherwise.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Yeah, this is — even at this time, with social media, people still don't know. I have a neighbor who, until recently, thought I was wearing hijab because I'm forced to, because my husband or my son are around — there was a situation where she told me, "Oh, go inside, your husband, your son is coming," as if I'm only wearing it out of fear of my son or husband, which isn't the case. I love when people do that, because it's a good opportunity to explain — you don't explain randomly, you need people to ask you these questions or say these things to you. I remember another neighbor who asked, doesn't it feel bad that you're allowed to drive a car here in the West — which is funny, because we drive cars everywhere, I don't know why they think that's something we're not allowed to do as Muslims. Yeah, until now, unfortunately, there are misconceptions about Muslim women.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

You know, life is very hard — you go through things you don't imagine you can go through. I have a few things in mind... Maybe one of the hardest times we went through was when the war in Gaza was happening — this most recent one. My family is in the West Bank, so they were going through things too, but we felt shy even talking about any struggle my family was going through in the West Bank compared to Gaza. Just seeing the genocide happening in front of our eyes, and not being able to do anything to stop it, that was one of the hardest things.

What I've seen — it wasn't just me, I think it was a global feeling of helplessness, not being able to do anything. I always thought, how do people with no faith handle this? Because the only thing that made me, or anybody going through this, able to make sense of what was happening was faith — just knowing there is a judgment day, and everyone who committed any tyranny, any killing, any genocide, is going to stand in front of God and be asked. That gives a lot of peace of mind, knowing that God is just, that he's not going to let these people — the people who did the genocide, the people who supported it — go without being punished. Without faith, I don't know how people can handle these events.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

I feel like I have a responsibility — I always feel a responsibility to fix, or at least to play some small role in changing people's perspective, people's ideas. These things, when you have a culture, ways that have been there for a long time, it doesn't take one step to change, one action — it has to go through a period of time and a lot of change from other people. Just knowing that a word from you, standing for what you believe, can have a small change — I think that's always been my approach, to use these opportunities to make a change, to talk to people, to open dialogue, to help people understand why something is not right or needs to change.

As a young person, when you believe in something, you try to stand your ground. I remember hearing a lot of things from family members, like, "You're too young, why are you doing this to yourself, you're so beautiful, you shouldn't — you're eventually going to wear it anyway, why wear it this early?" But that didn't make sense to me. When I was that young, in seventh grade, I wasn't able to handle it the way I learned to later, especially with older people — you still have to have respect for older people, so sometimes negotiating or debating with them isn't very helpful. What I did was just stand my ground — I didn't listen, and I tried to surround myself with people who would support me, that I could go back to for support. When I got a little older, it became easier to actually discuss any opinion other people had and bring actual support for my position, from Islam, from religion, from history — the more you listen, the more you learn, the more knowledge you gain, and the better you become at defending what you believe in.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s09 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

Responsible for what?

I believe, for choices, the best bond is your bond with God, because you can rely on people, but with experience and a lot of disappointment, you learn not to rely on them too much, so you won't get disappointed. But I believe the most important bond, I would say, is the mother bond — I don't know if I'm answering the right question.

I believe this is the basic bond, that your bond with your mother shapes everything after that, and I was blessed to have one of the best moms. After that, also, as a woman, your relationship with your dad — as a woman, you need a male figure in your life, and if you don't have that with brothers, I had it with my dad. Just knowing there's a man who would always support you no matter what, love you no matter what, be proud of you — I think that mom and dad are the most important relationships, the ones that either fix or ruin everything after. Also, being a mother, your relationship with your kids — I think that's what makes life beautiful after that, gives you another perspective, another aim and goal, because once you become a mom, everything shifts, it becomes more about these lives, these souls, that you brought into the world.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s10 | question_id: role_family_community -->

There are many aspects — family is very important, I'm talking about extended family, parents, brothers and sisters. After God, they're your backbone, and if you have their love and support, you're good to go.

In my family, at this age — maybe when you're younger, they're your backbone, your support, but once your mom and dad get older, it becomes your responsibility to take care of them. That's a very important role, and no matter how busy you are, you always have to make time to serve them, because that's in our culture and our religion — this is what brings blessing to your life, taking care of the souls that took care of you when you were young. As a mom, in my own family, your role changes — when the kids are young, you're basically everything for them, the support, the source of love, security, advice, and that starts fading as they grow up, but the responsibility doesn't go away — you're always going to support them and love them and be there for them, no matter how far or how old they get.

I also believe I was more active in the community when I was younger than I am now — maybe I'm busier now. I use the community here to support myself, but maybe that's one of my shortcomings — I should have more of a role, support the community more. What I try to do is participate in many community events, but maybe I should do a little bit more.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s11 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

I think that happened after September 11th — a lot of people around us made us feel like we were a threat, that we didn't belong. It happened for a while. I lived at that time in an area where a lot of Muslims lived, and we had a lot of attacks on the masjids, on our apartment complex, on the street. We were told not to leave the house, because people tried, many times, to pull the hijab off some women's heads. It was a lot — that was one of the hardest times to be Muslim in the West.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

Can you repeat that question again?

I have many roles here — I'm a student, and I've been in the same school for a long time, so I feel like I belong there, I know a lot of professors and students, and having that support system makes you feel part of the belonging. A big part of feeling that I belong is the Muslim community here — we have a great Muslim community, and it's like home to me, especially because I don't have family around — they're my family, that's where I feel like I belong.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s13 | question_id: akhirah -->

It shapes everything — just knowing that this life doesn't end when you die, that it's just a gateway to the real, forever life. It shapes your perspective on everything. You know you're here as part of a test, here to be tested, to strengthen yourself, to get to know God, to build yourself in a way that helps you get to heaven, because we believe you have to work for it, you have to deserve it, and that takes a lot of energy, a lot of shaping in your life — every step you take, the way you think, interact with people, raise your kids, interact with your family, your community, people you don't know — everything is going to be judged, even your intentions, your thoughts. So it makes you work on every detail in your life — what you look at, what you listen to, how you talk to people, how you respond to others — everything is going to be judged, everything you're going to be asked about, so you have to try your best to deserve God's mercy.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s14 | question_id: qadr_control -->

In the last two years I've been going through something that flipped my life — everything happened, and it was out of my control. I worked so hard for years to build something, and in the last two years it was destroyed. You don't question it exactly, but after something like that happens, you know that in this life, everything is in God's hands — it's not about how smart you are, how much money you have, how strong you are. The only thing that happens is what Allah wants, not what you want.

We know that this life is a test — we're not here just to have fun, live, eat, and enjoy. God will put you through things that bring out either the best or the worst in you, and that's how you get tested. It's like when you're in a class and you study — the professor, at the end of the semester, is going to test you, to see how deeply you know the material. It's the same way — God will put you through things that bring out what's actually inside you, and if you've worked hard on yourself, you'll be able to withstand any hardship, knowing that every pleasure here is temporary. Your kids are temporary, your health is temporary, it's all going to go eventually — you're going to lose your health, your kids won't stay with you forever, your wealth might go any minute. Even the simple blessings — your eyesight, your five senses, your ability to walk — these are all temporary. So this life is temporary, it's a test, and that's how you're able to withstand any difficulty, knowing that everything happens by God's will and for a reason.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s15 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

What I know is, everything is connected — whatever you listen to affects your understanding of things, your feelings. If you're surrounded by people who are very negative, always listening to negative comments, you're going to be affected, you're going to be down, you might end up like them. If you're surrounded by hardworking people, or if you go to the gym, it's going to boost you, because physical action does have an effect on the heart and mind. We believe that when your actions — remembrance of God, saying God's name repeatedly, reading Quran, praying — this always has a strong effect on the heart, it softens the heart somehow.

The number one intention, when you do any act of worship, should always be to please your Creator — just knowing that even if it has no other effect, you're pleasing the one who created you, the one who deserves to be worshipped. I think that's enough, but it also helps to know the reason behind an action you do. For example, wearing hijab — we wear it because God told us to, but it also has an effect on communities. I remember, back when I was doing my bachelor's, people would ask me, "Why are you doing this to yourself?" — but when people interact with a covered woman, they actually talk to her as a human being, not as a body. We know how visual men are, how affected they are by what they see. I always used to give this example: if you want to hire a secretary, and a beautiful girl, uncovered, showing all of her hair and skin, comes in, there's a big chance you're going to hire her because of how she looks, or talk to her because of how she looks, rather than what's actually going on inside her mind, whether she deserves the position. But as a Muslim, wherever we go, I feel like people judge us based on who we are as a human being, not as a body.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s16 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah has many aspects. One is obeying his commands, following the religion he sent, that he accepted for us, that he wanted us to follow. Submission also means accepting his decree — and I don't want that to sound negative. You try your best, and in a difficult situation you try to change anything you're not comfortable with, but sometimes things happen that are out of your hands, so at some point you have to know that everything God writes for you is for your own good, and you just have to look for the reason and accept it. I think that's a big part of being submitted to God.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s17 | question_id: liberation -->

A big part of my liberation is being Muslim — that's actually the source of my liberation, because I know the only ultimate submission has to be to God. Even when you submit to rules here and there, you submit to them under God's submission too — for example, when you live in a country, you have to obey its rules, because in Islam we're taught that when you're in an area, you have to submit to the rulings of that area, as long as they don't interfere with God's rulings. A big part of liberation comes from the fact that the ultimate entity you're submitting to is God, so any injustice, anything else — because as a human, you're going to submit to something, even if it's not to God, you might be submitting to social pressure or something else — but your ultimate goal should be what pleases Allah, and everything else comes after. Liberation comes from knowing you don't have to follow what other people follow. I think that's a big part of my liberation, because at the end, most people follow the trend, follow what everybody else does, but as a Muslim you don't do that — you follow what your religion is telling you, what God is telling you, what makes sense to you, not blindly, because Islam tells you to always think, read, and increase your knowledge. You don't just follow because everybody follows. I think that's a big part of liberation, I guess.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s18 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

Number one, what matters most is doing it just for the sake of God — you're practicing not to show people or please people, your ultimate goal should always be to please God. But in addition, living as a Muslim in the West, you're not the majority, you're a minority, and unfortunately a minority that the media and a lot of people have misconceptions about, so you're a representation of the faith too — when you make a mistake, people won't say you did it as an individual, they'll blame the whole religion for it. So there's a lot of responsibility just living in the West, being on your best behavior, being a good practicing Muslim, showing what Islam is really about. I think this is a responsibility that people mostly in the West have as Muslims.

Representing Islam in the best way, in a way people actually understand — being a good Muslim tells a lot of people here what Islam is about, because you might be the only Muslim that person ever interacts with, so you can be a good representation or a bad one. That's a big responsibility.

There's a lot of misconception, I would say a war against Muslims here — Western media tries to stain us with all kinds of wrong ideas, that we're violent, terrorists, all about killing, all about forcing people to do things. And that's not true.

## HOW DO WE CREATE CHANGE OR BETTER COEXISTENCE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-003_s19 | question_id: followup__how_do_we_create_change_or_better_coexistence -->

First of all, be a good model to other people. Show the reality of Muslims, show how Muslims care about communities and other people and want the best for them. Talk to people, use any opportunity to open a dialogue and get your thoughts across to people who don't know, because that might be their only chance to learn about Islam from you.

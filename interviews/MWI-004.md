# Interview MWI-004

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-004_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

So I am a mother of three, married for close to nineteen years now, and my day is a typical housewife's day. It starts with Salat al-Fajr, then we move on to making breakfast for the family. I'm a homeschooling mom, so we have schoolwork, then comes our second prayer of the day, Salat al-Dhuhr. My day basically revolves around our salah times — anything we plan, anything we do, is planned around salah times. If we have to go shopping, my day is planned accordingly. Right now it's summer, so the days are longer, and the time between the salahs is longer, so that's how my day is planned — between Salat al-Dhuhr and Salat al-Asr is my shopping time, my going-out time, and we have to be back by Maghrib, because the time between Asr and Maghrib is shorter. Then the day ends after Salat al-Isha.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-004_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

As in this house, or in the U.S.?

I've been here, well, roughly sixteen years. Honestly, I believe my iman has gone up in the U.S. compared to how it was, because I was born and raised in Pakistan, in [CITY 1], and I feel like I've been able to learn more about myself and my religion, and appreciate the blessing of being Muslim, more in this country than I ever did growing up.

I've seen how — when I see these reverts, I've learned a lot from them, from their struggles, and how much love they give toward their religion, especially the struggle they go through with their families and communities when they come to Islam. I was so humbled by that, because I was born and raised in a Muslim family, and we do not appreciate our blessings enough as Muslims, because being born and raised Muslim, we weren't taught enough. I've learned more from these reverts, from people actually trying to understand our religion. In my sixteen years, I've experienced some of my husband's close acquaintances accept Islam, and I saw the love they came with toward the religion, and I was extremely humbled — that made me want to learn more, appreciate more.

I've also become more confident. Coming from Pakistan, I wasn't born and raised here — I felt more conscious of myself growing up in Pakistan, because I was a hijabi and niqabi, wearing an abaya, and I was more conscious of the people around me, because I came from a relatively middle-class family attending expensive universities — that's the reason I'm able to speak English, because I went to an expensive school, an expensive university, and when you have elite people around you, you get self-conscious, at least that's how it was for me. Coming to the U.S., I've become more confident in myself, and I truly believe it's a blessing from my Lord, because I was able to truly accept who I am and be comfortable in my own skin.

I'm into working with my hands — woodworking, gardening — and I'll go to a Home Depot or a Lowe's in my abaya, in my hijab, and buy tools, buy wood. There was one incident I'd like to share — I was in a black abaya and a black hijab; I usually wear colored clothing, but that day I was in a complete black outfit because that was what I could quickly grab. There was an African American son with his mom at the store — he was a middle-aged man, and his mom was older — and he pointed me out, "Look, Mom, look at that person," and he showed so much appreciation for how I was dressed, in an aisle where you'd usually find construction workers. It made me so happy — it doesn't matter who you are, it's how confident you are in yourself. I've been able to become more confident in my own identity as a Muslim woman who can do whatever she wants within the limits my Lord has described.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-004_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I grew up in Pakistan, so in the beginning it was mostly because it's part of the society you grew up in — a middle-class society in Pakistan is more conservative compared to some other communities. I don't want to sound offensive, but there's a gap — the more educated, the more westernized you become, the more respect you get, but in middle-class communities it's more conservative, so you kind of have to cover yourself when you go out. So it was part of our culture, I'll say it that way — my hijab was more cultural than anything else at first. If I attended weddings I wouldn't cover myself. But my story became more specific — I was honestly searching, around seventeen or eighteen years old. I came across the Barelvi sect in Pakistan, which is more open to welcoming women into their circles. As Maulana Rumi says — I don't know how to explain it in English — every being wants to go back to its origin, and our origin is going back toward our Lord, so it's like a spiral moving toward its center; every human being looks for that. The Barelvi sect in Pakistan is more welcoming toward women; the Deobandi sect is mostly for men — women are supposed to stay home. Pakistan is a patriarchal society. So when I was looking to find myself, I was introduced to this sect and started attending their programs, and that's how I became closer to deen. I started covering my face, started doing hijab and niqab, more for deen than culture. I did go to an extreme at some point — I started covering myself from all men, even my own cousins. I went very extreme, but then slowly calmed down, and I am where I am now. That's the short end of it.

## WHAT DIFFICULTIES HAVE YOU FACED WEARING HIJAB, AND HOW DID YOU RESOLVE THEM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-004_s07 | question_id: followup__what_difficulties_have_you_faced_wearing_hijab_and -->

Let me give a brief summary — it was very specific, to be honest. I was at a business school in Pakistan, one of the only three niqabis in the university. Looking back now, as an adult, I think I could have opted for being a hijabi instead of a niqabi — I was still a teenager, and I see it now as a teenage rebellious phase, but I was a niqabi. This was one of the top business schools in [CITY 1], with thousands of students, and I was one of only three niqabis in my year — that says something about how big the gap was. There were many hijabis, but even beyond that, most girls didn't wear hijab at all — just shirts and kurtas, no scarves. So that's how big the gap was, and I only had one friend who would stick by me. I was extremely conscious that most girls didn't want to hang out with me because I was a niqabi, because they felt I wasn't part of their circle. I came from a middle-class family, and a lot of these students were upper-middle-class, and in my heart I felt uncomfortable — it could have been my subconscious, I was biased against myself, I don't know the exact reasons, but I was kind of a pariah in my year. I didn't have many friends, just this one girl who was from Saudi Arabia, who'd moved to Pakistan for higher education — I guess she understood, maybe she'd had niqabi friends before. But within two semesters I got married, so I left, I didn't continue my higher education in Pakistan.

In my family, my mother's side has no segregation at all; my father's side has extreme segregation, so much that if the women are in one house, the men are in a different house altogether. My mother's side, everybody hangs out together. That was really confusing for me as a teenager, attending events in both families. That made me more conscious. Then I got married, and even my in-laws, though culturally religious, had these issues — they would cover their faces outside the house but not inside the house, from their own cousins and male family members. For them, covering was mostly about the people they didn't know, not the men in the family. So that was also confusing for me. Holding on to my hijab, my niqab, was hard, but all I did was keep moving on — that's all I could do. Eventually I stopped doing niqab and found a middle ground with just my hijab, but I didn't let go of my abaya, because that was something important to me. So even at family events, I'll have longer, looser clothing, and my hijab is just a part of me — like your arms and legs — so I just kept moving on despite all the confusion around me.

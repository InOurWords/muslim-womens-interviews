# Interview MWI-005

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

As-salamu alaykum. Faith shows up multiple times, because our faith has different aspects. In terms of spirituality, of course, the morning starts with Fajr prayer — waking up and remembering Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala first thing in the morning, regardless of how bad the night was due to a baby waking up or kids being sick. It's a requirement, and it also roots me to my faith. Throughout the day I find myself making silent du'as anytime something is disturbing me or difficult, just asking Allah for help, whether I'm stuck in traffic or in the pickup line at my kids' school. Faith shows up spiritually in my prayers throughout the day — Dhuhr, Asr, Maghrib, Isha — and it shows up anytime I'm struggling with something; I have this constant remembrance of Allah. It also shows up in supermarkets, sometimes at the mall. I won't say every time I go out I meet somebody, but at least once a week I'll have someone complimenting my hijab or asking why I wear it, whether I'm taking my kids to swim classes or at the mall. Something I find interesting is especially when I'm shopping for makeup — there are different concepts people have about Muslim women, like whether we're able to go shopping by ourselves or spend money on certain things. Sometimes people have a very different perception about our religion, and that would be the third form where faith shows up: when people ask questions related to my faith.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

We are very welcoming and hospitable. I want to be transparent: the majority of my community, where I live, are open-minded people who will be warm and welcoming, who will share about their faith and answer your questions. That's what I would say about my community. Of course, my community is also very diverse, with different backgrounds and ethnicities — even the Muslims in my community differ in their religious background. Some are very religious, some are just learning their religion, some are Muslims by name, and some are practicing. As an individual, I would describe myself as confident and vocal, someone who is proud of her Muslim faith and understands that her Muslim faith can help her fit in anywhere and succeed in her life.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I was born and raised in the United States. Over time, things definitely changed. In my elementary school years I never wore hijab, so people often thought I was Hispanic. Even then I was very vocal about my faith — when Ramadan came I would tell my classmates why I was taking off school for Eid, which wasn't very common back in the '90s. On Eid I would actually go to school and bring goodie bags and gifts for my classmates just to share my faith, and when they offered food to share for someone's birthday, I would openly say if it had gelatin in it I couldn't have it.

I also didn't go to a diverse elementary school — I was the only Muslim girl in my entire school. Then in middle school and high school, 9/11 happened, and everybody had a perception of Muslims depending on what they'd watched or which news channel they followed. That's when I started wearing hijab. I remember being in a [GROCERY CHAIN 1] with my father just buying vegetables, and a very upset woman came up and asked me if I was Bin Laden's niece — it was a very straight question, no beating around the bush. Instead of getting scared, I explained to her that no, I wasn't, and that not every Muslim is related to or (is) a certain person. During that time I didn't meet many accepting people, and I was living in an area that wasn't diverse at all — we were the only Muslim family in our entire neighborhood, and the only brown family too. That's the kind of community I grew up in. Later I went to an Islamic boarding school, so at that point I was mostly around other Muslims. But when we would go out, during middle school and high school I saw the way people reacted to how I was dressed, or the things they would say, happening more and more. Then I started driving, and there were quite a few times I was at a red light — I wore hijab from a pretty young age, and my father is your typical elderly Muslim man with a long beard — where the car next to us, someone would say something to us with their hands, with their fingers. That happened quite a few times.

Later, as I got older, I got married and had my children, and that's when it hit me that raising a child in the West who is proud of their Muslim identity is very difficult. You have to put in a lot of energy, you have to be strong, you have to have a lot of conversations with them — it's not just about giving them a Muslim name and expecting them to grow up (proud). That's when I realized how much effort our parents probably had to put in to make us proud of our religion and not fear what people would say to us. That gave me a completely different perspective. When I became a mother, it was more about how do I make my kids love my religion, love their faith, not just mine — especially in this world of social media, where people have more and more exposure and more and more perspectives depending on who they're following and what they're watching, and that's how people's mentality about Islam gets shaped. It's a really hard time to raise children. That's when I found myself doing a lot of research to make sure my kids love their faith and are proud to represent it, not scared of it — especially because that's also when Islamophobia started increasing, especially for women. A Muslim man can be walking on the street and you won't even know he's Muslim until you ask him and find out his name is Muhammad or something, but a practicing Muslim woman wearing hijab is automatically recognized as Muslim. So she's more prone to Islamophobic crimes than a Muslim man is. Raising girls in that environment made me look at my faith in a very different way and teach it in a very different way as well.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I'm from a cultural family, and nobody explained to me in the beginning why I should wear hijab — I was told I had to wear it. It was an honor thing for my mother, never really pushed by my father. My mother's family was very pro-hijab without the religious background behind it, so it was like, you wear hijab but only in front of servants, not in front of cousins — it was really confusing. I just knew you had to wear hijab when you left the house. I was the person who wore hijab and took it off at school, and who only wore hijab when my mom was around, because my father never pushed it — ironically he's more religious than my mother.

Then I ended up at the Islamic institution in [CITY 1], where the uniform was niqab, not just hijab — if you left the madrasa for a field trip, the mall, or the airport, you wore an abaya, a hijab, and a niqab. I started wearing niqab there because I had to. Over time I accepted it, and then, as we studied Qur'an tafsir, I understood the point of hijab — that it's in the Qur'an, that there's a purpose behind it. I was one of those girls, even in the 2000s, who thought, why do girls wear it and boys don't — I actually had that mindset. But slowly, alhamdulillah, I came to realize the wisdom, the hikmah, behind it, and that you get to a point where you love Allah and realize there are certain commands from Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala that you don't sugarcoat — you just wear it.

My niqab journey is a bit different. We also have to understand that many of these madaris are culturally influenced — in the Indo-Pak subcontinent, most madrasas that stem from there hold that niqab is mandatory, because according to Imam Abu Hanifa niqab is wajib, especially in times of fitna. When I was in madrasa I started wearing niqab too, and since I'm not the type of person embarrassed to do or say anything in public, it wasn't hard for me to wear it outside. Over the years I got used to it, and it became my second skin — I couldn't live without it, and I did everything with my niqab on. All the milestones people find difficult to achieve with niqab — I got my license, I finished college, I got married — all of that happened while I wore niqab.

Then, three years ago, I was teaching, and a student asked in my class, "Is niqab an obligation?" The room went silent, because that student and everybody in the class wore niqab because of how they'd been taught, and I wore niqab too. I don't know if she wanted my personal ruling or preference, but she asked, and I ended up saying no, niqab is not an obligation. When I said that, it became a big deal in my workplace and community — people started talking. That's when I started thinking: if this isn't fard, why am I wearing it myself? When you're twelve or thirteen and start at an Islamic school with a uniform, that makes sense, but over the years you get a mind of your own and need to do your own research and understand why you're wearing something. I have daughters, and I'm not the type of mother who forces her kids to wear niqab, so when I came home and shared that with my younger girls, they asked me why I wear it. That's when I went through this whole research process: why do we wear niqab, where does this ruling come from, why is it most prominent in certain schools of thought or cultures. Parenting really changed my perspective on how I was going to teach and learn my faith, and this was one of those moments — if I want my daughters to grow up knowing what is actually in their faith versus what is culturally influenced to the point where we call something mandatory in every situation and don't let girls ask questions, (that) was a real turning point in my journey.

So I actually removed my niqab. It was one of the toughest things I've done — I got bashed for it a lot. I removed it this year, though it took three years (after starting to question it) to actually take it off, and I did it cold turkey, just took it off one day. Honestly, after taking my niqab off, I've become closer to my deen. It sounds funny, but it's because at this point I feel like whatever I'm doing now is for Allah. When I was wearing niqab, I always felt like people assumed the stereotype of niqabis — that we're rigid and quiet — which is wrong; niqabis are some of the friendliest people in the world. But people would assume that because I wore niqab I must be so righteous and pious, and I'm not — it's just that with niqab, people assume it. So I felt like I was somebody I wasn't. Honestly, after taking it off, I really feel like someone who is actually following their deen.

For me, hijab makes me follow a command of Allah to get close to Him — that's the first thing. The second is that hijab, especially in the West, represents me as a Muslim woman, and maintaining my religious identity while living in a country that's a melting pot of religions is really important to me. The third is that it's a constant reminder, when I'm outside, that I'm doing everything for the sake of Allah, that I'm going to do everything He wants me to do. It just makes me get closer to Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala. The funny thing is I understood this only after all these years, as a mother — this isn't something I understood in my teenage years, how hijab makes you close to Allah. But now hijab is a constant reminder to me that Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala is with me.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s05 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Honestly, it doesn't even make me feel bad anymore, because the portrayal is already wrong within the Islamic community before it ever gets to non-Muslims. I come from a South Asian background, and I feel South Asians have stripped Muslim women of their rights more than anybody. I come from a culture where if a woman marries by her own choice, she's considered to have committed the worst sin in the world. I come from a culture where women who memorize the Quran aren't allowed to recite it in public, because we've taken the ruling that "a woman's voice is awrah" to a whole other level. If you're outspoken, in politics, or whatever, people think you're not even religious anymore, and if you are religious, you're expected to be confined to your home. As someone who graduated as an alimah, when I started teaching at the local masjid and speaking at events, my own classmates and family considered me to be doing something wrong — and it's funny, because Arab Muslims don't think it's wrong, Muslims from the African continent don't think it's wrong, but for some reason in my culture it is.

I don't even blame non-Muslims, because Muslims have ruined it for themselves. We've created a culture in our community where every single thing Muslim women were allowed to do in Islam, we twisted. For example, in the cultural Muslim community, the story of Aisha is taught to encourage girls to get married young — they'll mention that she got married young, but not how she never thanked the Prophet ﷺ after her father told her to, when the verse was revealed clearing her name of slander. That's so empowering — when her father told her to thank the Prophet ﷺ because the verse had been revealed and her name was cleared, she said no, I'm going to thank the One who sent the verse. In South Asian culture — and I'm sorry if I keep mentioning South Asian culture, but it's the culture I'm speaking from — we tell women that even if her husband is extremely abusive, well, he provides for you, so thank him. Even the way we teach Islam to our girls in South Asian culture, we make them start hating Islam most of the time, because we use Islam to tell them "you can't do this, you can't do this, you have to be like this." We use sabr as a kind of trophy — if you're living in an abusive relationship, well, Islam says to have sabr.

So, like I said, when a non-Muslim says something bad, some perspective about a Muslim woman, it doesn't upset me because I already know that person is ignorant. What upsets me more is when Muslims say things about Muslim women that are completely out of line — someone born and raised Muslim, not even a convert, from an "Islamic Republic" of this or that, and they don't even know what their own religion says about women. So yes, it upsets me, but not as much, because I already expect that from a non-Muslim, not from a Muslim. Muslim men and women have set the bar so low for Islam and for women that I don't even get surprised anymore.

Honestly, I think it's all about how someone was taught Islam. I know a few converts who became Muslim in a community that was prominently South Asian, and they actually think certain things are part of Islam that aren't, because culture and religion have mixed up over the years. South Asia, for example, was colonized for many years, and after that Hindus and Muslims lived together, and Muslims there ended up adopting a lot of Hindu customs without realizing none of it has a base in our religion. So people from those areas actually think they're doing something Islamic when they're not. It does depend on where somebody is from, and on who taught them and how they were taught.

To give the [ISLAMIC BOARDING SCHOOL 1] example — I remember being told that going to college is haram for girls. Why did that person say that? Because that's what they were told, and what they actually believed. We still have people in our community who say girls going to college is haram because of fitna, there being boys and men around. I'll say, well, isn't it haram for men too, then, since there are women there? And I'm told, no, men need an education to be providers. I say, well, they could run a burger stand — they don't need an education for that. So when I say "who," I mean: were you taught by somebody who was brainwashed into thinking culture is their religion, who was never taught the difference and will teach you the exact same thing — and in certain Islamic schools you can't even ask why, because that's treated like the end of the world? Or did you learn from somebody who actually did their own research and was taught by somebody who wasn't culturally influenced, versus somebody who is culturally influenced and needs to make things a certain way because it fits their culture and appeases the men in their community? That's the "who."

The "how" is also important, because of the way Islam is taught. Take the story of Aisha again — one person can teach it focusing only on the fact that she got married young, and another person can teach the same story and talk about how the Prophet ﷺ treated her, how she never lost herself, how she actually established an even stronger identity because she remained who she was — the fact that she'd knead dough and a goat would come and take it, because that's how relaxed she was; she wasn't walking on eggshells worrying about what she'd cooked before her husband got home. Now you have women in their fifties who panic because their husband is home and the food isn't cooked and he's going to yell at them. But that same story of Aisha, when I hear the elderly women in my family use it, is used to say things like, if a girl is twenty-two and says she's not getting married yet, "oh my God, she should already be married at this age" — that's the only lesson they take from it. So the "how" is: how do you teach Islam to your students so they understand that, as Muslim women, they have an identity.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Within my culture, definitely, people don't expect me to speak up. It's funny — when I used to wear niqab and started speaking about women's rights, somebody said to me that the way I dress and the way I talk are very different. In my culture, I'm expected — and this happened a year ago, I was doing a Hadith intensive and speaking about how the joint family system has no basis in Islam — an elderly, very educated lady came up to me afterward and said, "stop saying stuff like this, you're going to ruin the lives of a lot of girls, they're going to demand separate houses." I looked at her and said, well, that's the hope. My culture expects that if I have Islamic knowledge and wear hijab, I'm probably going to be saying something like "cook for your husband, that's going to get you to Jannah" or "don't go to college because it's haram." That's the perception within my culture.

Outside my community, it's sometimes a shocker when I start speaking English without an accent — it's funny that even now, people's faces change when they hear me speaking, whether at a store, hospital, or bank; you can tell, "oh, she's actually speaking English." A lot of people who aren't well versed about Muslim women just assume she's going to need an interpreter or won't be able to explain herself.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I've had six miscarriages, and I think really understanding what Qadr was at that time really helped, especially with the first three, which happened after my first child. I didn't want to only have one child, for personal reasons, and when I went to the doctor they said I had unexplained infertility — they did everything and couldn't find anything, which is even worse, because there's no medicine, nothing they can do; everything is fine, so why is this happening? As a human being you just want a solution for everything. Alhamdulillah, Allah gave me three healthy children after that, but during those times it was about telling myself that Allah does whatever is best for you.

I should throw this in too — I'm from South Asian culture, so I had three girls, and I loved them to pieces; my husband and I never thought our family wasn't complete, and we were done having children. But it was funny, because everywhere I went, if I seemed really happy or at an event or a da'wat, some auntie had to say, "everything about your life is good, but they can't give you a boy" — and people don't understand that that actually hurts. You've gone through an infertility journey, you have three beautiful, healthy daughters, you're so happy with your life because you thought you'd only have one child and then got more, and then suddenly you're made to feel incomplete.

Then it was from Allah — I never in a million years thought I would have more children, and then I was expecting, and I didn't even care about the gender. I made du'a to Allah that He only give me a boy if I could raise him to be a good man — that was my only du'a; if I was going to raise a terrible man, give me a girl instead, just give me a healthy child, but a boy only if I could raise him to be a good man. During that pregnancy, things were showing up on the ultrasound — that he had fluid in his head and other issues — so it was like I was getting what I prayed for, because maybe Allah knew I could raise a good man, but now I was going through all these medical issues. Faith came in again: Allah does whatever He does for a reason, Allah will do whatever is good for you, Allah will give you whatever is in your Qadar.

From my miscarriages, I think it all has to do with infertility — those were some of my darkest times, especially the consecutive miscarriages, and then the journey to having all my healthy children. For people who've had a miscarriage and then get pregnant again, that pregnancy is the longest year, the longest time of your life. I think that's when faith really helps: Allah will do whatever is good for me.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s10 | question_id: role_family_community -->

For my community — this is going to sound terrible, but I've reached a point where I've realized certain people in my community are a lost cause. There's no point in me sitting down with someone much older than me and telling them they can't expect their daughter-in-law to cook for all their sons every single day; there's no point having that conversation, because they're well-rooted in their culture and to them I'm just being disrespectful. I did that with my own family for a while, and then I realized I was wasting my energy when I could use it to change the upcoming generation instead. So I stopped trying to fix what was already damaged and tried to salvage what was left — the upcoming generation, who might otherwise assume whatever our elders are doing is right. I put my focus on women my age, women younger than me, women getting into adulthood right now — for a while I worked with teenagers. That generation is something I can fix, or at least attempt to, so the upcoming generation has a good version of what deen, what Islam, really looks like. That's where I stand in my community. I respect the elders. I'll share a funny story — I was leading a halaqah with people of all ages, and someone asked about tarawih, saying she'd had a long day. I said, look, it's Ramadan, if you can't pray tarawih at the end of a long day, that's fine. Afterward, a very elderly, respected community member came up and told me I was the first person she'd heard say inside the masjid that you don't have to pray tarawih. I said it because the woman who asked has five children, all under six, and is living a tough life — what am I supposed to tell her, that she'll get sin for not praying tarawih at the end of a long day when her husband is at work all day and she's with the kids and fasting? I don't want to disrespect the elders, I don't want to change their ways, I can't change their ways, so I respect them, I always ask for their du'as, I respect their decisions because that's what they know and what they thought was best. But my focus in my community is on my age and younger, the youth.

As for my family — as a South Asian (woman), I don't really have a role. I'm a wife, a mother, a daughter, a daughter-in-law, a niece. That's it. The one thing I have control over is my kids, and, surprisingly, whoever reads this one day will be shocked at how hard it is for a South Asian woman to actually have control over her own kids — even getting to the point of making decisions different from what my parents made for me is a battle. My mother thinks my kids should go to (the school in) [CITY 1]. According to my mother, I'm raising my kids all wrong — why does my daughter go to public school, why doesn't the younger one wear hijab yet like my oldest does. For a while I thought I was failing at parenting because of everything being said in my family, and then I reached a point where I didn't want my kids growing up thinking that being (a woman) means complete control over you, especially as a girl. So my role in my family is that I'm a mother — that's where my influence comes in, whatever religious changes I can make. Besides that, no.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s11 | question_id: belonging -->

I was born and raised here — I belong here. This is home; you're born here, raised here. When somebody asks me where I'm from and I say I'm from America, and they push back with "no, where are you really from," I ask them where they're from, because unless they're Native American, why are they asking me that? I'm born here, I'm raised here, I'm American — I'm a [STATE 2] native, born and raised. I never even left my state. Belonging means I should have the safety of practicing my religious identity, because that's what my country represents. As long as I can wear hijab here, which I should always be able to, and live my life according to my religion, I belong here, and I don't think anyone has the right to take that away from me.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s15 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

That my kids stay Muslim — I know that's controversial, because people will say, don't you care who they marry, how they dress? I care about all of that, but I've sat face to face with students who wear hijab and never take it off, yet say they're not Muslim. My most important thing is that my kids, my grandkids, my great-grandkids, are rooted in Islam: Allah is One, Muhammad is the last Prophet, we don't commit zina, we don't drink alcohol — all the major things that come with Islam. That they just stay Muslim — I make du'a for that. Of course I want them to be practicing too, but people don't realize that practicing doesn't equal belief, and belief comes first. That's so important — for them to stay Muslim, to love Islam.

I make sure. Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala has always put me in situations where I learned — I was a middle school and high school Islamic studies teacher for eleven years, and it taught me so much. I always remember one student who came to my class and wouldn't talk to me for three days; she hated my class. One day I had a one-on-one with her and said, every other teacher says you're really good with them, why don't you like me? She said, I don't like Islam, and your class is Islamic studies. I asked why she didn't like Islam, and she said Islam doesn't let us do anything — her brother has a girlfriend, she can't have a boyfriend; her brother goes to prom, she wanted to go last year and couldn't, and now she's stuck in this Islamic school. I told her, look, your parents aren't raising your brother right — they can't control your brother, and that has nothing to do with Islam. They're typical Pakistani parents who believe boys are boys and can do whatever they want. She didn't believe me at first, but three weeks later her mom called, came in, and said I'd turned her daughter against her. I said I hadn't — if that's how you're raising your son, you can't tell your daughter that Islam doesn't allow her something while it allows him. That girl still reaches out to me — she's a nurse now, and she still thanks me. She told me that if I hadn't (talked to her), her plan was to leave that Islamic school, go to college, and leave Islam — she had no intention of ever staying Muslim once she left her parents' house. That's just one story, and I hear that kind of thing every single day.

So my role has been to let my daughters ask questions, to let them know how much Allah loves them, to let them know the "why" behind why they can't have boyfriends, why we don't do drugs, the wisdom behind all of it, why we have to cover. Alhamdulillah, I thank Allah every day that I was able to get to the point of telling them that Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala put things in the Quran for their own benefit, and I'm blessed that my kids have actually come to understand that — but it took a lot of work. A lot of Muslim parents use Islam for parenting — "my kids can't have sleepovers" — not because Islam says so, but because I tell them, your dad and I don't allow sleepovers. We don't use Islam as a tool to stop our kids from doing something, because I've heard the whole "you can't sleep at so-and-so's house, it's haram" — no, it's not haram, be a proper parent when you have to be a parent, don't use Islam to control your children when it's not necessary. That's the role I've played, and it's been a struggle, and it's still a struggle with my younger kids — talking to them every single day, helping them understand what is actually haram and what is just something I don't want them to do, so they don't think everything is haram.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s16 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

Nothing is really going on consciously, but I can never leave the house without hijab — it's like a part of me. It's that Allah told me to do it, so I'm doing it, and I love Allah and Allah loves me.

I come from a culture — again, am I the only one who talks about culture so much? — where log kya kahenge, "what will people say," is a very big deal; you can ruin your entire life over it. Alhamdulillah, I actually thank my culture for being that intense about it, because I don't even care what people say at this point. I think it was pushed so much, for all the wrong reasons, that for me it ended up going into my heart instead. Taking the niqab off is one of the biggest examples — people said so much, they're still saying so much; somebody literally took a picture of me and sent it in a WhatsApp chat to ask if it was really me and why I'd taken it off. You get to a point where you think, I'm doing it for Allah, I don't care what people think.

Allah is my friend, and Allah is always there. He's closer to me than anything in the world, and He will resolve everything that I need.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-005_s18 | question_id: liberation -->

It's in my personality and my daily life. I wish people could actually peek into my heart and realize I really don't care, except for Allah. I'm at a point where — and this is going to sound disrespectful — I don't care what my parents say, and that's one of the most taboo things a South Asian woman can say. For me, if Allah is happy with me, that's what matters; it's in my daily life, my personality, the way I hold myself, the way I take care of all my daily affairs.

My mom is almost eighty, and she believes that if she takes her niqab off, she's going to Jahannam. She doesn't really know that I've taken mine off — when I visit her my niqab is off because I'm coming from the car, and outside she's not on social media so she doesn't see any flyers or posts. I've told her two or three times that I've taken it off; I don't think she realizes the depth of it, though one time she said, "do whatever." My dad knows — I told him — and he said he'd always wondered why I wasn't (taking it off already).

It's not just about my parents, though, it's about anything. I feel liberated when I'm able to follow my deen without (worrying), knowing that, alhamdulillah, I'm a good Muslim — maybe I don't know how my salat or my fasting is being measured by Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala, but I'm liberated because I don't need the definition of Islam from anybody — not my aunts, uncles, in-laws, spouse, children, parents, or the Muslim community. It's all because my connection with Allah is so strong that, alhamdulillah, I feel like I'm at a point in my journey with deen where I know that Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala — I don't want to say He's happy with me, because I don't know if He's happy with me or not.

# Interview MWI-006

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

As a student, an ordinary day consists of waking up, studying, praying, studying, then getting ready for school or going to my next set of classes and completing assignments. Coming home, I either do more studying for school or occasionally attend a halaqa if my local university has one going on before I head home. Then I come home, eat dinner, and sleep.

Faith shows up during the five daily prayers — whenever Dhuhr or Asr comes, I have to go pray; that's one place it comes into play. It also comes up generally when I'm interacting with non-Muslim colleagues — for example, if a guy extends his hand to shake mine, I have to say, actually, I don't do that, and if they're curious I explain why. It also shows up through the Muslim Student Association, or generally when I'm interacting with other Muslims. I also do some Islamic studies on the side — sometimes I go to my local masjid to study Quranic Arabic, and sometimes I go for hadith — so that's where it shows up as well.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would say the Muslim community is very open and very welcoming. At least from what I've seen, nobody has ever shunned anyone based on their faith, their identity, how they practice Islam, or how they don't practice it. Everybody has been pretty nice to everyone, generally. It's very open, especially on my campus — we've had so many converts, subhanAllah, that it's a very unique place to be, I think, compared to other universities. Generally it's a nice space to be, and I feel like when you're in a community where people share the same moral values and faith as you, it's easy to get along with them, communicate with them, and be a part of (it).

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've lived in the area I live in for the last, I want to say, fifteen years, and I definitely think it's shaped who I am as a Muslim. I was born in an area where there were a lot of Muslims, but I was raised here from the age of five, so I grew up around a lot of Muslims, whether Indian, Pakistani, Arab, or African — a bunch of different kinds of Muslims. I got to experience that, and I think it gave me a generalized sense of multiple perspectives and made me open to dialogue and conversation. For example, some people follow a certain madhhab and others follow different ones, and having open conversations about that without anyone being offended — I think being in a very diverse Muslim community has made me very open about these topics and very accepting of many different views.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes. I attend some classes — I've done hifz school, so I still do Qur'anic classes and review. I occasionally listen to lectures online; I think that's the main thing I do now that I'm in undergrad, because I have less time for a structured course. I sometimes try free online courses where you sit and listen to lecturers and take notes. Mostly I'll attend halaqas, or at my local masjid we sometimes have lectures or a scholar's seminar that comes through, so I'll go and attend those. That's generally how I seek my knowledge.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

That's a good question. My hijab story is pretty straightforward — I think it's the story that happens to many youth who have parents who wear hijab. When I was younger I saw my mom wearing hijab, so I decided I would do it too because I thought it was cool, and I started wearing it when I went places. I also wore it because I went to Islamic school when I was young, so it was part of our uniform anyway, but then there was a point where I wanted to wear it all the time, so I just ended up wearing it. So when it came time for it to become mandatory for me, it wasn't a huge shift — I was already doing it because I liked to, I thought it looked cool, so I ended up keeping it on. Of course I wasn't doing it for the right reasons at that time, but it kind of just became a natural thing for me.

I mean, I think originally, when I was a kid, I just did it because I saw my mom do it — you're influenced by the people around you and you kind of just follow what you see. But as you grow older you get a better understanding — this is an actual commandment in the Quran, women are supposed to wear hijab, they're supposed to be modest in a certain way. Of course there were struggles with it — hijab isn't just the headscarf, it's also the way you carry yourself and the kinds of clothes you wear generally, so that was a struggle growing up, especially when I transitioned to college. I was a homeschooled kid, so my community at that time was just the Muslim people I saw at my masjid, so wearing an abaya and hijab was easy then. But when I went to college, everyone was wearing shirts and pants and those kinds of things, and I kind of wanted to mimic that — but then you have to realize you're breaking the codes of hijab if you wear tight pants or a tight shirt. So that was a struggle, but alhamdulillah I was able to maintain it — my mom used to dress-code me quite a bit, so it helped.

It definitely singles me out. In a lot of spaces I've been in, at least growing up — in high school and college — I was typically the only hijabi. In university I studied engineering, so a lot of times it was either a male-dominated room or a room full of people who weren't necessarily Muslim; in my graduating class in college, I was the only practicing Muslim. It's interesting when people come up and start asking, "I've never seen you do this, why is that the case," or "why aren't you coming to the bar with us." They also look at you kind of strangely — I think a lot of hijabis have experienced this, where people see a hijabi walking into a room and think, "oh, that's a little different."

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I don't think they misunderstand anything per se — it's more their ignorance about it. I haven't had anyone make assumptions when they've walked up to me; it's more curiosity. A lot of them are just confused — I've been asked things like, "are you Hindu or something, is that why you wear that?" They just don't know what I'm wearing or what it means, so I have to explain it to them. They'll ask, "do you ever take that off," and I'll say, yeah, I take it off at home — explaining these simple things to them, because they generally just don't know. I don't think they have any bad assumptions about it, at least from what I've seen.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Honestly, I think it's a mix — first it's like, oh, I didn't realize people don't know this, because growing up in a very heavily Muslim-dominated community, especially as a homeschooler, I kind of forget sometimes that people don't know what Muslims are. So first it's surprise that this is even a question people would have. But then it's also kind of nice, because I'm glad they're asking me instead of just going to Google and wondering, "what's that thing she's wearing on her head?" I find it kind of endearing, honestly — like, you're nice enough to be curious and take the step to ask me these questions. It makes me feel nice, I guess.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I guess I can talk about difficulties with regard to academics. A lot of times you can put in as much effort as you want into your studies, or taking an exam, or applying to a certain program, but things don't necessarily end up the way you expect relative to the effort you put in — you can write the best essays and that won't necessarily translate into the outcome you're expecting. In those moments, the biggest thing I've embodied through undergrad is Qadara Allahu ma sha'a fa'al — whatever is best is going to occur, and Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala knows best; I've tied my camel and left it up to Him. That's how I manage difficulty generally — realizing I'm not in control. I have to try my best in everything, that's something we have to do, but if things don't turn out the way I want, that's okay — Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala sees my efforts and knows what's best for me. You don't know if that job was the best for you or not.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

Definitely. I think everyone has a different way of practicing Islam, and when somebody sees someone wearing hijab and an abaya instead of loose pants and a t-shirt, they automatically assume, "oh, this girl is hyper-religious, she might be a hafidha, she might be like this or that" — so they have these expectations of you when they interact with you. Even elders will have these expectations, but then when they actually talk to you for the first time they might be like, "oh, she's just a normal person, just like us, another undergrad." Sometimes these expectations make me a little uncomfortable and make me feel like I don't belong in certain communities. I used to go to a school called [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1] in the [METRO REGION 1] area, and at one point I left that school. Whenever I go back to that community — I still keep in contact with my teachers and have a lot of friends there — sometimes I'll go pray salah there on my way home, and when I see certain teachers or students, I feel like they look at me like I no longer belong, just because they're wearing their full black abayas and hijabs and I'm not necessarily wearing a black abaya, I'm wearing a different long dress or something, and that might single me out to some degree. Or the way I carry myself nowadays — I've changed since I was young, I behave a little differently, I talk a little differently, I'm more outspoken about my opinions, and I've noticed that changes how people treat me. My friends and teachers who know me treat me the same way, but other people in the community treat me slightly differently, and that makes me a little uncomfortable sometimes. So I do think expectations are placed on people based on their perceptions of you, though that's not the case everywhere — I think it's a matter of exposure. You might have a perception of the Muslim ummah based on one community, but if you've been exposed to many different communities, you're more open-minded and don't have these preconceived notions about people.

Yes, I'd say I've had a lot of difficulties, internally and externally, with the way I dress. Externally, as I grew up through my four years of undergrad, my mom especially would talk me through it — I might come out of my bedroom about to leave the house wearing something I thought was modest, and she'd say, "[INTERVIEWEE], I think this isn't a good idea, this part is see-through, I don't think that's good," and I'd be like, okay, but I really liked this outfit, why do I have to change right now — but then I'd go change. That was a little tough, but eventually I realized my mom was saying what's best for me, and it's better to be corrected by someone close to me than by some random auntie telling me, "beta, that's not the right thing to do," so I appreciate that.

Internally, I think the struggle — this might sound shallow — is that growing up, you're surrounded by so many pretty girls wearing really nice outfits, and if they believe that's the correct thing for them, they should do what they want. But if I believe that's not the correct way for me to dress, I should stay with my moral compass, stay with what I think is right — though it's tough, because sometimes you think, I also want to dress like that, but I can't. Eventually I got through it by shifting my style — I'm not necessarily wearing a black abaya, but it's still modest; I like wearing colors, so I worked out what colors to wear, going shopping with my mom and asking, "do you think this is okay to wear to school?" That's definitely helped.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s09 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

I think — not a person, but Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala. As a hafidha, I have this thing lingering over my head all the time, that I'm a walking Quran, so I should be behaving and carrying myself in a way that's conducive to that. Whenever I read Quran translations, or go to halaqas, it's like it's speaking to me directly and correcting me on certain things, so I definitely feel responsible, as a person who has accumulated at least this amount of knowledge, to act upon it. On top of that, I feel responsible to my family — these are the people who raised me, I'm the youngest in my family, they shaped my moral standards, and if I can't even follow the basic things they've taught me, that's not good. I agree with everything my parents tell me — they're very learned people and know what they're saying, so I should follow them. The last group I feel responsible to is the next generation — I'm pretty active in my local masjid and in the MSA community, and a lot of times you'll meet freshmen, sophomores, or high schoolers and middle schoolers at the masjid who look up to you because you're a bit further along in life; they ask you for advice. Making sure the way I behave, talk, and give advice is aligned with the Quran and Sunnah, something I'd want to be told as a youth, not misguiding them in any way, and being a good role model for them — those are the three groups.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s10 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I think, family-wise, it's just a natural thing — I'm the youngest, I'm also the only daughter, and I live in a Desi household, so the role I play in my family is kind of natural. Growing up, my mom would show me, "if this happens, make sure you do this, your job is to do this," those kinds of things, and also just watching my mom generally, I think I followed her footsteps — if I see something that needs to get done, I'll do it. I don't know too much about my role within my household beyond that; it's kind of just a natural thing I learned by watching. In terms of my community, my role is mostly as a member — I try not to take leadership roles because I don't like being the face of things, but if people need help with something, I'm always in the group chats, whether it's volunteer planning or anything else — "we need an idea for this," "could you help me out with this," "we need someone to sub for this class because the teacher suddenly needs it" — and I'll say, yeah, I can definitely help out if I'm free. So I kind of fill in the gaps where I can, and I'm also just a general student in my community — my local masjid holds a lot of Arabic classes and tafsir classes, so I go and attend, because it's my weekly slice of Islamic knowledge that I try to incorporate into my life.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s11 | question_id: belonging -->

To me, real belonging is when you enter a space and you don't feel on edge — you know that when you get there, people won't treat you as different, as someone they don't know. When you walk into the room you can easily greet people, and even if they don't know you super well, they'll treat you like a friend. That's belonging to me — you're not shunned, you're immediately accepted, even if you don't hold the same values or you differ on a certain topic; you're all respectful of one another and treat each other well.

I think so, yes, and in many of the spaces I'm a part of, I definitely feel that way.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s12 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Honestly, I think I'm very lucky. The non-Muslim spaces I've been in — whether people are Black American, Hispanic, these kinds of things — they've all been very respectful of my differences in faith, and they've also taught me about their faiths. At my university, people who are Jewish would tell me they fast on certain days too, and I'd be like, wow, I didn't know that, tell me more, and they'd tell me everything about it. At least in my experience living in this country, I haven't faced discrimination from the communities I've been part of, whether undergrad or the workforce — people have been generally very accepting, very curious. I genuinely feel confident as a Muslim living here in the US. I know there are other countries where it's much harder, like India or Uzbekistan, but at least here, in the area I live in, everyone's been pretty open, and I feel confident being a Muslim. If I have to pray and I'm at, I don't know, a gym or something, I'm okay with just taking out my prayer mat in the parking lot and praying there — I don't expect anyone to jump me or anything, I'm confident enough to do that.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s13 | question_id: akhirah -->

I think that comes into play whenever I'm making decisions I'm a little wary of. When I think of the Akhirah, I think of judgment — how my actions are going to be weighed on the Day of Judgment. For example, a guy acquaintance at school might ask if I want to grab coffee, and even if it's platonic because he has a girlfriend and other joint acquaintances are coming, I personally try to avoid going out with a group if there isn't a higher girl-to-guy ratio — I just try to avoid it. Things like that I keep in mind, because you don't want to get close to even doing anything that might be sinful. Or it might be salah time and the window is closing while I'm in the middle of class, and I have to debate whether to leave the classroom — will the professor treat me differently if I leave? Technically I could just leave and he won't say anything, but he might have a skewed perception of me — but do I care about his perception of me, or the Lord's perception? So in those moments I have to think about whether to do it or not. Or saying no to certain invites — during graduation season, a lot of my acquaintances are guys, so they'll text me invites like, "you should come to my grad party," and I have to say, sorry, I can't come, and make up an excuse, because I don't feel comfortable going to a guy's grad party, especially because he's a guy. So, things like that.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s14 | question_id: qadr_control -->

When something's beyond my control, it's the Qadr of Allah — everything happens for a reason, everything has been pre-written, and there are certain things you genuinely can't control. So you just have to acknowledge that and keep it in the back of your head: I do what I can, and if things happen a certain way, then that's okay. That's the main thing I hold onto.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s15 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

I think making sure you still get down the basic things of being a Muslim without compromising, even if you live in the West — salah is a big thing, making sure you don't compromise on your salah, or fasting, even when, for instance, there's a lunch meeting and everybody's ordering food and you just have to sit in the back. Making sure you don't compromise on the fundamentals, and also remembering that being a Muslim isn't just praying, fasting, or reading Quran — it's also being a good person. I feel like a lot of people forget that Islam is peace; you should treat everyone well despite their moral standards, their perceptions of things, their religion. Being a Muslim in the West is a combination of keeping down your basics and also being a good person, following the Sunnah of the Prophet, sallallahu alayhi wa sallam, treating everyone well — Islam is a way of life, and living that out as a Muslim the best that you can.

My friends say I'm a very level-headed person — I don't get upset, I don't show very strong emotions, and if I show any strong emotion it's mostly joy. They think I'm very calm and understanding, though I don't necessarily see that in myself, maybe because I have a different perception of myself at home. But usually when I'm outside, I'm calm, nice, and people think I'm extroverted even though I don't think I am. I do try, if I see someone who might not be included in a group, sitting on the side, to go introduce myself and try to make them feel more welcome, whether it's a Muslim or non-Muslim space. I think that's generally how I try to embody being a Muslim in outside spaces — and also not compromising. In undergrad it's come up a lot of times where I have to tell my teammates, in an in-person meeting or working on a prototype, "hey, it's my prayer time, do you mind if I step out for five minutes," and I'll go to a corner of the building, pray, and come back — everybody's been super nice about it, nobody's ever said anything against it. So it's about being confident in your values, explaining it to people if they're curious, and just being a nice person.

Before, if you'd talked to me in my freshman year of undergrad, I might have been very hesitant to say these kinds of things and explain them to people. Maghrib time, for example, is a very small window — you have to pray within thirty to forty-five minutes — and sometimes I wouldn't even say anything to my friends, I'd just leave and come back and say, "that was a long bathroom break." At that time I was a little shy about it, but over time I became more confident. The biggest thing that shifted my mindset was realizing — I'd been seeing this growing up but never actually acknowledged it — why are Christians so confident saying "we love Jesus," coming to your doorstep, these missionaries, and why are Jewish people so confident about their holidays, throwing a party, being proud and confident about their religion — and why weren't we as proud and confident about ours? People understand the different holidays and practices these other groups have more than they understand Muslims, even though, at least at my university, we're almost the same size, a very large Muslim community. Realizing that made me ask, why am I not confident in my own religion, why am I not educating people? Nobody treats conservative Christians or practicing Jewish people badly for it, so they shouldn't treat a Muslim person badly either. So I started doing that — of course it was a little hesitant at first. My friends actually think I'm insane — in the engineering department we have these giant workspaces, basically a bunch of lab benches, and if Maghrib is coming in while I'm working on something I can't walk away from, I'll just pray right there, and my Muslim friends look at me like, "[INTERVIEWEE], why do you do this?" And I'm like, nobody's going to do anything, why should I be afraid? So I think in the beginning it was hard, but over time, acknowledging that people just generally don't care — they really don't care, and if they do care, it's mostly out of curiosity. I haven't met a "Karen," so to speak, who has called me out for my Muslim practices, alhamdulillah.

Not really — if anything has happened to me, it was more of a passing situation. I did an internship in a certain area of the US that was known for its drug and poverty issues, and sometimes walking from the train station to my workplace and back, there'd be people who'd say strange racial slurs and Islamophobic slurs, but it was just a thing of passing — I'd walk past them and not give them too much mind. In professional spaces, academic spaces, or fun spaces like the gym, nobody's ever said anything.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s16 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

Before I went to undergrad, I was always surrounded by Muslims, always surrounded by girls — I wasn't really doing anything besides being at home, going to a masjid, going to Islamic school or hifz school, so my community was very — I'd say sheltered, if that's the right word — everyone was the same, nobody was challenging any of my moral standards, nobody was thinking anything differently from what I already knew. Coming into undergrad was the first time I was in a space where — this might sound strange, but it was my first time being in a class where everybody was my age, because I attended community college and did homeschooling, so I either didn't have classmates or the people were much older and didn't really talk to me. So coming into undergrad was the first time I had people my age, the first time seeing Muslims who practiced Islam differently from me, the first time meeting people of different religions. I learned a lot about different people's ideas of religion, identity, and moral standards, but I also learned what I value, because growing up I had this perception of what my moral standards were, but after experiencing more you get a more open mind, you learn to shift your ideas on certain topics based on what you've learned. My ideas on certain topics have definitely shifted quite a bit — religion is a big one, and also how I treat people; my perception of the LGBTQ community has shifted quite a bit, my perception of people generally, growing up with "stranger danger" (thinking).

My biggest growth was going from an introvert to more of an ambivert or extrovert — being nice to people, being proactive in spaces, being open-minded. I grew a lot; I just don't know how to explain it fully.

I think it's a mix of both. Growing up, faith was always described to me as Islam, Quran, salah, tafsir, studying Arabic — these kinds of things — and not necessarily as, how do you embody Islam in your character, how do you embody Islam through your reactions in certain situations. That's something I learned in undergrad. So I think faith has driven a bit of that, but also just my personal curiosity — I'm a generally curious person, I like getting to know people and learning about different topics — but I do think faith has definitely shaped my attitudes toward things as well.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s17 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah, to me, is remembering that nothing is in my control — everything is a ni'mah, even taking this breath, speaking this word, nothing comes without His permission. Just understanding that — I don't want to say it like this, but — you're kind of just a pawn in this larger game of life, and your movements are partially determined by your decisions, but the end result of the whole game is already predetermined, and you're just living through it without much control over it. Your goal as a pawn in this game is for everything you do to be to please Him, because before we came onto this earth, Allah said, you're going to be human, you're going to have your own decisions to make, your own society, your own nation, you're going to be judged on the Day of Judgment — are you ready to do this? And we said yes, back then. So you have to be the best person you can be and hope that whatever you do, as long as you do it with the best intention and believe it's the correct thing to do, then inshallah you'll do your best to enter Jannah — but in the end it's only up to His mercy that that happens. So submission, for me, is realizing that I'm a pawn in this larger game of life, and my job is to do my best, and whatever the result is going to be is up to Him.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s18 | question_id: akhirah -->

I think I mentioned this before, but awareness of the Akhirah, knowing I'm going to be judged at the end of the day — even Islamically, learning through the stories and hadiths we know, like what the Prophet sallallahu alayhi wa sallam saw happen to people who lied, or the punishment tied to committing zina — knowing these things guides you toward what you should and shouldn't do, and that's awareness of the Akhirah. Knowing these hints, these really scary things that could happen to you for doing things that our modern society might think is normal but that are Islamically forbidden, major sins — it definitely shapes the way you act in certain situations, at least for me.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-006_s19 | question_id: liberation -->

For me, I feel most liberated when I'm with my closest friends doing something we enjoy — but also something my parents approve of, and that we know Islamically is something that benefits us. One of my favorite things to do, and the thing I feel most free about, because my parents are also very approving of it, is going with my friend to a weekly halaqa we really enjoy. My mom is kind of strict and doesn't let me go hang out with friends very often, so when I ask, "Mom, can I go with so-and-so to this halaqa," and she says yes, that's fine — I'm like, wow, you really don't let me go do anything else, but you let me go drive solo with her to this one thing. That's a moment when I feel liberated, because I have my parents' trust, and I'm able to hang out with someone I genuinely feel I can be myself with, have real conversations with, and we're doing something we both enjoy, whether it's the halaqa, or going to a cafe, or going for a hike. I feel most liberated when I'm doing things I enjoy, that my parents approve of, and that I hope Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala doesn't think is incorrect, and when I'm spending time with people who genuinely get me, who are open-minded, people I grew up with and am comfortable around.

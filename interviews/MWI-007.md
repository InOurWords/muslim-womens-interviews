# Interview MWI-007

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

An ordinary day — I am a teacher, a middle school teacher. Right now I'm on summer break, but typically during the school year I would wake up around 4, 4:30, get ready as far as praying Fajr and all of that, prep my work for the upcoming day and go over everything, then leave the house around 5:30, six o'clock. I work in [STATE 2], so I have a bit of a drive. I start my job around 7:10, usually come home around 4, 4:30, spend time with family, then prepare work for the next day, and start the cycle all over again.

I'm really lucky because, obviously, I'm Muslim, and one of the benefits of salah is that we pray five times a day, which gives us the opportunity to constantly recenter ourselves — I'm so grateful for that. Salah is an important time in the day because it helps you recenter what's important and remember that, honestly, all the stresses you feel day to day are not important. I think that influences how I view my job and my work, especially working at the school I do — it's not a very diverse school, and for that reason I think I have a different outlook than a lot of my coworkers, who are very stressed on a day-to-day basis. One of the things I really like about myself — and I do attribute a lot of this to salah, to constantly recentering myself — is not getting too stressed out during the workday, because as a teacher you're kind of working throughout the day. I also mentioned I work in [STATE 2], so I have a bit of a drive — I really force myself to use that time to listen to Quran, make dhikr, or listen to a YouTube lecture. I've come to enjoy my drive; it's actually my favorite time of the day. I think because I'm so busy during the day I can find myself not fitting in dhikr or Quran, so I use the time on my commute — an hour there, an hour back. On the hour there, I don't allow myself to listen to any news or a podcast; I always put on an Islamic-related video. On the way back, I allow myself to listen to a podcast or the news.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

To be honest, I don't really think of myself as different, so I think I would struggle to answer that question directly. If I had to describe myself, or my community, in a couple of words, I would say that I live in a very close-knit community. I have a very small circle, and I like it that way — I feel like my circle and my community are very supportive and very inspiring. I feel like we're all striving to be better, to better ourselves.

If they expressed interest in coming to my community, I would be a hundred percent for that. I think I would describe it as a place where they may encounter things that are different from their normal life, but I think there's still beauty in that. I also think my community is unique in that there are so many different cultures you can notice when you're visiting the masjid — it's a melting pot, everyone's from different places, but everyone sees past that because the number one unifier is that we're all Muslim, which I think is very similar to other places of worship. So I think they would see things they resonate with, and I think they would also see things that shock them, that are different to them.

In terms of rituals — and in terms of places of worship. I've visited churches and synagogues before, and I'd say mosques are kind of the closest in terms of... I think it comes down to cultural practices, but there's a lot of hugs, a lot of kissing on the cheeks, that kind of thing. I think that would be one small thing that would shock them.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s02 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I think there's definitely a range. With social media, I feel like people are able to see — and it's tough, because with social media there are accounts that reaffirm what you already think a Muslim woman is like, and then there are accounts that may surprise you. I would say that in the area I live, [CITY 1, STATE 1], it's so diverse that I don't tend to think much about how other people view me — I feel like people have an accurate view of what a Muslim woman is because they interact with Muslims so much day to day. But in the area where I work — I work in [CITY 2, STATE 2], which is a very rural area, and I am one hundred percent the only Muslim on the staff. They have a diversity issue across the board — basically the entire staff is Caucasian. I know for a fact that a lot of the kids I teach, I am the first Muslim woman they've met. This is my sixth year teaching here, and I feel like it's gotten better, but my first year was a very interesting experience, because when the kids would walk in on the first day, a lot of them would do double takes. A lot of the kids have told me, you're the first woman in a scarf I've seen in person. I think with that comes a lot of responsibility.

During, I'd say, around October, whenever they're doing their unit on Islam in social studies, they ask things like, have you ever been to Mecca, why do you wear your scarf, why do you wear long sleeves and long pants. To be honest, I'm glad they're asking, because sometimes I can tell some of the kids have a question but don't ask it. The kids who do ask will ask things like, what does your hair look like, how long is your hair. Whenever someone does ask, it gets really quiet around me because everyone's waiting to hear what I'll say.

Not as much as you would think, with the adults. I don't know if it's because nowadays you can Google any question you have, or if it's culturally, in American culture, considered rude to ask certain questions. I've gotten a couple of questions, but I could count on one hand the number my colleagues have asked me. I think it's honestly just because you can Google whatever you're curious about.

To be honest, I don't know how they see me. They were a very welcoming group. I think at points I view myself as different, but at points I don't — my mom is American, I grew up here, so there are points where I feel different, and points where I feel like I'm American — I don't know, my mom's from [STATE 3]. I feel like I can fit in, but then there are points where I stand out. When I was younger I did mind that I stood out, that I was different — I don't know if I've gaslit myself into thinking it's okay that I'm different, but now, at twenty-seven, I almost like the fact that there's something special about me, that I am different. I kind of like the different parts about myself, as cheesy as that sounds.

The fact that I wear a scarf, for example — it's such an evident thing, it's almost the first thing you see about me. And there's a little bit of pressure I've always felt with that — I always feel like I have to, and this could go back to what others think of me; maybe I'm cognizant of the fact that people may have perceptions about what a Muslim woman is, so I'm always smiling, always upbeat, positive — I constantly feel like I have to manage people's impression of me. But, in a strange way, I do like the bar I feel like I have to set for myself. I like that I feel like I'm representing something bigger — I like the fact that when my students leave me, even if they're not going to stay in touch, they had the opportunity to interact with me and see what a Muslim person is, if that makes sense at all.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s03 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I think it definitely has changed. I'm very lucky that I went to an Islamic school until eighth grade — I always talk about the small circle I mentioned earlier; all of my closest friends, we all went through nursery, elementary, and middle school together, and some of us went to different high schools, but we're still very close. We always talk about how grateful we are for the foundation we got at an Islamic middle school and how we have a strong understanding of our faith — not all of us are hijabis, or some of us started out as hijabis and now are not, so even within my close group of friends, due to our experiences, we're all at different places. I think my childhood, growing up, I was very much surrounded by other Muslims, and for that reason I had a very strong sense of self, just because it was very affirmed when I was younger. I don't think I lost that going through high school and college, but there was a time when I got almost insecure about my sense of self — you go through that time of questioning what you were taught, which I think is very healthy, like, do I really believe this. Nothing to the extreme, but then in my young adulthood that strong sense of self returned, and now, at twenty-seven, I have a very unshakable sense of self — I truly feel like I know who I am, and I'm okay with that.

I would say yes, I do, and I'm really grateful because I have a lot of sources for that. I'm really into podcasts. Even when I'm not able to make Jummah, if I remember to on a Friday, they livestream their khutbahs, so I'll listen to those. Other than that, I think you just stumble upon videos with short snippets of Islamic knowledge on a daily basis. So I get knowledge from a variety of places.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

My hijab story — there wasn't really a point in time I remember saying, I'm going to wear the hijab today. By the end of middle school I still wasn't wearing my hijab a hundred percent — I would in public, but if I got in the car after school I would immediately take it off, then put it back on before going into Walmart or somewhere like that. Going into high school, my mentality was that it would be so much harder to put it on and go into school one day wearing it, then start wearing it, and if I ever wanted to take it off, take it off (later) — so I decided to start off wearing it (from the beginning).

I think, like I mentioned earlier, I'm very grateful for it because it's a constant reminder that I am Muslim, and with that comes a certain standard for myself. I do appreciate how people treat me with it on — this is something I've come to realize the older I've gotten — I think there's a certain level of respect that people give you automatically if you wear a scarf, and I've noticed that very frequently in daily interactions. But yeah, I'm very grateful for it.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I do know that people have different views of what Islam is and what Muslim women are like. When I was younger, in my teens and as a young adult, I did harp on that and constantly think about what other people thought of me, and I think that was due to comments people would make. My mom is a convert, and her family isn't Muslim — recently my little sister got married, and she was telling me a funny story about something our grandmother asked her.

Basically, to cut the story short, our grandmother was asking her a bunch of questions about her husband, who, due to various circumstances, she hadn't met yet — she wasn't able to come down for the wedding. Our grandmother said, don't tell your mom I asked you this, but does your husband treat you right? My little sister started laughing and said, yeah, he treats me right. Then our grandmother said, you know, he has that stereotypical Arab look, doesn't smile in pictures — he's very sweet, but he doesn't put his hands on you, right? And my sister said, no! That's my own family holding those views. Even when my mom married my dad, my grandmother had a lot of strong opinions about it, because that was a long time ago. I think there are things people think about Muslims, and things people think about Arabs, so that's like a double whammy. I know people hold those views, and to be completely honest, there are probably people in the world those stereotypes apply to, but the problem is that those are overgeneralizations — people take one cultural practice and apply it to a whole group of people. But honestly, I can see that changing in the next couple of years — there are so many Muslim voices on social media, and as the world, especially America, gets more diverse, I can see people interacting more with Muslim people and Muslim women on a day-to-day basis. I think that's what truly helps change those mentalities and preconceptions, when people actually ask those questions and hear from Muslim women straight from their own mouths. I feel like that will start to change, and I can see it changing.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

What I was mentioning at the beginning of the interview was just my mentality in life — I'm not sure if it's a defense mechanism or a healthy practice, but truly I don't allow myself to harp on small things. If it's something that won't matter in a year or in five years, I'm not going to get angry about it — I'd rather just let things go, let it roll off my back. It's the same with difficult times in life — I know they're going to get easier. I'm not sure where I get that from, or if it's naturally my personality; it's not how people around me, my family and my circle of friends, deal with problems, so I don't think it was modeled for me. I don't know why I'm like that, but I don't harp on things too much. For example, my mom recently went through a health scare that involved getting sent to the emergency room — yes, I was worried about her, but I had a very positive outlook, telling myself we're going to get through this. She's doing great now, alhamdulillah. I try not to say to myself, "you're dealing with this right now," because, to be honest, I don't feel like my problems in life are that significant compared to other people's, so I don't like to complain or say "I'm so stressed" or "I have so many problems." I don't really operate like that. Again, I'm not sure if that's a defense mechanism or a healthy or unhealthy thing, but it would probably take a lot for me to shut down.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

I do. I think the expectations for Muslim women are a little higher than they are for men. Even growing up, I have three sisters and three brothers, and there's just... To be honest, I think that religiously the pressures and expectations are pretty even, but culturally, I do think that, the way things have progressed, there's more pressure on Muslim women in how we conduct ourselves and carry ourselves than there is on Muslim men.

Both — within the community and outside of it. Within the community, I just feel like your image is very important. The way I've always viewed it — and again, no one has ever explicitly said this to me — is that you're always a representation of your family, and I think that's a cultural thing. I was always a bit of a goody-two-shoes in school, and I think the reason was that I always thought whatever I do is going to get back to my parents, is going to be a representation of them. My parents raised my siblings and me in a way where they would trust us until we did something to lose that trust, and I think how they raised us made me never want to lose that trust and always want to present myself in a positive way, because I always felt that pressure — whatever I do is a representation of them.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s09 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

So, when I was younger — and again, I said this a little bit earlier — I did struggle a lot with being different, with getting stared at. Oh my gosh, I am very sensitive to being in an area and getting a lot of stares. My mom is from [TOWN 1, STATE 3], a very small town, and whenever we go there... I have a vivid memory of being in high school, going to a buffet with her family, who are not Muslim, and just being in such a crowded restaurant with everyone constantly staring at our table, it felt like. I don't know what they were thinking, but I think I did used to make a lot of assumptions about why they were staring at me. When I was younger I really, really struggled with stares, and even until now, I don't really like going to the beach in a bikini — I guess some of that is still there, I don't like standing out. There are certain times I wish I wasn't different, so I'd say that's the biggest difficulty. I've never found the concept of hijab itself to be difficult — I've always seen the women around me wearing hijab, and actually the hijab itself I really love. I have a great respect for the concept of modesty. I'm not opposed to the hijab — I think I'm just opposed to what other people think of me, or just the staring, and knowing that they may be thinking this or that.

No, and I think a lot of people would find that surprising, considering my dad is an imam, but I can't remember a time when my parents said, okay, you have to wear hijab, or why aren't you wearing hijab — I really can't remember a time when they said that. All of my sisters and I started wearing hijab full-time at different times — my older sister loved the hijab, she's in baby pictures wearing it at five or six years old, she's just always worn it. My younger sister was wearing it by second grade. Me, like I said, I wasn't fully wearing it until high school. But I don't remember a time when anyone in my family, or my parents, said take it off, or don't wear it, or you have to wear it.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s10 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

Probably my family. I think it has a very strong impact on my choices — what's coming to mind is kind of what I just talked about, how I act in public; I think a lot of that I get from my family. But at the end of the day, I don't feel like I have to do things because of my family... what I'm thinking of is like me deciding to become a teacher. It's not that my parents were opposed to that, but I did feel like I had to go a certain route career-wise that I didn't end up going on. Was that from my parents? No, that was more so culturally — my parents have never explicitly said, we want you to become this or that.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s11 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I'm number five (in my family). Not really — I don't feel like I have to lead by example in that particular sense.

I think I stand by what I said earlier, that I do feel like I have certain expectations — not that anyone has ever explicitly said that to me, but I feel the need to overcompensate in that respect. Where I'm struggling with the question is the word "role," because if anything, I feel like I truly have almost a background role — I'm not really someone who has a leadership role in my community, and I don't really feel the need to get involved in my community to that extent. Even when I go to Jama'ah, for example, I'm in and I'm out — I don't really stay and converse too much with people, not because I don't like the people there, but I just like having a very background role; I kind of just go and do my thing and leave. In my family, I guess I have different roles at different times. I do have the role of being an older sister — I have a younger brother and younger sister, so with that comes checking in on them from time to time, being there whenever they need me. As far as my parents, as they're getting older, all of my older siblings have either moved out or are no longer in the state, so I've kind of taken on that caring role for my parents — I'm the oldest child they have here in [STATE 1], and I've started to feel that in the last couple of years.

I think I would run away from anything that requires me to be too out there, if I think of "role" that way — I would really rather be in the background.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

I think belonging here in the West means getting to a point where you're comfortable with the culture, to the point where you're familiar with the area around you, you can speak the language, you understand the nuances of how that society functions. Once you've attained that, I'd say you have a certain sense of belonging — on a day-to-day basis, when you can maneuver through life without much difficulty, I'd say you've attained a certain sense of belonging.

Absolutely, I think I've attained that. Like I said, I grew up here, and my mom is American, so I feel like that gives me a sense of — not ownership, but like, no matter what anyone says, I always know that I am American. If someone were to say something like "go back to your country," I do feel like this is the country I belong in. Conversely, my dad is Syrian, and I wouldn't say that if I went to Syria right now and lived there for three months, I think it would take a while for me to feel like I belong — which is interesting, because I do covet the Arab and Syrian sides of me a lot. But I think if I went to Syria right now, I wouldn't feel like I didn't belong exactly, but I would feel like I had to get to that point of being able to maneuver in that society, because I think I belong here more than I do there — which is kind of sad to say, because I do covet those Syrian parts of me and hold them very near and dear to my heart.

I think it's a combination of all of it — me knowing that ethnically I am American, that my mom is from [STATE 3], that her dad was Native American — I feel like, to an extra degree, I do belong here, and I feel like I check all the boxes of being American, as far as my comfortability being here. I feel like I don't have that with my Syrian side, and I think I covet it so much because I'd love to be equally as comfortable with both languages and really understand the culture. But to be honest, I think this is because I grew up here — some aspects of American culture I prefer over the toxic side of Arab culture.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s13 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

I think a lot of times I don't feel like there is a conflict, because of the idea of America as a place where people of different religions and cultures should come — this country was built on immigrants, that kind of thing. Conceptually, my story and my family's story is what America is all about. But if I'm not thinking conceptually, if I'm thinking about what America actually does, about its actions — those are very distasteful, and that's where the conflict comes in. There are certain things America stands for, not what America says it stands for, but what it does, that conflicts with things I hold dear. So those two things are kind of on opposing sides.

Take Syria, for example — I really don't appreciate how America conducts itself on the foreign stage. Regardless of the president, it's not really about the president, it's about America's actions — how involved we are in stirring up things overseas, or with Gaza. Am I proud of my home? Yes. But sometimes I'm not proud of the fact that I live in a country that supports certain things and does certain things overseas. It's not a singular event — if you really look into certain things, it's not pretty.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-007_s14 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

Do I think there's a conflict between my faith and the country I live in — again, conceptually, no. Even though America is kind of considered a Christian country, I don't really view it like that. I think that on a day-to-day basis, if you take the news out of it, people, from what I've observed, enjoy the fact that America is that diverse. I was at the airport the other day and there was a guy praying in the parking lot, and no one was disturbing him, and he felt comfortable enough to do that — that's the really beautiful part of living in a country this diverse. So I don't really feel like the country, or my faith, conflicts with the country, because I do feel like we still have religious freedom — the fact that you can live your life and wear a hijab loud and proud, walking around with it, is a testament to the fact that you have religious freedom. In that respect, I don't think they're at odds.

I feel like around my family is where I feel safest, and that safety kind of leads to feeling liberated.

Every woman — because if you were caught, like how some people are caught acting up on an airplane or being super rude to somebody at a store, and you're wearing a hijab, I would be disappointed that you're negatively representing Islam, that you're reaffirming, maybe, if someone does view Muslim women negatively, that view, by acting that way. So if you're going to wear a hijab, there is, in my opinion, a big responsibility, because you represent something bigger than yourself — but that's just my personal opinion.

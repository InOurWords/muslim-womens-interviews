# Interview MWI-008

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Keeping my house in order — that's pretty much it, along with family and work.

Faith is basically what our day revolves around. We have to wake up for prayer, so we're constantly reminded of our purpose — whatever it is that we do, we have our faith in mind. For me, as a Muslim woman wearing hijab, when I go outside I'm a representative of my faith, so I carry that with me everywhere I go.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

That depends on how they approach me and what questions they have. I've worked with Islamic nonprofit organizations, and we do a lot of da'wah work — of course, any interaction, everyday interactions, can be da'wah, a means of educating others about Islam. So basically I tell them that we live lives that are constantly conscious and aware of the existence of a Creator, and we obey the commandments He sent. For us, the most important thing is monotheism, the belief in one God, following the prophets He sent, and just being the best human beings we can be.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've been in the US for probably thirty-two years, and in the [CITY 1] area for about twenty years total.

I'm a convert — I converted to Islam when I was twenty years old, but I started learning about it fairly young, in my teens. My experience has been a little different than maybe someone raised in a Muslim family. I came from a Catholic background, was raised in that faith, and always believed in God, always prayed to the Creator, but I didn't know at that time exactly who that Creator was, and I was confused about that for a long time until I found Islam and started comparing Christianity to Islam. When I first converted, I wanted to go all in — since I'd already been exposed for a few years before converting, I wanted to start wearing hijab and start practicing right away, but I met some resistance from family members, so I had to hold back a little. With the hijab specifically, I'd say it took about a year before I was able to start wearing it properly. I actually converted a year before September 11th, so once that happened, it also caused a lot of friction with family members, but eventually they understood that I was committed to my faith and wanted to live it wholeheartedly, including wearing hijab. I think the biggest change after that came after I got married and had children — that's when hijab doesn't just become part of you, it becomes part of your family, because you're constantly with your children as a woman, so you're visibly Muslim, and you become either a target or an ambassador, depending on who you're dealing with when you're outside. That has shaped me to become more aware of my surroundings, stronger in my faith, and more prepared to answer questions and to protect myself and my family with regard to any hostility that may arise from my identity as a Muslim woman.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO ISLAM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s03 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_come_to_islam -->

I started learning about Islam when I came across the autobiography of Malcolm X. I started reading it when I was about fifteen, sixteen years old, and I also watched the movie, directed by Spike Lee, about his life. I was fascinated by Islam, and specifically by what happened to him after he made the pilgrimage to Mecca — before that he was part of the Nation of Islam, more into the Black power or Black Muslim movement, but once he went to the Middle East and saw Muslims of every race, culture, and ethnicity, he realized Islam was actually a unifying force for good rather than something that divides. He realized that racism wasn't a problem throughout the world in the way he'd thought, but rather a problem specific to the United States — of course it exists elsewhere too, but he was able to separate the two and see that Islam was the answer to those kinds of isms, like racism and colorism. When I learned about that, I was very curious what exactly Islam was. About a year after I was exposed to Malcolm X, I met a Muslim family from Egypt, and they helped me get hold of literature about Islam and answer questions I had. I actually approached them with my questions, and they graciously answered and offered me whatever reading material I needed. That's how I started reading about Islam on my own, and that eventually led me to convert.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

As I was reading about Islam, I already knew that if I was going to start practicing this religion, I wanted to wear hijab, because covering was something that was already constant in my life, even as a Catholic — I was raised around nuns, I was in Catholic school, and the women there, in the school and the church, wore a habit very similar to what a Muslim woman wears. As a Catholic, the figure of the Virgin Mary is very important, and at home, church, and school I would see images of her wearing a veil. So I always knew that covering was something that would make a woman closer to God — as Catholics we learned that the Virgin Mary, peace be upon her, was the epitome of a righteous woman. So it didn't seem strange to me, when I learned about Islam, that women should cover — it seemed like the natural thing to do, something that had been lost in other cultures and religions. So I embraced hijab wholeheartedly, and I wanted to start wearing it right after I converted. But I was living with my parents, and they didn't really approve of that, because it was new for them, even though they were Catholic too and saw the correlation — they were also more aware that society doesn't accept this as a normal thing, especially from someone of my background. So at first I had some resistance, and I would only wear it to the masjid when I went to pray, or if I was going out with other Muslim girls to a Muslim gathering. Eventually, a few months after I converted, I was able to get a job with a couple of Muslim doctors who had a practice, and because they were Muslim doctors I convinced my parents that, since I'd be working in their office, I should wear hijab to work. My parents agreed, and I started wearing hijab to work. Then, a few months later, September 11th happened, and at that point my parents asked me to take the hijab off, because they thought it was too dangerous for me to wear it outside, given the hostility toward Muslims right after 9/11. So what I would do is put the hijab in my purse when I left the house, then put it on once my parents didn't know, and remove it before coming home so they wouldn't realize I'd been wearing it outside. That happened for a while, until I eventually moved away for college, and at that point I told them I was going to wear hijab full-time. They saw that I was able to get a job — I actually had a job interview wearing hijab, and I got the job. When they saw I was able to do those things with hijab, they accepted it, and I've been wearing it ever since — I think it's been since 2002 that I started wearing it regularly.

I think it's just as the Qur'an says — it's an identifier, a form of obedience; you're following the commandment of Allah. It also separates a person from everyone else, in the sense that Allah says you'll be known as a believing woman. Another thing it does is deter you from doing things that aren't becoming of hijab — when you're covered and outside, people understand you're a Muslim woman, or at least a woman of faith, so they won't expect bad behavior from you, indecency, rowdy behavior. It really makes you aware of God, raises that God-consciousness, and puts a standard on you of modesty and decency that maybe you wouldn't have without that constant reminder that you're representing your faith the moment you step outside.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

There's this prevailing misunderstanding, or stereotype, about Muslim women being oppressed, or obligated to wear it, forced to wear it by the men in our lives — our husbands, our fathers. That's just not the case; it's a very personal decision. I'm sure that in some cultures women may feel more pressure to wear it, but for me it wasn't pressure at all — it was something I wanted to do for myself. There's also this notion that Muslim women are uneducated, that the more covered you are, the more uneducated you are, but I've found it's completely the opposite. Honestly, out of all the women I've met in my life, I think Muslim women are among the most educated, the most successful academically and professionally. I'm really proud to be among Muslim women, especially those who've chosen to wear hijab, because it's a courageous thing to do, especially now, in this age.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

I try to help inform people, but it does get frustrating at times, because there are people who choose to remain ignorant. I think the more years pass, the more fed up you get with these different attacks on Muslim women and on Muslims in general. But you have to choose your battles and know who to ignore and who is genuinely searching for truth versus who just wants to attack Islam by any means possible — usually they'll attack women because they feel it's the easiest target, and that's just sad, not really worth our time. At the end of the day, I've also learned that this is just part of our test in this life — dealing with the ignorant haters. All the prophets had to deal with the same thing, and I'm sure the women of their time did too. So we just have to keep moving on and being strong for our faith.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I've moved around quite a lot in my life, because of the nature of my father's work — we used to move every two or three years. I've had to be uprooted so many times, and even after I got married we still moved around quite a bit. What that has made difficult for me has been establishing friendships and having a sense of belonging in my community, and I think that's made even more difficult by the fact that I'm a convert, and that I'm not of the same ethnicity as the majority of people in our congregations at the mosque. That lack of support, of genuine friendships, is difficult, because it can get very lonely — I don't live in the same city as my parents, so I don't have family around; I only have one older brother, and he lives pretty far away. That's been, I think, probably the most difficult part of my life. But the way I see it, if it hadn't been for these moves and these interactions, people coming in and out of my life, even without producing lasting relationships, it helped — had I stayed back home and not moved around so much, I would have never had the opportunity to learn as much as I have. So I'm grateful for that.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

There are expectations placed on women from all angles. We're expected, not just as Muslim women but as women in general, to be able to have a perfect life, a perfect household, to carry ourselves with perfect adab, perfect manners, at all times, to keep it together, stay beautiful, stay fit — so many things, but it's not realistic. Then, of course, from within the Muslim community, there are expectations about how to behave, what's acceptable and what's not, and who is "more Muslim" than other people. I've had encounters where people question your faith, question your knowledge, basically because of who you are or your background. As for how to overcome that — we're all on our own journeys, we all know the difficulties in our own lives, so we just have to ignore people's expectations and know that the only One we serve is Allah, at the end of the day, and that's who we're returning to. As long as we're doing what we have to do to please Allah, anything else is really just noise.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s09 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

Alhamdulillah, I haven't had many difficulties. There was a period when I wore niqab, just to test it out — the community I was in at the time, the majority of women wore niqab, so I would wear it to the masjid. Then I moved to Egypt for a while and wore it there, and came back to the US expecting to continue wearing it. But I found it was very difficult — I felt disconnected from people, and the level of discrimination was even more intense with niqab. So it eventually became a decision I had to make, to stop wearing it altogether. I wore it maybe a year and a half to two years — a year and a half consistently, maybe two years on and off. It was a huge burden to decide to take it off, because I felt like I was doing something wrong, but then I had to reconcile with that. I read about the rulings on it and concluded this is not an obligation for a woman, and that it would be better for me, for my family, not to wear it. I was also attacked — not physically, but verbally, in front of my children, on more than one occasion, when I was out in stores wearing niqab. That was another deterrent — I said, I can't bring this attention to myself, because I'm not just bringing it to myself, I'm bringing it to my kids, and I want to keep them safe. So that was the reason I stopped wearing it.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s10 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

My family, absolutely — my family, my children, my husband, and then my parents. I think they shape my choices a lot — definitely for anything professionally, academically, economically, all decisions really go back to keeping my family in mind. Of course, my family is second to Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala, so any decisions that concern my religion, I have to know I'm serving Allah first, but after that, it's my family.

No — I think faith is something personal. I guess if you go back to the niqab issue... but ultimately I decided I didn't want to wear it, and even that decision was shaped by my relationship with Allah. I was asking myself, at the point where I wanted to take it off, was I taking it off for people, was I putting it on for people at this point, or was I doing it for Allah? And I realized I wasn't doing it for Allah anymore at the end — the reason it was so difficult for me to take it off was because I kept thinking about what people would think, now that they were used to seeing me like this, if I took it off. So at that point I said, I'm not doing it for Allah anymore, so that's why (I stopped). But any other decision that has to do with my faith always goes back to Allah, not people.

Not that I'm aware of — it was difficult for me, but I didn't feel anything outwardly; at least no one let me feel anything. I discussed my decision with other sisters I knew, and I was surprised when one of them told me she had done the same thing — I had no idea she'd worn niqab before, but she told me she took it off because of her children, because of the safety issue. Once I saw it wasn't something rare, I felt more comfortable, and it was just a matter of me overcoming my own fears about what people would think or how they would react.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s11 | question_id: role_family_community -->

That's a deep question. I understand my role in my family as raising my children and being the best example for them, making sure I'm doing the things that please Allah the most, and that I'm taking care of my household and being a good wife. Outside, in my community, I feel like family takes priority, but community comes second — how can I serve my community? I think being able to do whatever you can, being present, is important, helping whenever help is needed. But I have to admit it can be difficult to juggle both — I have a bigger family, a lot of children, and they take a lot of my time, so knowing I can't do as much volunteer work can sometimes make me feel guilty that I'm not doing enough. But I try my best.

Alhamdulillah, I've been given the opportunity to do a lot of nonprofit work, work in da'wah outreach, and also in education and writing. I think my writing is my biggest contribution to the community — I write on issues that affect our community, and that feels like the biggest way I give back. I used to be more active going out into the community, doing more things physically, but that was when I had fewer children and was younger. Now I have a role that's more in the background, but at least I feel like I do something, and that's a blessing in itself.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

Belonging — I feel like it's difficult to feel a sense of belonging, especially with rising Islamophobia and anti-Muslim bigotry. We often feel othered, and that's on a macro scale; on a micro scale, even within our own community, we can sometimes feel like we don't belong, depending on whether we're people of color, whether we're of a different ethnic background than the majority of the congregation, or whether our level of practice is different from other people's — we may feel like we don't belong in certain spaces. At the same time, there are moments when the Muslim community feels like your safe space, because of how much hate comes from other groups. I also feel like people of faith in other communities offer that sense of belonging, because they're going through some of the same things the Muslim community is going through. I've had the opportunity to study interreligious relations alongside people from different faith backgrounds — Catholic, Christian, Jewish — and you see that people who are committed to social justice, committed to their faith, are fighting some of the same battles we're fighting, and they recognize the difficulties, so they offer that sense of belonging too. But ultimately you have to be in a place where you feel like you fit in, where you feel close to Allah — I think that's the ultimate tranquility, having that reminder that Allah is with you no matter what.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s13 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

I think what Muslims are facing is definitely different, because we're not the majority — we're just a little over one percent of the population here. The anti-Muslim hate, the xenophobia, the Islamophobia, is a real problem. But there's also an underlying problem: this society has become less and less attached to religion. Some people call it a Christian nation — it wasn't founded on Christianity, but it is a Christian-majority nation — yet even Christian churches have lost a lot of their congregations; a lot of people have abandoned their churches. People of faith, especially in leadership positions, see these dwindling numbers of people going to church, being involved in their places of worship. There are seminaries throughout the US that house not just Christians and Jews but also Muslims, studying in these institutions, and there are dwindling numbers of students there too — some institutions have had to close down or shift to online learning to offset costs. Because of that, leadership sees a threat coming from people who want to do away with faith altogether, or with public displays of religion, and they see that the Muslim community, since we're the biggest target of that, means there can be a threat to themselves as well — if you do away with one faith, eventually it comes down to the next one, and the next one. I think that's what has sounded the alarm on that issue.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s14 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

Honestly, outside, I have not. You sometimes feel an air of tension depending on where you are, because of your faith, because of the way you look, or at least you assume that. But I think the place you see a lot of the hatred, a lot of things boiling, is online. We've had this shift to social media over the past ten or fifteen years, more and more on our phones, our devices, interacting with people online, and you see the polarization, people spewing all kinds of hatred online, and that makes it more apparent that there's a problem. When we're living our day-to-day lives, going outside, going from place to place, our jobs, school, or even just to the supermarket, we don't feel the anti-Muslim hate — if we're blessed enough, we're not going to experience any physical attacks or anything like that. But when we go into that online space, where people interact without any filters, you see the things people are saying, and you wonder, is this what people are actually thinking, because they've been emboldened by having a screen to hide behind, so they're able to say whatever's actually on their mind. That creates a kind of tension when you go out and wonder if the people you see online are the same people you're interacting with outside.

## WHO GETS TO REPRESENT MUSLIM WOMEN IN RESEARCH, MEDIA, OR AI?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s15 | question_id: followup__who_gets_to_represent_muslim_women_in_research -->

The reception has been mixed. Some people are open and welcoming, and there are others who still have some suspicions, or who have expressed how difficult it is for Muslims to get a platform to even speak on their own religion — which I find really fascinating, because I've heard from Christian scholars and speakers who are out giving lectures, offering classes, and speaking on Islam. I asked, why is it that Muslims aren't able to speak for themselves? And I was told that people don't trust Muslims. It's eye-opening that people would choose to listen to someone outside our faith to explain our faith, rather than speak to one of us directly, so we can explain ourselves and our motivations behind our decisions to practice.

In academia, especially, to be white and Christian and male gets you a pass — that's the first tier. To be white, Christian, and female gets you the second tier. Academic spaces are dominated by white Christians, so they get the credibility and the opportunities to speak about others more frequently than people of color, and definitely more frequently than people of minority religions, regardless of race or ethnicity. So, as Muslims, we don't have the same clout to speak, and that's because there's a system in place that really suppresses the voices of people of color, of people of minority religions, in this country and elsewhere in the West.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s16 | question_id: akhirah -->

The Akhirah is a reminder — we're only here temporarily, so we have to prepare for leaving this world, and anything we do, we have to maximize our effort to do something good. I think about the concept of sadaqah jariyah, ongoing charity — what can I do that's going to help me even after I leave this world? That's why I really enjoy writing; I feel it's a powerful way to leave a lasting message, because writing is something that stays with us. Books, articles, even online articles, can stand the test of time and continue to offer me a chance to do good, even when I'm gone.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s17 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Allah is in control. Allah is always in control, and we fool ourselves into thinking that we control things, but at the end of the day we have to remind ourselves that Allah is in control of everything, and we have to put our trust completely in His infinite knowledge and wisdom.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s18 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

What matters most, of course, is doing these things with sincerity, for the sake of Allah first and foremost. But one of the worldly benefits is that we're here as a minority religion in the United States, and we have the opportunity to show people what Islam really is — so we have that responsibility because we're here. As for me, I was born in the Western hemisphere, so this is my home, and I don't have much choice in that matter — of course I could pick up and try to move to another country, but it's much more difficult for somebody born in the United States, with a US passport, to move their family to a different country and stay there permanently. So we're here, and we have the opportunity to show people what Islam really is, to contribute to this society in a positive way, and through our daily interactions to show people who Muslims really are. Those of us living here have that great opportunity to acquire good deeds and show people the best of us, so they can see that Islam is more than what they see on the news or depicted on television and online.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s19 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

Self-awareness — I think I mentioned before that hijab provides a reminder, and I think it's the same with acts of worship: praying five times a day, fasting, they all serve as a reminder that God is watching, and that we're responsible to Him for our actions. Those acts of worship deter us from doing things that are evil or don't align with our faith, so they provide a certain awareness and mindfulness of who we are. For me, as a Muslim woman, I feel like hijab and the things I do as a Muslim are part of my identity — they become part of you, and I think this is especially the case for Muslim women. Hijab becomes part of you, you can't separate yourself from it, because hijab is the first thing people see when they look at you, so it just becomes part of who you are.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s20 | question_id: liberation -->

Liberation is freeing your mind — freeing your mind of what people, of what society, is choosing for you, or what the majority is doing, or what people deem acceptable or unacceptable, man-made trends and things like that. Freeing your mind from those things is liberation. Liberation is being able to practice your faith and know that no one can take that away from you, and being able to choose what you want to do in your life — to me, that is liberation. There's this notion in this society that women need to be liberated, especially Muslim women, because people see us in hijab, and hijab is supposed to be something that oppresses. But meanwhile, women in this society aren't free either — they're not free to choose to be a person of faith; a person of faith who dresses modestly is looked down upon. Only about a hundred and fifty years ago, all women in this country dressed modestly, no matter their religious background. So there's this hypocrisy about what women should and shouldn't do, and that's not liberation — that's more oppressive, for women not to be able (to choose modesty) and to work and be like men and things like that. I feel like that's more oppressive than a Muslim woman who's able to protect her modesty, isn't harassed outside because of the way she looks, and is able to practice her faith freely and live her life according to what she believes is best for her. To be able to choose that for yourself — that is more liberating than anything else.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s21 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah — it's just a beautiful way that we say it in my language, instead of submission we say surrender, like surrendering yourself. That is surrendering every part of you, everything that you do, to your Creator.

Submission can feel like it has a negative connotation, like you're submissive, right? But when it comes to the Creator, you're surrendering yourself, you're giving yourself to your Creator, your deeds and your actions, to the Creator. When you think of it that way, you realize that, because we belong to God, we're admitting to ourselves that we're not in control, and that we owe everything to our Creator. So we give ourselves, we surrender ourselves, to Him.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-008_s22 | question_id: liberation -->

There's a hadith that says the world is a prison for the believer and a paradise for the disbeliever. So when you think of yourself as submitted into this world, you're a prisoner to this world, but when you think about the Creator, that's beyond this reality — submitting, surrendering yourself to your Creator, goes beyond that and is liberating you from this world. Our paradise is with our Creator, but our prison is in this life.

# Interview MWI-009

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Right now I'm a recent graduate from [UNIVERSITY 1], so I'm taking life a little slow, just relaxing, and working my side job, which is nannying. I drive to my shift, usually in different parts of the [METRO REGION 1], but a lot of times they have shifts in the [SUBURB 1]/[CITY 1] area, so I drive down there, clock in, watch the kid I'm assigned to, then clock out and go home — that's been my daily routine the past couple of weeks. In regards to interacting with people, that happens a lot when I'm shopping and stuff like that, and I'd say most of my experiences interacting with people have been positive, now and in the past. I've been wearing hijab since I was about five or six years old, from a very young age, and at one point I even wore niqab, but that was only for about a year, and I never took it off since then, or the hijab. So alhamdulillah, I don't think I've ever had a negative experience, and I would attest that to me mainly being around Muslims.

I grew up going to [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1], which is a private Islamic school. I was there from pre-K all the way to eighth grade, so most of my childhood experience was being at that school, where everyone was Muslim. My extracurriculars were also at that school — I did karate there too, predominantly with Muslims as well — and with my family we would go around to the rec center and the pools, and that's the way I would interact with other people from different faiths and people of all kinds.

But I would say I never felt isolated or targeted because of my hijab. I will say that when Islamophobia was extra targeted in the media, there was a rise in attacks — it's still happening now, but I'd say maybe around 2018, 2019, around those years — I felt a little scared, seeing the things that were happening to people who looked like me, people who were Muslim, people who wore hijab, how they were targeted, and hearing their experiences. I definitely felt scared sometimes to go outside with my hijab, and especially during the time I was wearing niqab, I was even more scared. But alhamdulillah, I think Allah blessed me with being around good people, and I never had to interact with anybody who had negative thoughts of me or who would hate me for wearing hijab. I will say people definitely have questions — I've encountered that a lot, people coming up and asking why I wear it, if I'm hot in it, and stuff like that. Since I grew up around Muslims, I was happy people asked, to show that they were curious and actually wanted to learn, so I answered them happily.

Faith is a very big part of my life. I try to use it as a guide every step of the way, so I think it shows in every choice I make — it's backed up by my religion, always at the forefront of my mind. For example, let's say I'm clocking into my nannying shifts — a lot of times I'm working with different families who don't know me, so I'm meeting them for the first time when I knock on their door. One time I knocked on the door and the father answered and held his hand out, like, "oh, nice to meet you, my name is this and that." As a Muslim woman, I'm not supposed to shake hands with another man who isn't a close relative. That doesn't happen too often, since I'm not really interacting with men much, but when it does, I get a little shy or embarrassed to say no to that person and shy away their hand, or say, "oh, I can't do this, I'm a Muslim woman," and state my values right then and there. I've been more intentional about that, so when that incident happened, I put my hand to my chest and said, "oh, I'm sorry, I don't shake hands with men," and the person was very responsive to that. I guess that's one way my faith showed up in that moment, when I made that decision to stand by my faith and my values.

In other cases in the past, when a man held out his hand and I had to say no but was too shy or embarrassed, I didn't say no, and ended up shaking his hand, and felt really guilty, like I hadn't stood by my values — which is what led me to be more intentional, to stand by my values even if nobody else around me is, even if there are Muslims who sometimes shake hands with other men. That's sometimes why other people, non-Muslims, don't understand when I say no. I had an incident once at school where I made that decision to stop shaking hands with men, and said, "oh, I'm sorry, I don't shake hands with men," and they said, "oh, but this other person did" — and that other person was a Muslim woman — and I said, "yeah, but everybody goes about their life their own way and makes their own decisions." So that's one way where, even if other Muslims aren't doing something and I have to be the odd one out, my faith stands up for me.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I don't think I've been asked that question, but if they were to ask, I would say that my community is very close, and we're very much bound by our faith — our faith very much connects us, and we're always there for each other. I'll say specifically my local community, instead of the entire Muslim community: I'm talking about the [MASJID 1] community, because that's what I've been around the most. Even though I did grow up around [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1], I haven't been there in many years, so [MASJID 1] is now my community. We're very connected, and we all have different strengths and play into each other's strengths. We meet often, we have different youth events, and I love that we can all come together and talk about our issues and relate to each other, especially living in the West, where sometimes you don't meet someone going through the same thing as you and can feel alone in that. To come to a masjid and be around my community, we can all talk about what we're dealing with and feel less alone, because we know the other person is most likely feeling that way too. Yes, I've been part of the [STATE 1] community all my life.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

From when I was a child, I grew up around predominantly Muslims, so that was just what I am — I'm a Muslim — and it was something I didn't even have to think about, it was very ingrained in me. But that was part of an issue for me too, because eventually I needed to go find who I am outside of that community. Since I'd been around this community my whole life and been told certain things my entire life, when I wasn't around these people, I didn't know where I stood, what my values and opinions were outside of it.

After I went to college, I was actually in an environment with very few Muslims — going from being around Muslims literally all the time to a space with barely any. I tried to look out for Muslims there and made friends, though those friends happened to be from the mosque and went to that same school, so I stuck with them. In the first semester I didn't really know anybody — the friends I'd made from the mosque weren't there yet either — so I was basically alone. I didn't feel like I really connected with anybody, and didn't even feel the need to, I guess, because I felt faith was such a big part of me that I wouldn't be able to connect with someone of a different faith, since we didn't hold the same values. So I isolated myself a little, and also started dressing differently because I wanted to fit in and not stand out. I had always worn abayas — that was my dress code, I never wore anything else — but at that school I didn't want to wear one anymore, because I didn't see a single person there wearing one. So I started wearing pants and shirts, sometimes skirts, which I'd worn before too, but not the abaya specifically — that I stopped wearing.

At first it was a little exciting, something new and different, but I'd say I also felt a little empty, like I was losing part of myself. Even though it seemed small, it was still a value I held closely, connected to my faith, a big part of me, so shying away from it made me lose myself a little. After that semester, when I reconnected with friends from the mosque who came to that same school, I stuck around them, and we hung out for the rest of my time there. I felt more comfortable wearing abayas again, and then I also started seeing other Muslim students at the school wearing abayas, so I felt even more comfortable.

The biggest difference was when I transferred to a bigger university, [UNIVERSITY 1], which had a lot of Muslims. I was so happy to go there, because at [COLLEGE 1], the previous school, there were barely any Muslims — there was Jumu'ah and an MSA, but it wasn't really active or pushed, so I never went, and my schedule didn't align either, so I never made it to the events they had. But at [UNIVERSITY 1], everything was very different — I saw Muslims all the time, always around, saying salaams, dressing the way I'm most comfortable dressing. There was Jumu'ah, and I started going back to it — I hadn't been able to at [COLLEGE 1] — and I felt a lot more connected to my community and my faith that way. They had a lot of events, and even though I think it's a PWI, I found a lot of diverse communities there. There were also other cultural communities — I'm Somali, Egyptian, and Yemeni, so there was a Somali club and an Egyptian club, a lot more opportunities to connect with different parts of myself at that school.

So that's how my faith and identity changed as I grew. First I was in a stage where I was constantly around people like me, around my community a lot, and that was all I knew. Then in my adolescent stage, figuring out who I was, I felt a little lost and wanted to get away from the community to figure that out on my own. Then I went into college and was put in an environment where I was literally alone — that was a struggle, and I did lose a bit of myself wanting to fit in and not stand out. Then after reconnecting with my community, I reconnected with my values and what was really important to me. Yeah, that's where I am now.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

When I was younger, for as long as I can remember, I absolutely loved the hijab. I started wearing it around age five or six because I loved it so much — I loved playing around with different styles and dressing it up different ways. I don't think I wore it every single day from age five, but for sure by the time I reached puberty, around nine years old, I wore it every day, and I haven't taken it off since. I love my hijab. I struggled a little with wearing it the correct way — in my adolescence, around thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, I had thoughts of not wanting to wear it correctly, wanting to show my hair, wanting to experiment with different styles. I went through that phase, and I'd say I still go through some of those struggles — I want to experiment with different styles, even though I know what hijab is; I see other people getting to experiment with styles in their lives, with hijab too, people wear it so many different ways, and I want to experiment sometimes too, even though I know it may not be the most correct way.

Even though I do struggle with it, I can never see myself taking it off — it's part of me, that's who I am, I can't imagine not wearing hijab, that wouldn't be who I am. I'd say it's also been a protection for me, from things that are bad. When I see people in the streets and they compliment my hijab, say how much they like it, and know I'm Muslim, they come up and start asking me questions about Islam. I'd say wearing hijab is a representation of my religion, and since my religion is a big part of me, I really value that.

At the beginning of my life, I wore it just because that's what everyone around me did — my mom wore it, everyone around me wore it, that's just what everyone did. Later in life is when I had to understand and learn the real meaning of hijab — not that I wasn't taught it, but I had to seek that out for myself. I learned that later, and I'm still learning. Despite struggling sometimes with hijab, and not always wanting to wear it the right way, I have to understand there is a certain way ordained for Muslim women by God, for reasons He has told us, but also some reasons are just (part of) His wisdom, and I have to trust that. That's something I took in, I'd say, later in my life.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I'd say sometimes non-Muslims may be a little intimidated by people in hijab and think we're all the same — that all hijabis are a certain way, act a certain way, are very rigid, and don't have personality. Sometimes I do have that in the back of my head — whenever I'm meeting someone who doesn't know much about Muslims, or it's their first time seeing a hijabi, I have that thought that they probably think I'm a certain way, that they have a preconceived notion of who I am. Then they get to know me and it's like, I'm not what they thought a Muslim woman would be. I think people sometimes don't think of Muslim women as actual people who have different personalities — we're not a completely different kind of creature, we're all human, we think similarly, we work the same, it's just that we have different values, and this head covering is part of something that I value.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

I've never encountered any discriminatory acts toward me because of my hijab. I will say I've seen some false representation, not specifically in my life, but in the media. In certain shows I watch, I see people wearing hijab and they're not really representing it the way it should be — for example, there was a Spanish show where a girl was a hijabi and later took the hijab off because of a boy; the meaning of hijab wasn't really represented accurately in that show, I believe. I see that often in small clips here and there in the media — shows and movies where the actual meaning of hijab isn't really talked about, you just see the covering, and it's not really addressed why this person is covering. So I don't think it's been represented accurately in the media, but I'd say recently we're getting more accurate representation. Growing up, I never saw an accurate representation of Muslim women unless it was from YouTube and Instagram, from personal accounts of Muslim hijabi women — I would follow those because I thought that was very relatable content, since they were Muslim women doing editorials and daily vlogs and stuff like that, so I thought that was accurate because they were representing themselves. But from the broader media, growing up, I didn't see any accurate representations.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

Let me think — a difficulty I faced recently, I'd say financial difficulties. Before going to [UNIVERSITY 1], I knew I had to be the one to completely pay for my tuition myself. At that point I was just working my nannying job, my babysitting job, and that's not consistent — I had to find another job, find a way to pay my way through the next two years at university, and it was a lot more money than community college, so I was very much stressing. But I really had conviction that I wanted to go to [UNIVERSITY 1], so the previous month I took a semester off and saved up as much as I could, but I only saved enough for one semester, so I decided to go with that and look for jobs during that semester to pay my way for the next three. That was a really stressful time, because looking for jobs itself is stressful, and looking for jobs that fit my school schedule was even more stressful, since it was also my first semester at university and the workload was a lot different than at community college. It was really hard adjusting, and I was constantly in the mindset of needing to make this money, needing to find ways — it was a constant stress on me.

During that time, I relied a lot on my faith, relied a lot on God, and put my conviction in Him that He would help me, even though I didn't see a solution in front of me. I didn't have one, I just worked as much as I could, but it wasn't amounting to the amount I needed. I really held on to my faith, and thankfully I graduated, and I do have some money left to pay, but not a lot — I don't have much debt. I give a lot of credit to my faith, because I tried my hardest — I really did tie my camel — I tried as hard as I could to find ways to make money, even while in school, and with my reliance and conviction in God, I made it through and overcame that difficulty and stressful time.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

Yes, I do think there are some internal expectations within the Muslim community, especially for Muslim women. We're kind of the representation of Islam — when Muslim men go out in public, you can't really tell if they're Muslim. So I'll say yes, Muslim women, hijabi women specifically, are meant to act a certain way, because we're basically representing Islam.

I was basically saying that because Muslim women outwardly represent Islam through hijab, you can quickly identify that a woman is Muslim if she's wearing one, in contrast to men, where you can't really tell if they're Muslim or not. Because of that, Muslim women are expected to act in a way that represents Islam — she can't really do wrong, or do things that contradict Islam, because then she'll give people the wrong idea. So I think there's extra pressure on women who wear hijab to be, in quotes, on their best behavior, because people are watching and they know you're Muslim. I think that pressure of always having to act right, in a certain way, kind of intimidates some Muslim women who want to wear hijab from doing so, because of that pressure. I see a lot of Muslim women actually taking off their hijab because of that, because there's so much pressure from their community to be perfect.

As for external expectations, from outside people, I'm not quite sure — I don't assume so, because they don't really know what hijab is or what it represents. When someone sees a Muslim woman, they kind of judge the entirety of Islam based off that; it's almost an inherent reaction, like: this hijab-wearing woman I know is a Muslim, she's acting this way, so this is how all of Islam is, how all Muslims act. So when someone sees a Muslim woman wearing hijab, they may judge other Muslims based on her actions, whether good or bad, because they associate her with other Muslims.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s13 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

That's a good question. To answer it, thinking about people's experiences in Muslim countries in contrast to mine — I see myself longing for that, to be in a Muslim country where you go out and a lot of people, maybe not every single person, are Muslim. You don't have to find Muslims in the crowd, they're just there, and people automatically understand things about you, particularly faith-wise — they just understand and accept it, and I don't have to explain certain choices I have to make. In Muslim countries, they celebrate Ramadan and Eid, and those celebrations are a big thing, whereas in this country it's not.

Here, as a faith, we're a minority, and some parts of our faith aren't really accepted the way they are for other faiths — for example, Christian holidays like Christmas and Easter are a big celebration, people get work and school off for them, but for Muslims we don't get that for our celebrations. So there's kind of a divide, where certain parts of my faith aren't really made for this country, whereas in Muslim countries it's the opposite.

Eid isn't celebrated the way we celebrate it ourselves. It often falls on different days during the year, and sometimes it lands on school or work days, so you don't get time off, and it doesn't even feel like a holiday, whereas for Christmas and Easter there's a lot of advertisement in stores and you get time off — it's a big, accepted thing here, where Eid and Ramadan aren't. Another thing is halal meat — recently there are more stores selling zabihah halal meat, but in the past there weren't. I remember going to [GROCERY CHAIN 1] a couple of months ago and seeing they actually have a halal section, and I was really surprised, I was shocked. I know Costco has it too now, maybe for a couple of years, I just hadn't noticed. But those things weren't normal growing up — you couldn't go to a regular store and ask for halal meat, it would have to be a halal store. So there are certain things where you have to put in extra effort to accommodate your values, whereas other people don't have to, because it's embedded in this country — I'll say specifically the Christian faith. There are also stores, like [GROCERY CHAIN 2], that have a kosher section, so they're accommodating Jewish culture too, but I don't see any halal sections in those stores. That's where I see the difference and the divide.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

There's a hadith, or a saying, that in comparison to God's throne, we're like a ring in a very vast desert — if you dropped a ring in a very vast desert, that's how small we are in comparison to the throne of God. ## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-009_s20 | question_id: liberation -->

I think liberation means the freedom to express yourself in any way you want. I experience that, I guess, through my faith, and through feeling accepted, being able to explore and do what I want while also still being accepted by my community and the people around me. That's how I feel liberated.

I felt really liberated seeing all the Muslims around me doing what they do — they were all in different clubs, and a really big one was the MSA. They held many different events — a barbecue, an event where you could play bowling and pool and different games — a bunch of different activities, whether directly related to faith or just recreational, and that was very liberating for me, because I could be around like-minded people and still do all the activities that people in other communities do. If those other communities did those activities, I might not feel as comfortable going, because I don't really know anybody, and I don't feel like I'll connect with anybody, since we don't have one big thing in common, which is religion. So those were certain times I felt liberated, and joining [UNIVERSITY 1] was a very liberating experience, especially seeing Muslim hijabi women wearing long clothing — I also saw a lot of niqabis there, which was very surprising, because I had never seen a niqabi at my previous college, even though it was smaller. Just seeing young adult Muslim women sticking to and holding onto their values and faith, even in a big college like [UNIVERSITY 1], which is a PWI, so there are so many different people who may not understand why you do what you do, and sometimes you're in classes where you're the only POC, or the only hijabi, or the only Muslim — seeing those people still hold onto their values made me feel liberated. It made me feel more comfortable to do the same.

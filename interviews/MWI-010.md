# Interview MWI-010

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

I'm an only child. I have two parents who work full-time, so I think a lot of my day is me left to my own devices, kind of getting through my day, in general.

Well, I sleep late, so I wake up late — the first time I wake up is for Fajr, and then usually I go back to sleep and wake up again for the day. Most of the time my days include college classes; in the past three years they have, before that it was classes in general, so I'd say I'm a student above everything else. I'll have something to eat — most of the time I do skip breakfast — then I'll try to do some work after whatever class I have, and I'll end the day with an episode of something.

Faith shows up in Salah most consistently, and it shows up in conversations with people. When I speak to someone, it always starts with salaam — that's when I'm speaking to Muslims, which is the majority of the people I surround myself with. The only time I was surrounded by non-Muslims was my two years at [UNIVERSITY 1], and even then Islam showed up in my conversations, because even when I made friends, I'd tell them, for five minutes I'm just going to go pray, and sometimes I'd explain, sometimes I wouldn't. In the past two years is the first time I even had a non-Muslim friend, so all of these experiences were pretty brand new for me. Just talking about Islam is probably where it shows up, and it shows up on Fridays, because I try to be more involved — I try to do more Quran on Fridays, I try to go to Jumu'ah, kind of reconnect. Another part of my routine is a Quran class once a week that's not on a Friday. I think those are the most consistent ways Islam shows up in my life.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I struggled with that a lot in the past two years. I went to [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1] — I would have a hard time explaining it, so what I ended up sticking with was that [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1] is a K-through-12 private Muslim school, and that's kind of how I'd introduce myself to people when they asked about my schooling experience.

I would explain that I spent my time there on the girls' side — my teachers were women, my classmates were girls — and that influenced the way I saw things, like group projects, where I had a hard time communicating with my male classmates. Eventually, in my last semester, I got really good at treating everyone properly — not professionally, just without being intimidated, I guess. I'd say my community is really close, and I had to remove some of that closeness when I was interacting with people at [UNIVERSITY 1]. For example, my teachers at [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1] versus my professors at [UNIVERSITY 1] — there was more distance, and that was hard to transition into, because in my environment you know someone's grandparents, their siblings, their parents, how they live their lives, for the most part. As Muslims, we know everyone's at Jumu'ah on Fridays, or this day is special — Ashura recently came in, and that's something that brings me closer to my community members, because I kind of know them. It was different to explain that to people, because they'd be like, how is it possible that you're close to this many people, or that you know such things about this many people? And I'm like, the base is that we're all Muslim first. I'm not going to lie, eventually I just ended up not explaining — even with Salah, toward the end of my time at [UNIVERSITY 1], I would just say I have a meeting, and then I'd leave and come back to whatever we were doing.

It would be like the list of questions — hi, what's your major, what high school did you go to — because there were a lot of networking-type events, so I was constantly introducing myself. Hi, my name is [INTERVIEWEE], I'm an econ major, and then the follow-up questions, but it would be a couple minutes long and then you'd do it with the next person.

I had this one friend — she's Indian, and India has a lot of Muslims, but her family wasn't Muslim — and she knew enough about me that when I had to go pray or anything, it wasn't even a conversation, she'd be like, oh, okay. But she had Muslim friends too — two guys specifically — and whatever I told her about being Muslim was contrary to how they were acting. For example, she had a graduation party, and the boys were drinking at the party, and I'd say, I don't drink, I'm Muslim, and she'd look over and see the guy drinking, and I was like, okay.

They were Muslim, and I would constantly be — this was the first time I'd been around Muslims who weren't from my community. The Muslims I grew up with had a certain standard of practicing Islam, and the Muslims I ended up meeting came from everywhere in the U.S., from backgrounds that weren't maybe as strict as the way I was raised, so I'd end up contradicting myself in front of non-Muslims who had met these other Muslims. Sometimes I was kind of dumbfounded, like, now what do I say? It was a tricky thing to navigate. I remember, I told her I was Muslim and she still invited me to the party — it was really just at her apartment, a couple people — and I went, said hi, and then left, because they were acting a little, you know, as these things go.

A more specific example would be interactions between opposite genders — I'd have a certain boundary and other Muslims wouldn't, so I'd look like the more extreme side of the spectrum of Muslims at [UNIVERSITY 1], if that makes sense. Girls would be giving guys hugs, and I'd be like, no — I'll stand over here, or I'll wave, whatever the case may be, and it would confuse a lot of people. Over time I ended up just saying, I have a meeting, when I had to pray, instead of saying, I'm going to pray. But for the people who did understand there was a different spectrum of Muslims — there was another girl, she was white actually, from [STATE 1], and her background gave her more interaction with Muslims. I wasn't her only Muslim friend, and her previous Muslim friend also prayed, so when I said, while we were studying one day, hey, I'm going to go pray, she'd say, yeah, it's probably around the time, and I was like, how do you know that? So it was a degree of interactions, and they were all different.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've been in this community, I think, since kindergarten — I haven't really left this nook of [MASJID 1], [CITY 1], I've lived here a really long time. But my sense of self —

I'll draw a parallel to my time in [CITY 2], since I've been there twice now, each time about two weeks. When I was in that community, my sense of being a Muslim woman changed, and when I'm in my community here, it's also different. The way I grew up, everything was pretty harshly stated in terms of how I, as a Muslim woman, was meant to dress, act, and speak.

When I went into an environment where Muslim girls were encouraged to do things, I hadn't even thought that a whole community would be okay with their daughters — the girls of the community — doing something like that; it would just be natural there. I felt myself more inclined to make Islam a part of my — Islam is a huge part of my identity anyway, but more often than not it feels like a responsibility here. In [CITY 2], I remember thinking it was easier to be a Muslim woman in an environment where people aren't constantly telling me how to be one.

At first I thought it was because a lot of the Muslims I was meeting there followed the Hanafi madhhab, whereas [MASJID 1] is Hanbali — over in [CITY 2] there are a lot of Hanafi masajid. I had an easier time thinking about my future and what was possible, whereas here I feel very confined to a certain path — it's like you either do this or you don't, and that's what makes you a good Muslim woman, when you look back on your life at the end of it.

Here, something that comes to mind is post-grad life. I'm being told that I'm getting older, that times are changing, that people need stability and family before they reach for other goals. In interactions with friends, we're subconsciously mirroring to each other what we've been told, and when we have accomplishments, like getting a job or traveling, it becomes a bigger, more complicated conversation. With parents, family, and friends — even yesterday I went to someone's graduation party, and the group of girls there had been together since elementary school at [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1], and we were discussing how we weren't allowed to travel, not even out of state for college, or when someone has a better opportunity, like an internship a couple months long in another state — it's an amazing opportunity, good for your resume, good for your career trajectory, good for figuring out if you even like this career path you picked at twenty years old. But we're not being allowed — it's more confinement, everyone's being pushed toward staying home in a very frustrating way. I'm seeing people have different reactions in personal interactions that they wouldn't have if they were able to leave their homes and experience things and become more evolved as people. Instead I'm seeing girls I grew up with backtracking to looking like they were raised in the 90s — not that the 90s were super confined, but I feel like we're moving backwards.

I think it might be my circle of people — even when I branch out a little, I'm hit with the same set of values. When I was in [CITY 2], I met girls from different states, specifically at the [ISLAMIC INSTITUTE 1] community, and all those girls had come in from out of state for education, for learning. Hearing their life experiences, I remember thinking I wished some of the girls I knew here had parents who would release them a little to have some of those experiences. It didn't make me sad, but it made me understand our differences more starkly.

I don't know why, because it's not by ethnicity — every friend I have is from a different ethnicity, and all the girls I met over there were too, so I don't think it's culture. It might be that our parents are trying to preserve Islam the best way they think they can, by monitoring us, trying to make sure we don't lose what they think the U.S. is making us lose — what they've taught us, what we've learned. And we've learned a lot, I think. At this point I'm 23, my friends are around 22 to 24, and I see such a difference in our development versus that of our peers, whether at school, at work, or in different states. Everyone's talking as if our lives are over, and I think that's so detrimental, because I feel like I have a lot left to do.

Now everyone's kind of just waiting for marriage, then talking about getting married, having a kid, raising it, and I'm like, I want to do different things — maybe I want to go study overseas in two years and learn something, or move to another state and take a job opportunity and figure out how to do that.

My parents let me travel for my friend's [ISLAMIC INSTITUTE 1] graduation — I didn't study there. When I was there for two weeks, I went to a lot of their final parties, a lot of the halaqas, and met a lot of new people. Another thing I'm noticing is that when you have experiences, you think differently, and then you act differently, and when you don't have a lot of experiences, you're more confined to daily suffocation — mundane things become very suffocating, and then you take it out on the people closest to you, if that makes sense.

I'm seeing that in a lot of my friends now — post-grad, a lot of us are looking for jobs, and it's not easy, so there's like one to two years at home, and their parents are like, well, you can't go out, you can't do this, you can't do that, so everyone's kind of banging their head against a wall trying to figure out how to have the pressure released from them.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

I do. Attending [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1] was my mom's way of me seeking religious knowledge, and it was very helpful — I got a good base of Quran, Arabic, Islamic studies. On my own, I would volunteer at [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1], for the youth — I was there for like four years. At [ISLAMIC INSTITUTE 2] I was a consistent volunteer, and I'd attend those classes, and the local masajid as well. I'm also enrolled in — I don't know if you know — [ONLINE ISLAMIC ACADEMY 1]. It's kind of like a catch-all two-year program for all the bases of Islamic knowledge, though I hesitate to say exactly where they're based, since it's [ONLINE ISLAMIC ACADEMY 1], it might just be through the Saudi government, I'm not sure. I'm going to start in August. Along with my classes, my dhikr, tafsir classes — I do try to seek Islamic knowledge, but inshallah I want to do it in a more structured way in the future.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I've been wearing it since I was a kid, so it wasn't really an active choice, but I'd say when I was in middle school I wore it looser, and that was probably the closest I got to not wearing it the way I do now — I think now I wear it pretty coveringly. I think hijab is also your clothing, so the journey to skirts and abayas was also something that took time. Even now I'll still wear pants from time to time, but looser-fitting. I don't think I really had a problem with that — sometimes it would just be that something didn't fit and I'd need a size bigger so it would sit closer. Overall it was pretty simple, I guess. I think hijab in an environment with Muslims is one thing; in an environment without Muslims, you tend to gravitate more toward pants and less loose clothing, just to fit in. But I don't think I ever went to the side of just not wearing it.

Maybe a couple years ago, when I started looking into hijab materials and abaya styles and things like that, that's probably when I started thinking more about how to make myself feel good in hijab instead of just wearing it out of habit. It makes me feel more comfortable going outside, and I think I first noticed that specifically when I went to Saudi for the first time — I chose on my own to wear niqab there, because I was more comfortable in that country, since if you're the only face people can see, you're the only face being looked at. Hijab just makes me feel comfortable.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

That even amongst girls who don't wear hijab — I have family friends where my mom told me, when I was growing up, that people had questioned her about why I was wearing hijab so young. She would literally put hijab on me and I'd be running around in a short-sleeve shirt. I think that was important, because for me it wasn't something I had to think too long about — it was just something I always did, like when you get ready to go outside, you wear hijab. Those same people, their kids are now very grown, mid-20s, late-20s, early-20s, and they're not covering at all — and it's gotten worse, they're more on the side of non-Muslim attire than just regular attire and not wearing hijab. That's something I noticed. Another thing is that the same people I grew up with, we probably had similar lives, but they would isolate me for being too religious, or it would be an assumption. I'm actually not the best Muslim ever, I promise, but it would be more difficult to connect with some people because I'd be isolated for being too religious. No, it would be an assumption — not based on conversations, just the hijab.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Yes. If I was in a position of closeness to the person who misunderstood, I would clarify. If I wasn't, I'd kind of just let it slide, unfortunately, because it would become a thing they'd talk to their friend about, and it would turn into an altercation. I really don't think that if someone misunderstands something, it's my place — I'm not an alimah, I'm not a sheikh, it's not my place to give nasiha like that. But if it's someone I'm close to, I'll say, hey, this is actually wrong, Islam doesn't say that, this is how I've always practiced it — whether it's the hugging thing, the shaking hands thing, or praying salah on time. Those are things that if I know the person who misunderstood it, I would clarify, because I know that to be true and I know what this person is doing is not. That's as far as I would take it. If I'm close to the person, I'd correct their misunderstanding; if I'm not close to them, I wouldn't want to put myself in a position where they go talk to the Muslim person and put me in a situation of drama — it's not what I went to school for.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

To stick on the theme we've been talking about, I'd say my identity as a Muslim woman in the [UNIVERSITY 1] environment is something I had to think about a lot. There were points where low iman found me more often than not, because I was constantly surrounded by people who seemed like they didn't put in as much effort into doing certain things, but they still got the same results as me, sometimes better — not in terms of academics, but maybe it was easier for them to make friends and maintain them, or easier to get internships or jobs, or have these achievements, and I'd find that frustrating in the moment.

But when it would be summer or winter break, the frustration would dissipate, because I'd be in my own environment again, and I'd understand the way life kind of goes, and my iman would go back up. So I faced this up and down — there was a lot of turmoil in that for about two years straight, and I think, hopefully, now that I'm out of there, it'll go back to normal.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

I think if there was something I could say to a lot of people, it would be that I wish we saw our lives in a different way. There's so much opportunity out there, so many experiences waiting to be had, so many different ways of achieving the same thing, that I really wish, instead of monitoring each other, instead of being jealous, instead of being scared that someone's going to put your experience down so you don't share it, and everyone isolating themselves to protect themselves from evil eye or whatever it may be — it would be better if we could see that we all have time, we all have the opportunity to live full lives without just waiting for marriage, or even graduation, to happen to us. If we could take it easier and stop waiting for the next thing, we'd have fuller lives, and it would make us happier people, because we have, mashallah, a lot of time and energy, and support from our parents, financial or emotional, and if we could take all of that in and let go of what might or might not happen, we'd have better experiences of living — because I look around and see girls who are anxious and exhausted and depressed, and I think we can really make a choice not to live this way.

Culturally, communities that have — for example, in Morocco, there are Christians, there are Jews, there are Muslims, but there's also a culture of not giving too much information, for everybody, whether Muslim or not. But when you come to the West, to our environment here in the U.S., people are much more open with their lives, so there's a lot more connection, and I feel like Muslim girls are constantly scared of each other, maybe subconsciously — how close can you be to someone when all you ever say to them is, hi, how are you doing, alhamdulillah, and that's it? There's nothing below the surface in terms of connection. How are you going to connect with someone if you don't know anything about them?

## HAS WEARING HIJAB CREATED BARRIERS IN EDUCATION OR EMPLOYMENT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s09 | question_id: followup__has_wearing_hijab_created_barriers_in_education_or -->

I think I didn't feel like myself in a lot of my interactions there, because I was putting on this persona of disinterest and nonchalance, because that's how everyone around me was acting. There were people I went to class with two semesters in a row, saw multiple times a week, and I couldn't tell you their names — it was so distant, and that was really hard for me to go through, but I learned, I guess.

I think my degree of practice played into it — I'd wear abayas to school, and hijab of course, and there were times my entire row would be empty, everyone sitting in front of me or behind me. It was the strangest thing, but over time you get used to it, and when I'm outside school I have community, so it wasn't something that put me down, but it was something I noticed.

I'd look around at other Muslims and their experiences, and the ones who did have friends of the opposite gender, or connections with non-Muslim classmates, were living lives I didn't want for myself. So I decided if the alternative was to get involved in things that might distract me or reduce my iman, even though I'm already struggling with it, it wasn't really worth getting hung up on — though that wasn't my initial reaction, that's a conclusion I came to after thinking about it. My initial reaction was loneliness, feeling like, how is this fair — but then you get over it.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s11 | question_id: role_family_community -->

In my family, I'm kind of living what they were unable to do. Even my major — one of my parents began college but wasn't able to complete it, and that was their major, their program. Even finishing high school — one parent wasn't able to do that, so school is connected to that.

In my community, I guess I'm a volunteer, because I end up in situations helping out — event childcare or just being part of helping — that's probably my most consistent role.

As an only child, I feel like I have a lot of free time, for some reason, or maybe I want to make my time free, but I think a lot of people I know seem to have less, so I just go out and help. It helps with connection — I'm the kind of person who feels better interacting with people, whether I've met them before or not, and then I come home feeling better than when I left. Maybe that's why.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

Belonging — I'd say in employment, belonging would be a workplace that lets you be employed without releasing you from your responsibilities as a Muslim. What I look for in a workplace is a place that will allow me to take off during the last ten nights of Ramadan, somewhere to pray salah. At [UNIVERSITY 1] we had the MSA space, which allowed for salah during the day, and a space for weekly Jumu'ah. Belonging in the West would be the same as belonging anywhere else. When I was in [CITY 2], the masajid gave me a lot of belonging, because they had spaces for wudu, where you could wash your feet the way I do at home, and handheld bidets in the bathrooms. Belonging as a Muslim there made me feel like my wudu was accepted, my salah was accepted — on this side, that's more what I get from my house than my workplace or school.

Yes, a little — not that people need to be thinking about me twenty-four seven, but basically, yes. It's literally just wudu and salah, being comfortable knowing I'm able to pray in whatever space I'm in. In general, I'm one of those people who walks around with a prayer mat and will just take it out and start praying — everyone will look at me, but I don't really care. Me and my friend go to [CITY 3] a lot, and we both have those little things in our prayer mats, and we'll just pick a corner of the grass and pray. Nothing has happened to us so far, and inshallah nothing does. If you want to belong in an environment, you'll figure it out.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s13 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

This country — I think the older I get, the more I realize that perhaps we didn't belong in the first place. I was just really young, so it wasn't as stark for me, but the older I get, the more I realize that very systemic things in this country are anti — not only anti-Islam, but anti any structured religion.

Even before getting into the different sects — when I meet Orthodox Christians or Orthodox Jews, the non-extreme ones, I find their experiences relate to mine in unexpected ways. I'd met Christians before [UNIVERSITY 1], but at [UNIVERSITY 1] is the first time I met Orthodox Jews, and a lot of them had the same struggles I did. I met a girl who also went to a Jewish private school, and it was the funniest conversation — every single point was matching. She was shocked about how wild some of the students could get, how distant the professors were from students, how there wasn't really respect in professor-student relationships — for example, at the end of every lecture, some students, while the professor is still talking, will just get up, pack, zip up their bags, and leave, because it's the end of the time slot. I'm like, he can finish his sentence, thirty seconds isn't the end of the world — and she was relating to all of it. I remember thinking, I wish you didn't love Israel the way you do, because we could have been friends.

She was part of the student union, and I saw her walking around with those shirts on October 7th, but she was a nice person right up until I found that out, so it was conflicting information for me — why am I relating to you so much? But I did meet very normal, very nice Orthodox Jews too, same situation. I remember that one girl specifically because she hit every nail on the head of my experiences as well.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s14 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

A threat to what? Not personally, but I do know of Muslim girls in the MSA who were being stalked around campus after October 7th, and the police were called on them. There was a Jewish Students for Peace group, and another one, and they'd host events on the main mall at [UNIVERSITY 1] with police surrounding the perimeter, as if the Muslim students were going to attack them. I found that very funny, because the aggressors and stalkers of Muslim girls on campus were the ones being protected with police from Muslims — that dichotomy was very, very annoying. I told my mom about it, and that day she called me early and told me to come home, because it was getting dangerous on campus. That was my fault, because I told her something that had happened — things don't really escalate on that campus more often than not, there's no escalation, but she was freaking out, she literally told me to skip class and come home. That was the first time that ever happened. It was a bunch of conflicting situations, but it did not happen to me personally, feeling like I was a threat.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s15 | question_id: akhirah -->

When I was younger, in high school, it wasn't as prevalent — I was in Islamic studies at [ISLAMIC SCHOOL 1], hearing about the Akhirah, the Day of Judgment, hearing that everything happens for a reason, that if a du'a isn't answered you might get something better in the Akhirah, or even in this life. But it didn't really sink in for me until around 2022, when I started thinking that we have entire lives left to live. I started zooming out from my day-to-day experience and seeing it as part one of a journey: life in the grave is one part, the Day of Judgment is one part, and then the last part is when you're actually in wherever you end up — inshallah, for all of us, Jannah — but that's another life to live. When you zoom out from your experiences, everything becomes easier to deal with — disappointment, isolation, loneliness, all of it doesn't affect you the way it once would have. Even if you feel it temporarily, even very strongly for a while, it's still not as much as you'd feel it if you didn't think about how this is just part one of several.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s16 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Before, I used to want a lot of control over what's happening around me, what's happening to me, what kind of experiences I'm in — I wanted to know all the details, all the questions answered, the plan. I still am like that, but now I'm more certain that things are going to work out however the chips fall, and I have a more hands-off approach.

If I'm sure I've done everything I could to do things the correct way, even if I make a mistake, I still tried to do it correctly — that gives me peace and comfort, makes me less stressed, less anxious about the situation. When I look around at some of my classmates, I see they don't have that same peace, and they're having different experiences, acting very wild, and I don't want to live what I've seen someone live through. Even if my experience doesn't make me feel pretty when I look in the mirror — because as a Muslim woman, you dress well for the sake of Allah — and even if I'm having the worst day, no makeup, all of that, the alternative doesn't attract me. Yeah, the broader, non-Muslim community — that's what I mean.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s17 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

When I think about practicing in the long run, what I'm working on right now is making sure my secular experiences are of benefit, and to increase my Islamic knowledge and have that shape how I do things secularly — what company I work for, what job I do, what repercussions come from the work I do, and who I give my energy to.

I mean, in the wider world — if I work for a company that does bad in the world, and my job strengthens that badness, I don't think the repercussions just go into the air. If you work for a place that does bad things, you get a portion of that. I want to be able to do work where I'm certain the repercussions are positive.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-010_s19 | question_id: submission -->

It means just trying my best to fulfill my obligations, trying my best to have supplementary acts of worship outside the obligatory ones. I think it just means trying my best, even if the scope of what I'm doing is small. Liberation?

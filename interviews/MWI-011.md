# Interview MWI-011

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

An ordinary day in my life usually starts with school. Between my childhood and three years with family, I went for a GED, and then continued with career and academics thereafter. That's how I began my career.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

We are people dedicated to our families, kind-hearted, wanting the best for others as we wish for ourselves, and making a positive impact in the lives of ourselves, our families, and the community we care about.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've been in the United States since 1998, so that would be about 28 years this year. Comparing from the time I started in this country till now, there's been a lot of positive changes, and also a lot of negativity, and that actually spurs from 9/11, when the World Trade Center was attacked. From there, being a Muslim became more difficult, especially as a hijabi — walking around the streets became more difficult. Having the fear that I could be attacked always came to my mind, and especially going to school, completing my undergrad was really challenging, because working as a student, not having a car, having to move around — even on public transportation, at least about 75% of the time, I always faced negativity from people on the bus and around me. That really impacted how I felt in terms of my confidence level. As much as I was confident, the attack and Islamophobia really affected how I did things, how I tried to embark on things and make decisions. I preferred to be within a community, or within the house, where I felt more comfortable, rather than going outside — even working was difficult, because it was more dangerous, since people would call me names just because of my identity. But I did not want to back down, and I continued wearing my hijab — my dressing was part of me, so I was not going to back down on it. But I realized it affected the way I went about my daily life outside the house. I'd say that was after 9/11, thereabouts, maybe around the mid-2000s.

That gave me a little bit of confidence, because of the environment I found myself in and the people I worked with — they loved me for who I was, and also protected me. If there was any backlash from anybody outside when we went out, they were there for me, even though they were non-Muslims, because of the way I already carried myself around them — it felt comfortable, that as a Muslim, we're just like other people who want the best for ourselves and our families. That helped me regain a lot of confidence, starting with my children and all of that. I guess now the backlash is back, because of the political atmosphere, but I think overall the confidence I gained back then has helped me navigate the challenges of now.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes, I do seek religious knowledge actively. I turn to scholars, I turn to my husband — he's more knowledgeable in the faith than me — and I turn to the Book of Allah, the Quran, for guidance, and also the sayings of the Prophet, peace be upon him, for guidance on how to face whatever challenge I may be dealing with.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

My hijab story is so funny. I started wearing hijab back in my native country, Nigeria. I went to a Christian school — being in that Christian environment, we were forced, not even given an option, to practice Christianity. My dad was not enforcing hijab, my parents did not enforce us to wear it — we didn't even grow up with the knowledge of girls wearing hijab. We were only taught to be modest in dressing and to have the best of characters representing ourselves outside our homes.

I remember I got sick at a period of time, and that sickness really impacted the way I viewed life. That was around the time I was going to get admission into college — that year, me and my sister were supposed to start college, but I couldn't, because of my sickness. That sickness made me turn back to Allah, and I started reading the Quran. As I read, I started realizing all the falsehoods we'd been taught in high school. That strained the relationship I had with my siblings and my parents, because me and my sister, who went to the same school, had that mindset that Christianity was the answer, that it wasn't a sin. But now, going outside my own shell, outside the knowledge my parents had given me, I had to navigate the challenge of finding the truth. It was either I continued with Christianity, what we'd been forced to do, or I found a way to understand — if Christianity was the answer, I'd understand what I was doing, and if Islam was the answer, I'd understand that too. So I started reading the Quran, and the more I read, the more conviction I gained that this was the truth.

The next year I gained admission — I went through the application process again and got into a polytechnic in Nigeria, and that really changed my perspective, because when I stepped into that university, regardless of the way I dressed, the people who came to my aid — this was a place I didn't know anybody, didn't know where I was going to sleep, had no idea how I was going to live my life there — the people who came to my aid were Muslims, and they weren't even fully practicing hijab or anything. I met a friend I still have a relationship with now, as a sister — she took me by the hand when she saw me crying, and we went through the admission process together, how to register and all of that. I realized Islam was just the truth — Islam, the way of life, was what gave me that peace of mind, that regardless of what I did, I could go back to Allah and ask for forgiveness and not be criticized, not be put down. After my first semester, I saw a lot of sisters wearing hijab, and they started attending Muslim Students' Association meetings, and I just fell in love with the way Islam was presented — it wasn't hard, it wasn't harsh, it was subtle, in a way that made me feel, okay, even without wearing hijab, I could be a Muslim who could become better and understand the reason why Allah asked women to wear hijab. Once I understood the ayah Allah revealed about covering, in Surat al-Ahzab, I realized this was a conviction I would obey my Lord with — I would be better, rather than trying to please the people around me who wanted me to be part of them. So the struggle of trying to balance it out — I decided no, I was going to stand firm on my deen, my religion, and get better at it, because I understood it more. That's also what led me to make sure that when I have kids in the future, I'd be able to help them understand the reason behind hijab, rather than just putting it on them.

The hijab protects me, it gives me confidence, it gives me the reassurance that obeying Allah is the ultimate, and when I obey my Creator, everything else becomes easy, because Allah said with hardship comes ease as well. So regardless of what I may be facing, I know that hijab is just an outer covering — it comes from my inner self, from having the conviction that Allah is the one who told me to cover, and in obeying Him, He has told me in the Quran that whoever obeys His commands will surely get a reward, which is happiness. So anything that happens to me, I know it's only by the design of Allah, not any human being. My hijab has given me the confidence to navigate challenges in life and know that this is just a passing trial, and the ultimate goal is working toward the life we're promised in the Akhirah.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I think people categorize us as — maybe privileged, subdued, that we cannot make it in life, that we should not make it in life because we are women. The misunderstanding the media has portrayed to people of the Western world has been really negative, and that's the reason a lot of people in America, or the Western world, who have no real knowledge of hijabis, of the role of women in Islam, misunderstand us as women wearing hijab as being subdued — that we cannot have a career, cannot make it in life, that we're subject to harshness and can't make it past a certain level, even in education. For example, that education isn't something we should have.

I remember a time I was teaching, and a parent came to me — her child was in my classroom — and I believe she looked at me because of the hijab and thought, how can this person even be a teacher, she's not worthy of being a teacher. She tried to challenge me one day, and as she did, she wanted to know my educational background. Once I told her, she just apologized to me, because she'd had it at the back of her mind, from the way the media has portrayed Muslim women, that we're not able to have a good education, not given that opportunity, and she'd had that negativity pushed onto her. That really changed her perspective toward me from that day onward — the respect she gave me afterward was much more than the way she treated me at the beginning of our relationship as her child's teacher. That person was non-Muslim.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s06 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I remember, in 2015, after having three of my children, I faced the reality of either sending my children to public school or being in an Islamic environment. My house was far from the school, and I enjoyed my career as a teacher, I loved teaching, but I had to face the reality that it was becoming too stressful because of the distance, and we didn't want to live outside our means — whatever we had as income, me and my husband, we were going to live within it, we weren't going to get a house or a place of living that was more than our income could sustain. So we had to face the reality of either homeschooling our children to still give them the best education, or sending them to public school, because the alternative was just too stressful. That was the challenge we faced, and I remember trying to think, why do we have to go through this? But one thing was certain to us: our children were not going to go to public school, no matter what. We were not going to face the same challenges we'd faced with hijab, and the stories we'd heard from Muslim colleagues about children facing a lot of bullying in public schools, especially children who wear hijab — that could affect their confidence, and we didn't want our children to face that. We had girls, so it was a tough decision, but looking back, we're thankful to Allah that He guided us to make it — it's not a decision we've regretted one bit. Navigating it was the first challenge: how were we going to give our kids what we wanted them to have, and also let them have a social environment for their own well-being. We were able to balance that with activities for the kids, and alhamdulillah, they're young ladies now, thriving, in college, and I believe they've navigated the challenges they've faced as well.

First, we had to do some research — me and my husband had to sit down and decide, okay, it's only going to be one income, sustaining a family of five. So we sat down and wrote out all our expenses — what could we cut off, like cable TV, things that were more luxury than necessity. Cutting those off didn't impact our life in any way. My husband had to double down on his work, and I had to be the one staying home with the kids. We had to draw up a plan for how we were going to teach the kids the curriculum, to make sure they'd be able to keep up with other kids outside the home, and when it came to any state testing, they'd be able to pass, to be at the same level as kids outside our house. We consulted with people who were homeschooling, and got support from a few friends and our family too — when you don't stand alone, when you learn from others, you realize how much easier things can be. Having the right support, our family was there for us for the times I needed breaks, and that's how we navigated the situation.

When we made that decision, I realized, okay, I trained to do that work as a teacher, teaching other kids, and I also wanted to be in an Islamic environment — I didn't want to teach in a public school. I had gone through public school for my teacher training, and I'd made that decision within my heart that teaching in a public school wasn't something I'd want to do, except as a last resort. When we'd weighed all our options, I realized I could continue teaching, just teaching my own daughters instead. Especially for my five-year-old, who wasn't in school yet, I decided to take that on myself — I could take the same grade level I was teaching in school and put that energy into my daughter, and see if I could have a positive impact on her, if she could accomplish what the kids in my classroom could accomplish. I looked at that as a success for me. I realized the flexibility of homeschooling let me do a lot of things I was able to do more of outside the classroom, but also use my teacher mindset to teach her. When we decided to put her in an online Islamic school, and she went through testing, she was two grades above level, and that's how I looked at it as my success — that was what made me happy. I gave up something, but I saw something else in my own daughter that I probably wouldn't have seen had I not made that decision to stay home with my kids.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s07 | question_id: expectations -->

Definitely. The first thing, as a Muslim woman, you must be educated — you must be well-rounded to be able to have an impact. When we hear the stories of Aisha, radiAllahu anha, and Khadija, radiAllahu anha, and all the other wives, all the Muslim women back then, that's our own pathway too — we should also have a positive impact, and one of the ways we can do that is to be educated. You need to know what society is learning and be able to differentiate it, so you're not just in your own bubble, thinking this is the only way to approach things. Understanding what others who are non-Muslim think, understanding their thinking, and how you can make a positive impact, is what we build our community upon. A Muslim woman, even if she's going to be a stay-at-home mom, must be well-rounded, educated in the Western world and also in Islamic studies.

I think it goes back to our own parents. For me, at least within our Nigerian community, I'd say almost everybody had the same orientation from our parents, giving us that educational background — it wasn't a choice, you had to go to school, you had to learn — so that when people confront you with challenges, when people come to challenge your deen, you're able to understand where they're coming from and answer whatever questions they present. If you're living in ignorance, the way people will treat you, the way you'll be impacted by their thinking, you'll realize you'll be leaving your own being and your own beliefs to believe in what other people believe, because you don't have that deep understanding of what you've been leaving.

Yes, we had Islamic education at home, but it was on a basic level — not the way I'm teaching my own kids now, it's different. Yes, my school in Nigeria was a Christian school, but at home, and I lived in a boarding house, so it wasn't as if I was seeing my parents every day — we'd spend the whole semester at school, going to church and all of that, and then come home and practice our deen again.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s08 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

I feel most responsible, first of all, to my family, my nuclear family, and to myself. And to my Creator — I'm responsible to follow and obey the commands of my Creator, Allah, and responsible for myself, since the actions I take will either impact me positively or negatively. Once the decisions I make for myself impact my own family, if I've made positive decisions, I know that positive attitude carries through to my children, my spouse. But if I make wrong choices, there's a tendency I'll bring down my own family too. As the adage goes, show me your face and I'll tell you who you are — the people around you say something about the type of person you are. So if I try to be a good person, if I try to follow the commands of Allah, the decisions I make, for the most part, mean I want people of that same mindset around me, my circle, to help me when I'm going through difficulties. So definitely, my responsibility is to love my family, my extended family, and my community as well.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s09 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I think going back to the Quran, it lays down the basics of the role of a woman, the impact she has. Even for those who don't have a spouse — they always say, behind every successful man is a woman. I even look at, for the non-Muslim example, someone like Bill Clinton, the former president — when we say he was a successful man, even through his struggles, his wife was beside him, and that's how non-Muslims think of it too. For a Muslim woman, it's even more — you have that impact, you're the one training the next generation. When you train a child well, you train the nation well, because that child goes out to train others, and the cycle continues. But if the mother is ignorant of her role, of the importance of having a positive impact on her children, you'll realize you'll lose them, because society will take your children away with their own stories, their own falsehood, and you'll see the children drift from believing in the oneness of Allah.

I believe it's something wider society shares too, not just an individual belief. A lot of people, even non-Muslims, don't send their children to public school, because they believe the way they want to raise their children is different from the way the public system trains them, and a lot of that comes from the larger curriculum being taught — as an individual, you don't have the role to make changes in that system, so you either go along with it or find a way to create your own system, one aligned with your belief system. So I believe it's more of a general thing than just an individual thing.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s10 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

My faith and the country I live in — it's challenging, and it also gives me the assurance that the more people understand me as a Muslim woman, the more they go back and learn about Islam, and accept it, because they can reason, they can understand — it's not about logic, it's the truth, but you have to understand it, and understanding it means you can't be in ignorance, can't just follow someone saying something on social media. You have to sit down and educate yourself, and when you educate yourself, you empower yourself, and when you empower yourself, you can stand on top of the mountain knowing your empowerment and your knowledge are based on understanding your faith, and you're able to navigate anything based on that understanding. So what I've found is that standing my ground, not backing down on hijab, has helped me a lot in this country — it's given me the confidence to navigate things, in a respectful and firm way, so that people understand me, and I can have an impact through how I present myself to others. When I represent myself well, people can say, this person is a Muslim, and this is different from what they've heard about Muslim women, and that can lead them to educate themselves and understand Islam.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s11 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

Oh yes — going back to 9/11, it was a huge challenge, because I was working as a student. When we came to this country, it was just me and my brother — my parents brought us and went back, so we were left to navigate ourselves here. We had family friends, but the main navigation was left to us — either based on what our parents had taught us, and we continue that legacy, or we let society drift us away from that path of being Muslim. When 9/11 happened, even the first few days after, I remember getting on the bus, sitting down just like any other time, and I never realized how that would affect me. I remember a white woman screaming right in my face, calling me all the words they called us back then — you're Osama bin Laden, you want to kill us in this country, you need to go back to your country. It wasn't easy — I'd go home and cry, not want to go to work again. We didn't have a personal car, my brother didn't either, so we had to take public transportation to school and work and back home — it wasn't just getting on the bus once, it was an everyday thing, so that threat grew more and more. My boss offered, at that time, that one of my colleagues could pick me up and drop me off close to my house, but I realized, how long was that going to last? People were ignorant, people were angry about what happened, and they put that anger on Muslim women, because for men it was easier not to be identified as Muslim, but for a Muslim woman wearing hijab, they'd consider you a terrorist, Osama bin Laden, here to hurt them. I never made comments to anybody — I understood why they were angry, but I didn't understand why they had to direct that anger at me, an individual just trying to go about my daily life like them. That was the threat I faced then, and alhamdulillah, it strengthened me, it strengthened my iman — I knew this was a test from Allah, and the only way to pass it was to stand my ground on being a Muslim woman, and nothing less.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s12 | question_id: akhirah -->

About Jannah — that's one thing we do, sharing reminders as a family after morning prayers. Allah's promise to those who do good, who pray their salah, pay their zakat, do good to others, be kind — simple things — is that if you do these things, inshallah, by His mercy, you'll gain the pleasure of Allah and get into Jannah. That's written clearly in the Quran, that He has promised this. He created the heavens and the earth, He makes the night turn to day and the day turn to night — the signs around us give us that understanding that the One who created all of this is the only one who can give us the hereafter, His paradise. Following those simple things He's said in the Quran isn't complicated — just go about your daily life the way that pleases Allah, and continue to seek His mercy. Those are the simple, uncomplicated things we talk about in my family — smile at others, be kind, when you see someone holding something and want to go through a door, hold it for them, that's an act of charity. Those little things count, and you don't know which one thing you do that pleases Him. We also remind ourselves of the story of the people of the cave, trapped in that cave, each pleading to Allah that this one thing I've done, use it to open this gate for us — and Allah did. We use all these examples as our own reminders, that when we do good, we can use that to hope for the best outcome.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s13 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Not everything is something we would understand. I remember, I lost one of my sisters in 2014 to cancer — she was a medical doctor, and I kept thinking, she was a medical doctor and yet she couldn't cure herself. No matter all the things she'd read, all the things she did, none of it was going to cure her from that cancer. That journey lasted about six years, and she passed at 24. Throughout that journey, it gave me the insight that regardless of the trial, you just put your trust in Allah. It's tough, it's difficult — I couldn't make sense of it, that a doctor couldn't cure herself — but looking back, Allah has said He will test us with life and death, and that really opened my eyes, my horizon, that regardless of who you are, you'll be tested, and regardless of whether you can make sense of it or not, you just put your trust in Allah. With that sadness came the fact that my mom had passed away just four years before my sister — that grief was still there, but carrying on with the hope that Allah has my back. Even if I have to sit down and cry for a moment, it's okay, because I know He has my back through this ordeal. I don't need to, like in my country, where people will say, oh, maybe someone did this to this person, maybe someone cursed them — no, there's no reason to navigate that when you have Allah. That's what allows for the sakeenah to get you through the test.

Alhamdulillah, the grief is there all the time, but we always look back and say, alhamdulillah, she died as a believer, peace be with her.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s14 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

About the long run, I think it's just having that confidence and understanding that you're only answerable to Allah, not to humans. I always think, when it's time for Salah, and I'm scared that maybe if I try to pray something would happen to me, someone would attack me — which does happen — but I know that if I'm doing what pleases Allah, and He takes my soul at that point, I'll be happy, I'll have that sakeenah. When we're at the mall with my kids and it's time for salah, we try so hard to work our schedule around salah, but sometimes it does happen that we're still out shopping, and that's how it goes. I've taught my children, and led them by example — even at a funeral, we always have a prayer mat in our bag, and we go and pray, then continue whatever we're doing. Salah is our priority — if anything happens to us because we delayed too much, we'd find ourselves asking, why didn't I pray? Why didn't I pray on time? So to the best of my ability, me and my husband, we've tried to instill that in our children. Even when we're traveling and it's time for salah, we can combine our prayers — we find a place to stop, combine our salah, and continue the journey. Once salah time comes, you start to feel uneasy, you won't be at peace with yourself until you pray, because it's already part of you, and something that's part of you is hard to tear away, except if you put yourself in a community where Islam isn't practiced. So we navigate it so that our salah comes first — if we want to go out, we ask what time salah is coming, maybe we delay going out until after salah, or if it's a place where we know we won't be able to pray at all — like the airport — things have become much easier there too, you can find a place to pray now. Society has changed over time, and we're seeing that a lot of people see Muslims as just as good as them, or even better, and that's what we believe.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s15 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

This is in obedience to Allah — that's what Allah wants me to do. My wudu is my key to my salah, and my salah is my key to my hereafter. When you're praying, you're in total submission to your Creator, you're obeying Allah at that time, because that's what He ordained us to do — as it's said in scripture, He created us for nothing but to worship Him. So when that time of salah comes, what I'm thinking about is that this is related to Allah, and nothing else comes before Him. After that, Allah makes everything else easy — even the difficult things become easy, by His grace. Sometimes when I'm finding something very difficult — like I said, my day begins with tahajjud — I put it in front of Allah: make things easy for me, make this decision easy for me, the decision that's best for me. That's total submission to Allah, alhamdulillah.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s16 | question_id: submission -->

It means everything to me, actually. It means my conformity to my religion — my dressing, my eating, my mingling, my whole life, because Islam is a way of life. Everything I'm doing — the Prophet, peace be upon him, said that your daily activities, if you have the right intention, can be an act of worship. If I'm going to find a means for my family to eat, a source of income, or cooking for my children, for my family to be healthy, to not go hungry — that's an act of worship, because it's what Allah wants us to do. Everything connects — when I give my children food, Allah has given us the sustenance and the ability to cook it, and it's all an act of worship. When you have that mindset, subhanAllah, even when you're feeling tired, that energy comes to you, because you know every day won't be a perfect day. Even the best of man, the Prophet ﷺ, said, I'm nothing but a man like you, I am not perfect, there's no perfection. So I don't work toward perfection, I work toward doing my best, and when I do my best, I know Allah takes care of the rest.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-011_s17 | question_id: liberation -->

Liberation is knowing you're not answerable to anyone, you're not begging anyone for your sustenance. Maybe someone comes to you and says, if you don't practice your deen, I'll give you this much money, I'll give you this, I'll give you that — but you feel a great surge knowing you're not begging anyone for sustenance, that whatever little you have, putting your trust back in Allah, gives you that liberation. Especially with money — people feel that's the number one thing they're answerable to, that if I don't have it, I can't do this, I can't go. But if Allah's given me this, I can be happier than someone who has much more and has a lot of worries. Because the barakah is there — Allah puts the barakah in it — I can have one dollar and feel on top of the world, because I know my life is in His care, that He has my back in everything I'm doing. That's when I feel most liberated, every day, because when it comes to my daily activities, my husband trying to work according to company policy — but anything that's against our deen, we find a way to let his boss know, no, we don't practice this, I don't go for happy hour. I don't have to bend to your culture to have my Islam taken from me — I would not bend to that. I do the work, I can do what I need to do to take care of my family, but anything that crosses the line with my Islam, I go with my Islam, and I know Allah will take care of me, because I'm doing what He wants me to do.

# Interview MWI-012

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

An ordinary day in my life: I'm a homemaker and a homeschooler mainly, and a mother of three. In my spare time I teach Halaqa, the Islamic study circle, and I teach Quran one-on-one. A regular day would be, after I wake up in the morning I do my morning worship and have breakfast. On school days I would drop off my oldest child, who is a college student, at the train station, then come back and either exercise or have breakfast, then wake up my third child, who is in middle school, so she can start her day — that would be homeschooling, or maybe we would go to a homeschooling co-op. If we come back before lunch, I give lunch too. I also have my father-in-law at home, so I give him lunch, or if we are gone the whole day I set his lunch out. By afternoon I do dinner prep, and dinner would be around before or after Maghrib. Most of my Quran one-on-one teaching is in the afternoon on some days. I guess that would be a typical day.

How else does faith show up in a given day? Well, first of all, there is all the compulsory ritual worship — the five prayers. In the morning I do extra; I'm trying to memorize Quran, so a lot of time is for that, and some other worship habits I'm trying to build — sometimes successfully, sometimes not. I'm still training myself to have those habits. A lot of it is because I teach Quran and have to prep for my halaqas, and then there's teaching my kids, especially my youngest. Faith is basically the center — I can't really answer that separately, because your life is faith, and everything else is because of your faith. My service for my family and my in-laws is all based on faith. I don't really see a dichotomy where faith shows up sometimes and doesn't show up other times.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would say that I'm somebody who believes in God and tries to center her (life on that). I guess somebody who's never met a Muslim woman would assume I'm not Muslim, so I would center tawhid first — that we believe in absolute monotheism, that our faith centers around God, and a Muslim woman would be somebody who — and that's why I wear the hijab, because it is an act of obeying God.

The Muslim community would be a community of people with diverse backgrounds. It would be a community that believes in one God and believes in the existence of the prophets and the holy books. I guess I would center it around the pillars of faith and what we believe, but then I would add that we're a diverse community with many backgrounds and cultures, and also different levels of understanding and different levels of practice.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

The community I'm part of right now — I would see myself as part of several communities. I'm in [CITY 1, STATE 1]; there's a mosque five minutes from me, so you could say I'm part of that community. I'm also originally [COUNTRY], so I'm part of the [COUNTRY] community in [CITY 2], which is further away, and I teach in a different masjid, so I see myself as part of that community too, although maybe a little loosely since I just teach there and I'm not there day to day or at their events. And I feel very much part of the [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION] community because I attend the events regularly and I'm actively volunteering, so I consider myself part of all these communities.

How has it changed over time? My immediate community in [CITY 1] — when my kids were small and I first moved into this place, I was much more involved and much more regular; for example, I would attend every Jumu'ah, and I even started volunteering in the school based in that masjid and became a teacher there for about two years. When I stopped teaching, I still went there regularly, but as time went on I felt less connected to that community because there's a predominant culture there, and as a person who's not part of that culture, you feel it — you feel that you're not really seen. When the masjid leadership changed to a younger, more American-educated person who was more open to input from all communities and held events inviting community input, I got excited and active again. Then that new leadership was overturned by the older leadership, back to something that isn't exactly exclusive — nobody would say we're not open to other cultures, everything is technically open — but the atmosphere and feel of it is a kind of hidden, subtle exclusivity, I guess I can say that.

I've been in this community as long as I've been married, since 2005, so twenty-one years. I'm an idealist, so I always say the masjid is Allah's house, and the ones running it are people, and people aren't going to be perfect. So I was very doggedly just attending, attending, attending. Some of my friends, especially those more born and bred here, even though they're part of that culture, moved away because they felt it was too cultural, but I'm the one who's not from that culture and I kept saying no, this is Allah's house, you focus on Allah's house, and Allah's house is maintained by believers. For almost two decades I tried not to let the atmosphere affect me, but I think I kind of gave up a little, especially when someone made a harsh comment about my daughter, who was in her early teens, about her wearing pants. That was a small thing, but she became masjid-averse, and that affected me. Then COVID came, and we were inside and didn't go for Jumu'ah at all, and when we could go back to the masjid, I had by then found [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION], and that spiritual need was fulfilled by [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION] — Alhamdulillah, it really fulfilled that spiritual need. My eagerness, my idealist drive to always go to the masjid, kind of waned. After COVID, I stopped going to my closest, local community masjid as much for Jumu'ah — I come now and then, depending on the events, but not as regularly as before. For taraweeh, I still come regularly out of love for it, but outside Ramadan I'm not as consistent as before. So that has changed.

As for my sense of myself as a Muslim woman — Alhamdulillah, Alhamdulillah. Yes, it's definitely changed since I started wearing the hijab in my mid-20s, and I can say that was my gate to Islam. Before that I practiced Islam more ritualistically and very inconsistently — if I felt like praying, I'd pray; if it was convenient, I'd pray; if not, I didn't. I led a lifestyle that didn't refer to Islam at all; I just followed what felt good, what I thought was good, and how [ETHNICITY] culture and society were, so that's what I followed. Hijab became my gateway into Islam, because hijab was the hardest thing for me to apply. I went back and forth for nine years after I found out it was compulsory, and once Allah allowed me and made it easy for me, everything else after that became easy. I would always check whether something was halal or not; Islam became my guidance. I started learning, taking classes, and all my remorse over my previous lifestyle and my gratitude that Allah allowed me to change propelled me forward. The learning is a lifelong thing. So the evolution has been more learning and being more careful about the deen. Knowledge-wise, Alhamdulillah, I've improved a lot — I'm almost fifty now and I wore hijab at twenty-six. But I have to point out that sincerity-wise it's a struggle, always a struggle. In the early days I would read Yasin and tears would just come; I remember long tahajjud with tears, and that's kind of my benchmark, because I remember how passionate and remorseful you are when you first come to it, like a new convert. So I try to remind myself that knowledge-wise I'm exponentially further along, but sincerity-wise it's always up and down, and I have to fight within myself, especially now that I'm in spaces where I'm teaching — I have to remind myself about sincerity, about not taking it for granted, and about not letting it affect me when people are very respectful toward me. I can't say how the evolution is sincerity-wise; I just ask Allah to accept it and help me, but I get scared because I remember how sincere I was in those early days. I try to stay aware of that.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes, actually. When I first wore hijab I was in [STATE 2]; there was a masjid but no imam, so it was mostly from the internet. I remember reading the Quran, reading the ayah and right away reading the translation, and over the course of two years or so I became very familiar with Arabic and started noticing the root patterns — this means the same thing but the word is different, and then you see the letters are the same. It was very much self-taught. The masjid had a small library; people left cassettes there and I would listen to all of them. I printed out Q&As from the internet and that's how I learned. There was also a halaqa taught by an auntie, going through a tafsir book, though it wasn't as structured — that was also part of it.

When I moved to [CITY 2], things opened up for me — I found out there's a word-for-word translation, which was what I'd been doing all along by hand. I went to more classes, [ISLAMIC EDUCATION INSTITUTE 1], and online institutes like [ONLINE ISLAMIC INSTITUTE 1]. I should also mention there's an organization for [ETHNICITY] Muslims in America with regular halaqas, and a phone halaqa program I started around 2004 that I'm still part of — they were very instrumental in my early days. I would go to [ISLAMIC EDUCATION INSTITUTE 1] seminars when they came to [CITY 2], and I stumbled on a Quran institute while trying to memorize Quran after my parents passed away, because I wanted to do something for them. That memorization program didn't stick at first because my kids were smaller and I couldn't keep up, but I later found a Quran institute that taught tajweed and qira'at, and Alhamdulillah I got an ijazah from them. Then I found [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION]. From the qira'at program I realized that if you really want credibility, you have to go through a structured system — halaqas are good for spiritual maintenance, but a structured program gives you a foundation, a chain of teaching you can point to, even though there are different opinions, say, on pronunciation. I looked for an Alimah program, but I'm Shafi'i and most online programs were Hanafi. I tried one program that wasn't very organized, then I Googled a Shafi'i Alimah program online and found [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION], which is very organized, Mashallah, so I stuck with it. In my [ETHNICITY] community I'd somehow gotten invitations to teach, even though my knowledge was just from halaqas — the [ETHNICITY] community in [CITY 2] is small, and there aren't alimahs the way there are in the Desi community, so I felt a responsibility to be in a more serious, structured program, which is why I sought out the Alimah program. I didn't leave other institutes, but [RELIGIOUS INSTITUTION] became my main place of learning.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

Hijab is tied to my conversion story. I have to go back to my family — my parents. My father was gentle and soft, and we would organically pray together, but he never presented it as Islamic rules; we just prayed together. He was a diplomat, so we moved to different countries, and in every small [ETHNICITY] community we found ourselves in, he basically became the imam, even though he wasn't traditionally educated, just well-read and devout. It was never presented as rules. I remember being maybe seven or eight, him taking me to an outdoor restaurant at night, his face against the night sky full of stars, talking about Allah — I don't remember what he said, but I remember the feeling of awe. That was Islam for me.

Then I went to college in [COUNTRY], and the campus had a very strong Islamic movement and Da'wah movement, where the tangible benchmark of a successful da'wah was getting someone who didn't wear hijab to wear it. I was a rebel, and that approach didn't work on me — I reacted the opposite way, thinking, you think I'm a bad Muslim because I don't wear hijab? Fine, I'll color my hair purple. I still wanted to be spiritual, though, so I looked for a halaqa outside my campus, and I found one that turned out to be a misguided sect, almost a cult, actually banned by the government — they wanted a bay'ah (pledge) before moving to a new topic, and I refused, being the rebel that I am. A classmate I trusted helped me realize what it was, and I asked her to teach me instead, treating me as someone starting completely from zero. When we got to the topic of hijab I got stuck, because I realized that practicing Islam truly meant changing my lifestyle — and at the time my lifestyle was very un-Islamic; even my boyfriend used to drive me to these lessons. I wasn't ready, and I put religion in the back of my mind after I graduated and moved to Austria.

Then I moved to the US, and September 11 happened, and people started talking very badly about Islam — I was shocked, because I thought Americans were open-minded and advanced, and yet they couldn't see that religion is sacred. Around that time, a white convert who had taken off her hijab was staying with my roommate and me while she tried to get back on her feet and find work in that post-9/11 atmosphere. In my heart I protested — she was already there, already a Muslim, already someone who could show people Islam wasn't backward, and she was giving it up. That made me turn inward and ask, what about me? I'm educated too, and I do believe Islam is true. I started rethinking hijab and Islam. One day I decided to wear it, and then I had a kind of breakdown, because it had been nine years since I first learned hijab was compulsory back on my campus at seventeen or eighteen, and now I was twenty-six in [STATE 2]. I always thought that once I wanted to do it, I'd be able to. So when I wore it and then physically couldn't leave my apartment, I thought there must be something fundamentally wrong with me. I called my dad, who said it seemed like this had been on my mind a long time, and asked how my salah was; I said it was fine, I prayed. He told me to improve my salah, not to get angry, and if I found my mind going blank with nothing to think about, to do dhikr instead. I later realized that was like tazkiyah — my mind had accepted hijab, but my heart needed to be purified.

A few months later I went to the masjid, where I would always wear a loose hijab just for the visit. Driving back one day I thought, maybe I'll start wearing it properly in Ramadan — then I thought, how do I know I'll live through Ramadan? So as I was driving I thought, I'm wearing a hijab now, I just won't take it off again. And it was so easy, Alhamdulillah — that's Allah. From then on everything was easy; I stopped playing music in the car, and there was a thirst to learn everything I could. That's why I collected all the cassettes from the masjid. I became remorseful about my past lifestyle and became active in the community, volunteering in the Sunday school of that masjid in [STATE 2], and I realized I loved teaching. I changed my focus toward studying Islam and becoming a teacher. So hijab was the gate. It's very important to me because it took nine years, and I could have died in that time — I put myself in so many reckless situations, like wanting to try drag racing, going out with guys I didn't really know. Now that I have kids, I think, I was such a fool.

That nine years, from realizing hijab was mandatory to actually wearing it, Allah just got me to that point — Alhamdulillah. Hijab is very special to me because it's my gate to Islam, and I try to be mindful with women who don't wear it, because I remember what it felt like to be pushed and judged before I was ready. It can come at any point in someone's journey toward Allah, but for me it was really my gate, the hardest thing to submit to, so being able to do it is very special. Thank you for this interview, honestly, for reminding me how special it is.

Yes, definitely. I've had people ask me, did your dad tell you to wear it? And I just laugh, because my dad never told me to. It's a common misunderstanding — the internet gives people the perception that you're oppressed. But I feel like now it's much better.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

I haven't had that kind of direct question in a while — that was mostly early on, in my twenties. Especially here in [CITY 2], because it's so diverse, people are used to Muslims, but the encounters are more subtle now — like being in a grocery store and noticing a woman looking at me, clearly scared. I just smile and try to be friendly, though I do feel a pressure, when I'm out, to be more agreeable no matter how I'm actually feeling — though you don't want to fake it either. As a hijabi you're conscious of things — I'll always put my cart back where it belongs, even if I've parked far away, because I'm aware someone might see me and think, look at that Muslim woman not following the rules. I remember once talking to a white, non-Muslim friend about an issue I'd had, and she said, if it were me I'd just complain. I went quiet, and later I realized I hadn't complained because I'm a hijabi and I don't want to seem disagreeable in public. That used to weigh on me — you feel like you have to act nicely, be agreeable, because you assume people don't understand you or might be prejudiced, which I don't like, because then you're defeating yourself by assuming the worst. But I think somewhere in my forties you just stop caring so much what people think in general, so I feel freer now. I still act the way I should, but it doesn't weigh on me the way it used to.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s07 | question_id: expectations -->

I didn't relate what I described to "the community" so much as to myself. At the local masjid I've weaned myself away from, their approach to women is really based on their culture. When I'm in that community, I do feel there's an expectation for women to be a certain way — for example, at iftar, you can feel the men get preference in how food is distributed; if there's a limited amount and a lot of people, the men tend to get the better portion. My mother-in-law, who's also from outside that culture, and other women who aren't from it, would look at each other, because we feel it, whereas the women from that culture just accept it since that's what they're used to. In [ETHNICITY] culture it's not like that — it's equal, or sometimes women even go first.

At another masjid with that same predominant culture, I was there with my homeschooling kids and it got a little noisy, and someone behind the curtain shushed us rudely — you don't find that at the [ETHNICITY] masjid, which doesn't even really have a partition, just a low, loose one. At an iftar there, the women were cleaning up and laughing about something, and women from another culture started taking pictures of them, asking why. My son, who hadn't been to the [ETHNICITY] masjid in a while, noticed on the drive home how different it was — that the women were noisy, but nobody shushed them. It made me realize the women are really the engine of a community, and that the women taking pictures were probably struck by how joyful and free the [ETHNICITY] women seemed. I don't want to say one culture is better, but I do notice the difference in expectation as I move between communities — in that other community, women are expected to conform and just take it, and maybe they're used to it, so it doesn't register to them as repressive, but from my outside vantage point it can feel a little repressive. At one meeting about Ramadan that included some girls and women, the man leading it spoke in a way I found condescending toward the girls, and the girls themselves just stayed quiet and seemed okay with it. That worried me, especially for a girl who is also American — I wondered, what happens when they meet men outside the community who talk to them respectfully and sweetly; will they be too easily swayed because they're used to being talked down to at home?

I don't want to throw my brothers and sisters under the bus by saying "it's the culture, not Islam" to outsiders, because that's still my community, even when I've been to a masjid where the women's section is small and dingy, or the carpet isn't nice. I remember once, when I was working at that masjid school, we planned a bake sale where the teachers would run the table, and the masjid board said they didn't want the sisters standing out there selling — not wanting to put them on display, which felt disrespectful from where I stood, though I tried to understand it might be a different notion of respect from their point of view. Islam does teach respect for women, but the practicality of it passes through the prism of culture and comes out in different colors. I try to look at it that way, to empathize rather than think badly of others, but after two decades here I do sometimes feel a resentment toward this predominant culture, because it's become so dominant in the Muslim American sphere, and there are a lot of masjids like that — I think it's partly because back home, women don't go to the masjid, so there was never a need for a bigger space. As an [ETHNICITY], a minority within the American Muslim experience, I sometimes feel resentment, especially when I read that "Islam is oppressive to women" — no, it isn't; it's just different cultural ways of doing things. Sorry, this became a bit of a rant.

As for external expectations — meaning from non-Muslims — I try not to think about it too much, but let me give an example. In my late twenties, in [STATE 2], I entered a graduate research competition and won second place, already wearing hijab. When I received the award, a woman told me, you should be really proud of yourself. I smiled, but later I wondered why she said that — it seemed harmless, but there was something in it. I realized it was probably because I'm hijabi, and she assumed my faith was oppressive, so my succeeding defied her expectations, like I was somehow out of the norm. So maybe that's the external expectation — that when you succeed, it surprises people because of how they view us.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

My parents passed away about seventeen years ago, on the same day, and that was one of the biggest difficulties I can recall. How I made sense of it goes back to our belief that there's a decreed time written for each of us — there's no way around it. That's how I accepted it, though it was still painful and sad. Looking back, I feel it was humbling, because I realized I had subconsciously been treating my parents as a kind of backup plan — I married someone outside my own culture, and when they died I felt a sense of loss like a safety net had been pulled out from under me. I wondered why I felt that so acutely, and realized I'd been relying on them in a way that meant my reliance on Allah wasn't as complete as I thought. I might have gone through life thinking I was a good Muslim without ever recognizing that gap. Their passing made me realize that and, in a way, made me stronger — it pushed me toward wanting to do hifz for them and toward deeper Quran study. So the answer, again, is that I went back to my faith, trusting that it was Allah's will, and over time came to see it as something that was ultimately better for my iman — and certainly better for them, since neither of them suffered a long, difficult old age; my dad was sixty-two and my mom was fifty-seven or fifty-eight.

There's a hadith that your parents are crowned on the Day of Judgment (through good deeds like memorizing Quran on their behalf), and that became a big motivation for me. In the worldly sense, I felt like I hadn't done much for them — I finished my master's, worked a little, got married, had kids, and didn't really work after that — so I thought maybe I could present this for them, inshallah.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s09 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

I feel responsible for my kids. There's also a duty to myself. If I said only my husband, I wouldn't be being truthful — I feel more like an equal there, not really "responsible for" him. I owe it to myself to try to do well in this world and to be safe in the Akhirah. So I try, as much as I can, to make choices that wouldn't jeopardize my Akhirah.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s10 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I'm responsible for my family. There's a kind of balance, though — when I was taking a halaqa from my [ETHNICITY] teacher, she said we have a little responsibility to everyone: you're responsible for your family, yes, but you also have some responsibility to your community, and you should contribute what you can in your capacity. My husband is from a different culture than mine, and in the beginning there was a bit of a clash, because in his culture the expectation is more that family comes first and everything else is extra. He's since become more flexible about it. I'm someone who feels that if you wait for the house to be perfectly clean before doing anything else, you'll never get anywhere, because the house never ends. So I follow my [ETHNICITY] teacher's approach — she's understanding, Mashallah; when a halaqa runs a little longer, the women will say, okay, let's end now, because they know the husbands might not be as patient. Back in [STATE 2], the [ETHNICITY] women in a room upstairs would take their time at events, but you'd see the husbands tap the steel stair railing with their keys to call their wives down, and whichever wife's husband it was would go running. We were more relaxed about it, I suppose. Sorry, I don't mean to make this about one culture versus another; it just comes up.

For instance, when I was younger and talking to a brother — someone other than my husband — from a certain culture, I asked a friend from that same culture to help me, maybe teach me a dish so I'd have something to talk to him about. She asked, why would you consider him? Your women are more free. I didn't understand what she meant at the time, being young, but with more experience I came to understand it. I've heard similar comments since, from friends about Southeast Asian men in general — that they're less rigid about domestic roles — though I also know plenty of men from that other culture who are loving, gentle, and exactly how I'd expect a good man to be. I know it's a stereotype, and it's probably unfair to the men who aren't like that, but that's the impression that's out there.

As for my role in the wider community: I feel I have some responsibility to it, so when I'm invited to speak or teach, I try to make it work, fitting it in around my family without waiting for everything else to be finished first, because if everyone only looked out for their own family first, the community itself would suffer. It's a together thing.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s11 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Between Islam and America — if America needs Islam, it definitely needs Islam. All the ills you see in society — the unhappiness, the pressure on women to look a certain way, where appearance determines how people treat you and how successful you are — hijab takes that pressure away. It's not about your looks, it's about your character and conduct. I feel Islam is an answer to a lot of the ills of our society, but the society doesn't want it — it's a Judeo-Christian society, after all — though I think the more open-minded non-Muslim Americans become, the more they learn and, inshallah, the more they might see the good Islam can bring. So the relationship is kind of love-hate: America needs Islam, but America doesn't want Islam, or parts of it think they already know what Islam is and don't want it. I guess it's up to us as Muslims to conduct ourselves in a way that people can see the beauty of Islam.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s15 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

What matters most is that we're free to practice, and respected for the way we practice — that matters to me. Alhamdulillah, I feel better about that now than I once did.

If I could change something for the future, for my kids and grandkids, it would be the prevalence of Western culture's push toward just going after what you feel like, what you desire in the moment — I don't want to call it godlessness exactly, but there's a strong sense of chasing desire without restraint. That doesn't really align with Islam, where you can express yourself, but within a framework of what pleases Allah. I wish there were less of that pressure on kids and youth, though I don't know how you change something so built into the society.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s17 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah means letting go, understanding that Allah is Al-Hakim, the most wise, in everything, including his timing and the things he allows to happen — that even when you don't understand something, or even feel an aversion to it, you surrender to Allah, trusting there's something good in it, or that this is simply the way of the world. It means accepting things that happen, including things that cause you pain or sadness, and letting go of the idea that you know what's best. That's what submission to Allah means to me.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-012_s18 | question_id: liberation -->

I feel most liberated when I practice, because then you're focused on one thing, instead of being affected by what people think or say. When you practice, it's not about what people think, it's about Allah, and that's very liberating — who cares? Homeschooling is a good example — it goes against the grain, but my first motivation was raising my kids with Islam in mind, and centering on Allah, trusting this is a gift and a trust from Him, makes it not matter what people think about homeschooling. Or with hijab, or even just getting ready to go somewhere — I used to spend so long choosing clothes and putting on makeup, wondering who I was trying to impress, and realizing modesty means not needing that. That became liberating too — not caring what people think, because you care what Allah thinks, becomes freeing. I'm not saying you stop caring about people entirely, but when the focus on Allah takes over, that's liberating.

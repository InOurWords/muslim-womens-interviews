# Interview MWI-013

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Right now I work from home, so an ordinary day would be getting up around Fajr, doing my morning adhkar, a simple morning routine, and then logging into work from home. Usually after work I have either masjid events I attend or social gatherings I like to attend, just to keep myself out of the house for some time.

I do try to maintain consistent reading of the Quran. It's not a set number of pages every day, but I try to incorporate it in some way, whether that's Surah Al-Waqi'ah after Salat al-Maghrib or Surah Al-Mulk after Salat al-Isha. There are also portions of the Quran already incorporated in the morning and evening adhkar.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would describe myself as someone who is considerate, open-minded in the sense of not discriminating based on how someone looks, and just willing to help. My community would say that I show up — I'm pretty loyal to my commitments; when I say I'll organize an event or volunteer or show up somewhere, I keep my word.

My community itself is open. We are very warm hosts and we love to feed people — food is our love language, so anyone new to our community will get to see that firsthand for sure.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've been born and raised in America, in [STATE 1], for about fifteen years. I'd say I was raised in a bit of a bubble — surrounded by Muslims in my social circle, and in school too, since I was fortunate enough to attend Islamic private school. As time went on and I switched to public high school and then university, where there are people from all walks of life, it made me realize I am different from my community, but there are a lot more similarities than differences between me and my peers. So although I have to follow my religion based on what I think is right, that doesn't really affect my social interactions with people who aren't similar to me.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s03 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

I do seek religious knowledge actively. I just finished a two-year program at my local masjid, and I'm currently learning tafsir through my local masjid's imam's wife. She's kind of the teacher for the entire sister side, so I turn to her for any questions or fatwas.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s04 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

Hijab has always been present in my life. I was in Islamic private school, so it was part of our uniform, so transitioning from wearing it exclusively for uniform to wearing it full time was, Alhamdulillah, not at all difficult, because I was already used to it — it was just natural to wear it to the grocery store, for example. What does it do for me? I feel there's a level of inherent respect most of the time when I'm out in public around a majority of non-Muslims. I think there's a sense of modesty and boundaries that my hijab easily portrays to other people, so they understand I'm not one to mess with, for lack of a better phrase. Alhamdulillah, it has given me a layer of protection that I didn't fully realize until I had the hindsight to see it. There have also been unfortunate moments — hostile glares, or a clear difference in customer service, for example — but alhamdulillah, nothing major, so overall it has acted as a protector for me.

I officially started wearing it full time everywhere in middle school. In late high school, when I joined public high school, I came to understand that I chose to stick to the version I believe is right not just because I was constantly surrounded by it, or because my mom wears it, but because of the knowledge I sought out and the reasoning behind the commandment, and because I'd seen firsthand the protection it provides me in everyday life. So based on my experiences, knowledge, and introspection, I came to an internal decision that this is right for me, even though I had already been wearing it before reaching that decision. My schooling was a combination — elementary, middle school, and the beginning of high school was Islamic school, and then I transferred to public high school partway through.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I think to the common person there's an obvious misunderstanding — that this is forced on me, that I'm oppressed, that if I didn't wear it my dad would hit me or something. That's pretty common, I think, because the word "freedom" when it comes to wearing anything tends to lean toward one definition rather than the other. In America, as the years have gone by, the freedom to let a woman wear whatever she wants usually tends to mean she can show however much of her body she wants, but when it comes to the other end — covering up however much she wants — that's not seen as part of the definition of freedom; anything more than usual is deemed as something that must have been forced on her, that it can't be her choice. So I think that's a lack of understanding from the general public.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s06 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

There were a couple of times, especially during COVID when I was in college and we were all wearing masks, when I was ordering coffee and there was this inherent bias that I wasn't able to speak English, so the barista would start showing me cup sizes with their hands — "this is small, are you okay with small, or do you want medium" — like a side-by-side demonstration. I'd just say, I can speak, and they'd be taken aback. That happened a couple of times. Honestly, I just took it as a humorous incident. I don't try to look too deeply into it, especially when there was no negative reaction afterward — I'd say maybe it was an honest mistake, but I try to give people the benefit of the doubt.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

When I was graduating college, I had a difficult time finding a job. The job market was pretty bad at that time, and honestly still is. I was seeing people around me get a lot of offers, but at companies I didn't feel comfortable being part of. I ended up getting a position on campus at the school where I studied, which worked out in my favor, but during graduation, when everyone was talking about where they were heading next, I didn't really have an answer, and I felt kind of singled out in those conversations. I had to sit back and realize this was my test — was I willing to overlook certain company policies just for the sake of money or a title right after graduation, or was I willing to wait and see what God had planned for me? That time was pretty difficult.

This was around the time of the Palestinian conflict, when everyone was starting to realize where these countries even are and what history has to say about these events, and it was becoming clear who held which stance. Some companies were so blatant that it would have felt like a betrayal to myself as a Muslim to become part of them — whether defense companies or companies actively providing financial support to causes I don't think should be receiving that support. I tried my best to avoid those blatant betrayals of myself — of whether I'd be able to earn income from that, eat food bought with that money, live somewhere bought with that money, and tell myself that was correct.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

Of course, yes, a lot. The first thing that comes to mind is obviously wearing hijab a certain way, talking in a certain way, choosing certain fields of work and study — just this common trajectory of going to school and then immediately getting married and immediately starting a family. At least in my community I see that as a prominent factor; if someone over a certain age is still in college, still pursuing her education, or working in a career that doesn't leave room for a family yet, it's seen as her missing out, or not following the script.

When it comes to internal difficulties tied to hijab: when visiting places where I know diversity isn't present, I would internally tell myself not to be loud, verbally or physically — to be very pleasant with whoever I was talking to, take care of my own things, and not rely on people. For example, I visited a state that doesn't have many Muslims or immigrants, and it was pretty clear in convenience stores and restaurants that service workers were already uncomfortable seeing a woman in hijab. So I put an expectation on myself — if they got my order wrong, for example, I wouldn't call them back to fix it the way I would at home in [STATE 1], where diversity is present. There I would just take it as it is, try not to be the cause of anyone's inconvenience, and stay on the quieter side — meaning not calling back or sending food back, not constantly asking where something is when I could just look for it myself. I wouldn't say this resolved anything, really — it just prevented friction, in the sense that I could at least tell myself I wasn't causing anyone inconvenience or making them go out of their way for me.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s09 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

My parents. A lot of the time, when it comes to structuring and scheduling my day, or looking at the events I'm going to or the people I'm spending time with, there's this internal dialogue of, will they be okay with that? They know all my friends, and I feel this inherent responsibility to make sure they're never displeased with me, so they always know where I'm going and who I'm with. It's simply because there's a strong emphasis in our religion on this — displeasing your parents, in some sense, is displeasing God — and I want to make sure that's never the case for me. If that means constantly telling them where I'm going or who I'm with, that's totally fine by me.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s10 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I'm the eldest, so I have this inherent responsibility to make sure everyone's needs are taken care of — if my mom needs to go somewhere, I'm going with her; if my dad needs an appointment made or something done, I try to make sure that's taken care of. Beyond that, just making sure errands run smoothly and no one has any major issues that need attention.

Within my community — we've had a smaller batch graduate from the Islamic course I mentioned earlier, and we've been kind of involuntarily, but happily, recruited by our masjid to serve as the face of the next generation — leading youth events, teaching semester-long fiqh classes, and so on, since we've been given certification to do so. We're kind of the go-to people in our generation for that. So I feel a sense of responsibility to make sure the kids in elementary and middle school at least know someone closer to their age they can reach out to with religious questions.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s11 | question_id: belonging -->

Belonging is a pretty loaded question, but I'd say it means I personally don't feel like I need to keep moving. When I was visiting that other state I mentioned, I noticed I was internally always on the go, but in [STATE 1] I feel like I don't have to go anywhere — I can stay, mingle with people, and I don't have too many people questioning me. I'm just part of the background, essentially. That comes from my everyday experiences and interactions with all sorts of people, Muslim and non-Muslim, here.

In that other state, the first obvious thing was the staring — that struck me immediately — followed by curtness in replies, or just an unwillingness to help out. In a restaurant in [STATE 1], a server usually keeps an eye on their section and is willing to check on you, but in that state it wasn't the case; I'd even say we were being ignored and pushed to the side, with no one really willing to reach out and make sure we were okay or needed anything.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s12 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

It has its pros and cons. The religious freedom here in the West lets us follow the religion in a way that makes us comfortable, whether or not that's "right" is a different conversation — in other places, people trying to enforce religion do it in a way that doesn't conform to everyone's lifestyle. The trade-off is that it's sometimes a topic of conversation in certain gatherings — like at the library, someone asking why I actually wear the hijab, or whether I can really not eat pork, some of the more common questions.

Honestly, it makes me feel happy most of the time, because I give people the benefit of the doubt that it's coming from genuine curiosity rather than a way to dissect or validate their views on Islam. I like seeing the recognition on people's faces when they hear about the commonalities in our religions — that Mary also covered her hair, for instance, or that pork isn't allowed in some other religions either, and they see that common ground. I really enjoy it; it doesn't make me uncomfortable, though I think it takes reading your gut feeling and the intention of the person asking. If someone had malice in their heart, I imagine it would be best not to engage.

I'd lean toward no.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s14 | question_id: akhirah -->

This reminds me of a quote — the summer heat is hot, but Jahannam is hotter — so we really have to stick to what we know is right, straighten our intentions, and hope that Allah accepts us. I've seen people struggle with the idea of living comfortably in the West, but especially after seeing the Palestinians suffer, I've come to this realization that Islam is Islam — we know what's right and wrong, and we can't try to conform it to fit other people's insecurities, because we'll have to answer for every single action in the next life. Just imagining having to answer for what I'm doing right now scares me, so I try to follow whatever I can.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s15 | question_id: qadr_control -->

A few years ago, I would have thought it was a punishment for my sins. But I'm starting to realize that a lot of the time our experiences or difficulties are either a means of protection or Allah's way of making sure we stick to the right path and don't falter over something small. A lot of the time, once it passes, we realize in hindsight that we got through it, and that we're stronger than we think we are.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s16 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

Safety. Although prayer is important, especially as a Muslim woman, we're told that if, say, Maghrib starts while I'm driving on the highway, it's not a smart idea to pull over and pray on the road, because you never know who else is driving, and when we pray we're not in full awareness of our surroundings, so the concern is safety, both mine and that of the people around me. When it comes to practicing, I make sure it's not somewhere I'm truly visible — in college, I'd usually find an empty conference room, or some corner that didn't make me stand out, mostly for my own safety rather than to avoid making someone else uncomfortable.

Besides Salah, dress matters, obviously, and making sure what I eat is halal, which can be difficult sometimes.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s17 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

A lot of the time it's become muscle memory, but there are moments — especially in Ramadan, or during the ten days for Hajj — when I'm physically putting the hijab on in front of the mirror and I have to stop and remind myself that my intention in wearing it isn't just habit, it's that I'm trying to please Allah and that this is an act of worship, and I make the du'a that Allah accepts whatever I'm doing. It's unfortunately not an everyday practice for me — that's a personal weakness — but it does show up at certain points.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s18 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah simply means following whatever He said, without misconstruing it to make it fit your daily life — the key word is misconstruing, rather than making it fit. For example, we're always told to pray at the earliest time, but sometimes that's literally not possible, like when you're traveling on a plane or a train, and if you have to wait a little to pray, that's okay. But misconstruing would be something like saying interest is actually fine because it's everywhere in this country. So to me, submission to Allah means knowing what's absolutely wrong, making sure you stay away from that, and trying to live the rest of your life according to what He said is good.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-013_s19 | question_id: liberation -->

Liberation, to me, is the personal freedom to practice Islam. Alhamdulillah, for most of my life I've been in a state where I can wear hijab, or even wear the abaya to university, and the next day wear jeans, and no one is going to scold me or attack me for that personal choice.

# Interview MWI-014

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

I wake up in the morning for the Fajr prayer — right now that's around 4 a.m. Then I go back to sleep after praying, and I wake up again to go to work. Right now I work at a private practice; I'm a physician, so I see patients for consultations. I have a short break between 1 and 2 p.m., though I'm usually running late, so it's closer to 2 p.m. when I end my morning consultations, around the same time as the Dhuhr prayer, so I eat and pray, then start the afternoon consultations again, and usually finish after 6 p.m. and go home.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

There is no single Muslim woman — there are as many different Muslim women as there are women in general — but Muslim women are supposed to be helpful, kind, respectful, and shy; these are values they're supposed to hold, among others.

As for my community: they are awesome. I don't think there's just one community — here in France alone there are many — but we are supposed to take care of each other like a family, like members of one family caring for one another. That idea comes from the recommendation we get from our Prophet Muhammad, peace be upon him; it's one of his teachings.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've always lived here. As for how my sense of myself as a Muslim woman has changed — I feel a responsibility to show good values, to show, not exactly "excellence," but the best version of a human being, so that when people look at me, they see only good things. That's important to give a good vision of Islam; I feel we should all be examples for others who don't know Islam — women and men both, but because women who wear hijab are more recognizable, we carry more of that responsibility. It's a feeling I've had — for example, during (medical) school there was a voice in my head telling me that because I wear hijab, I have to be better than the others. I couldn't be a bad doctor; I had to be a good one, so I had to study more, learn more than others, so I would have the right answer and set a good example — not particularly about Islam, but in general, during (medical) school, around scholarship and academics.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Yes, certainly. First of all, I had to take off my hijab when I was a student, whenever I went to school or the hospital. When I was a student, and also during my five years of work after my diploma, hijab was forbidden in the hospital, so I had to take it off every time I entered and put it back on when I left. That job was mandatory for me to get experience, so I had no other choice but to go to the hospital, and I felt I had no other choice but to take it off. I would take it off in my car, in the parking lot, before going in. That's hard — at first you completely disapprove, and you feel obliged to do something you don't want to do; you feel treated like some kind of criminal, like you're doing something wrong when you're not. You also feel that they don't want the full you, just a part of you.

It's enforced with pictures — like signs showing it's forbidden, clearly written. If you don't comply, there have been many problems for sisters who wanted to keep it anyway — some couldn't work, couldn't go to school, lost their job. There's a recent story of one who had received scholarship money for her studies, and since she didn't want to take off her hijab, they dismissed her from the hospital, and she had to return all the money, for something like four years of study.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s04 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes. I seek it through an online institute — I take classes on weekdays.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I started when I was twenty. I was born into a Muslim family — my mother always wore the hijab, so it was natural for me to wear it eventually; it was just a question of when the right time was. After medical college, I decided to put it on — I had moved cities at that point, to the south of France for three years, so it was like starting a new life, and I started wearing it there. I already knew the French law about hijab when I put it on: it was strictly forbidden in high school, not forbidden in university, but forbidden in the hospital, so I knew I would have to accommodate that. I knew it was temporary — just for a time, to get my diplomas, and then afterward I would do what I wanted, and that's what I did.

Something is still missing, though, because I left the hospital, a place I really liked working — now I'm in private practice, but I loved working at the hospital and I left because of the hijab restriction, so they lost a doctor who wanted to stay, and I lost a team I liked. I did decide to leave because I couldn't wear my hijab there. In my private practice now, I am able to wear a form of hijab inside — not exactly a scarf like this, more like a small cap; I don't know quite how to describe it.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I don't know if people understand anything — they just assume what they hear from politics and the media, that Muslim women are obliged to wear it, that it's not their choice, and that it's the opposite of freedom for women. I think that's what they think when they see a hijabi — not most people, but people who don't live around Muslims. There are places in France with very few Muslims, and I think that's the feeling there.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s07 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

It's not so much about Muslim women in general as about the hijab specifically — I've heard that I'm foolish, that it's not my choice, that I'm siding with women being dominated by men, that kind of thing.

## HOW DID WEARING HIJAB CHANGE PEOPLE'S INTERACTIONS WITH YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s08 | question_id: followup__how_did_wearing_hijab_change_people_s_interactions -->

In high school, before I wore hijab, my life felt normal, like anyone else's — even now I feel I have a normal life like anyone. But the interactions did change: I felt more isolated after I started wearing the hijab than before. People don't come to you as easily as they did before.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s09 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

Racism is part of this too. In high school, before I wore hijab, I still felt racism from my teachers and the school administration. They didn't want me to pursue medical school; they pushed me toward literature instead, insisting a lot, because my grades were better in literature than in science — though not catastrophically different. I'm sure that if I hadn't been Arab, things would have gone differently. One teacher told me plainly, are you serious, you will never be able to do medical school, just forget about it, and called my house many times. They had a plan for me to go study literature in Belgium and then possibly transfer to medical school there. My mother, who wears hijab, didn't want the principal to see her and put even more pressure on me because of it, so she sent my father instead.

There were so many stories like mine, of children from North African or Arab immigrant families being steered away from higher study and toward something more like a trade, rather than being guided toward what was actually best for them. When I asked directly, they didn't hide it — they told parents plainly that it was better for their son or daughter to go a certain route instead.

## HAS WEARING HIJAB CREATED BARRIERS IN EDUCATION OR EMPLOYMENT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s10 | question_id: followup__has_wearing_hijab_created_barriers_in_education_or -->

The admission process is through a website where you list your choices; back when I applied, there was no in-person meeting — it was based on high school grades and recommendations, not a physical interview. I believe that's changed now, and there is an interview process today. I honestly don't know of any case of a hijab-wearing Muslim woman applying to medical school and being able to attend that interview wearing hijab — it's a recent change, and I just don't know.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s11 | question_id: expectations -->

I don't know that it's about the community so much as internal expectations — from myself and my family, more than from the whole community. We come from immigration, an economic exile, so there's an expectation that we should build a better life than our parents and the first generation had after leaving their countries. When it comes to religion, my family transmitted it to me and expects me to grow in it.

As for expectations from the broader French society: there are certainly some. They expect Muslim women to be visible and engaged in society, in teaching, medicine, law, so we're not left on the margins, so we can help each other and build ourselves up. But there's also a different expectation from the non-Muslim French community — they tend to favor Muslims who aren't very practicing, who might drink at parties with colleagues, or otherwise seem "integrated," or really, assimilated. I don't know if that's an expectation aimed specifically at Muslim women, but that's the general pattern. It's really associated with immigrants more broadly — since a lot of immigration here is from North Africa, and most of those immigrants are Muslim, and Africans generally are Muslim too, so when people say "immigrant," they often mean "Muslim."

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s12 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

It depends on whether a decision affects my family or is personal — if it's about the community, I'm responsible to the community; if it's personal, it's for myself and my family. I don't know if this is a good answer, but I'd say you have to have a good intention, and the outcome should be good — not to hurt anyone. That's the important thing, I think.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s13 | question_id: role_family_community -->

The role is the same in the family and in the community — to be present, to help others, not to hurt them, and to set a good example.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s14 | question_id: belonging -->

Belonging means being part of something. When you're part of a community or a family, you have to be present, to interact with others in a good way, to set a good example, to help others in need, and, when you're in need yourself, to be able to expect that same support from someone in your group.

Yes, I belong in France — because the story of my family is bound up with the story of France. We didn't choose that, but that's how it is, so yes, I belong. But I don't feel treated like every other French citizen; I don't feel my place is quite equal to (that of) other citizens, even though I do think my place is in France. It's not exactly the same story as my family's, but a mixed one — because of the colonization of my country, my family ended up in exile here, which is why there are so many North African immigrants in France, and that's why we belong here too.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s15 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

I'm not sure the French, ethnically French people, see me as French. On paper we have the same rights, but in daily life we don't feel that we have the same rights — we can't rise as high as we'd like; mosques are closing, Muslim schools are closing, we can't go swimming because they won't accept burkinis or separate swimming times for women. It's not a huge thing on its own, but it still signals that you're not treated like any other French citizen.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s16 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Alhamdulillah, there are still many ways to learn about the religion here — you can go to mosques, gather with other Muslims, sit and learn Quran at the mosque. There are many things you're free to do in France, one hundred percent, and probably worse elsewhere, so there are good things too — many French imams you can learn from, ways to travel and learn about your religion. But the more time passes, the more restrictions there are — like I said, Muslim schools are being closed because they're accused of "separatism," meaning that going to a Muslim school is treated as separating your life from society, wanting to stay only among Muslims, and that's forbidden, so they close the schools. There was also the controversy around the abaya — they stopped girls in school from wearing long dresses, even dresses that weren't technically an abaya; if you looked Arab, or like an immigrant, or Muslim, and wore a long dress, you couldn't enter the school. That law was around 2003 or so. There was also a recent law about the surgical cap some Muslim staff wear in hospitals — a nurse was dismissed from her job for wearing one, and there was another case of a non-Muslim woman who had a hair-related medical issue and was asked to produce a certificate proving she wasn't Muslim. It's terrible. It feels like they're watching for this constantly, especially before elections, when they start targeting Muslims more.

On the faith side, there are also closures around prayer — if you can't get to the mosque during prayer time, you simply can't pray; when you're outside, you can't pray anywhere, because it's forbidden.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s17 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

At work, I'm at my desk, in my own room, so I pray there. When I was in school and university, you simply don't pray there — usually you pray when you get back home.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s18 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

I don't know — they never made me feel that I was a threat. I don't know if they feel it, but I've never felt that myself.

## ARE MUSLIMS TREATED DIFFERENTLY FROM OTHER RELIGIOUS GROUPS IN FRANCE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s19 | question_id: followup__are_muslims_treated_differently_from_other_religious_groups -->

Jews and Christians have their own schools, and those schools aren't being closed — it's specifically Muslim schools that are closing. Jewish schools have police protection; you can't touch anything related to Jews, because that would be treated as antisemitism, so no one says anything against a Jewish school. Islamophobia is a problem in France, but officially they try to deny it.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s20 | question_id: akhirah -->

When you're convinced about the Akhira, you have to judge yourself every day — was this a good action, is this something forbidden, is this something that pleases Allah, is this a sin or bad behavior? That's what awareness of the Akhira should make you feel. It also reminds you that you're not eternal here — you're just passing through, and you will pass away, so you have to leave something good behind, in your world and in your society.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s21 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Inshallah, whatever Allah wants is the good thing. This happens every day, in minor things and big things — you say inshallah, you pray the istikhara (guidance) prayer if you want to, or you make du'a and leave it in Allah's hands.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s22 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

It's the same, really — there's nothing that matters more than something else; it's a package. If you're Muslim in France, you shouldn't have to choose between praying and wearing hijab; you should be able to practice fully, if you want to. It's not for politics or other people to interfere in how you understand Islam, in what's right or wrong practice — it's not for non-Muslims to decide for a Muslim.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s23 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

The meaning of laïcité is separating religion from the state — that the state and government shouldn't interfere in religious affairs, and that all religions should be free to practice as they wish as long as it doesn't hurt anyone. That's the true meaning of laïcité, but right now, in practice, it means that some religious people aren't able to practice their religion within society.

Yes, it affects me directly — in high school, at work. Women who wear the niqab can't go outside in it. At the beach, you can't wear a burkini. There was also a recent controversy because the actress Anne Hathaway wore something similar (in a film), and people said it was fine for her but wrong for Muslim women to wear the same thing in France. So yes, we are victims of this law.

## DO YOU FEEL OPPRESSED BY THE STATE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s24 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_oppressed_by_the_state -->

Sometimes, yes, I do feel that, but then I think about the many good things in France, and I know other countries have their own good and bad. For example, thinking about Morocco, since I'm Moroccan — yes, you can wear your hijab and work in it there, but you also see poor and sick people who can't get treatment; in France, anyone can get treatment. So there are good things and bad things everywhere. Sometimes I feel oppressed, and then I think about the good things. I feel oppressed because I want to be treated like any other citizen, to feel that I'm just as valuable as anyone else, like any French person.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s25 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

Yes, absolutely. You feel good, like you're doing the right thing, when you fast, when you pray, when you have a connection with Allah — you feel you're on the right path.

## WHAT IS IT LIKE TO FAST DURING RAMADAN IN FRANCE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s26 | question_id: followup__what_is_it_like_to_fast_during_ramadan -->

You're allowed to fast. You get a lot of the same questions from people every year, even though you fast every year — aren't you thirsty, do you want a small coffee, I have chewing gum if you want it — but you're allowed to fast.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s27 | question_id: submission -->

It means knowing that you have to return to Him, and that everything you do in life, you do for Him. You have to follow His guidance, because He's given you the way through the Quran and through the life of the Prophet, peace be upon him, so you have what you need to follow that path successfully.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-014_s28 | question_id: liberation -->

I've never really asked myself this question, because I've never felt the opposite of liberated — oppression, or something like being in jail. So yes, I'm liberated, I'm free — I know I'm free to have my faith, free to say I'm Muslim, free to pray, to fast, to go to the mosque, to travel. I feel free in everything, so I've never felt unfree, even living under France's laws. I know they're trying to restrict us, but we always find a way — if you can't go to school, you study online; if the mosque is closed, you gather with sisters and brothers, you meet in parks. We're always trying to find a way to do what we need to do. If I miss a prayer, like Zuhr or Asr, because I couldn't pray at school, I tell myself it's for a good reason, that it's only temporary, and that Allah knows I had no other choice.

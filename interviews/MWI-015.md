# Interview MWI-015

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

No ordinary day is quite the same, but on a day when I'm working: I get up, I get my things ready, I take my car, and I go to the place where I work. I'm a general practitioner, a doctor in medicine. I do my consultations, then I go back home, do things at home, and then I sleep. That's an ordinary day.

Already in the morning I'm waking up for Fajr, alhamdulillah — I pray, I do my morning adhkar, my morning supplications. Before I go to work every day, I entrust my patients to Allah. I do this every day. During work I'm wearing hijab, alhamdulillah, and it shows that I'm a Muslim, and everything I do during the day, at work, I do with the intention that it's something of khair, something good, that maybe Allah will reward me for. I don't know if it's the right word in English, but every morning before going to work I always do this du'a, like the du'a of Musa: "Rabbi shrah li sadri, wa yassir li amri, wahlul 'uqdatan min lisani, yafqahu qawli," and another du'a from the Quran.

I ask of Allah — because I'm the one with the patience, I try to do my best, but at the end, the best physician, if I can say this, is Allah. I'm not (solely) responsible for my patient; I give that responsibility to Allah. I entrust my patient to Allah and tell Him, they are Your patient, not my patient, and I ask You to do good for them, to cure them, and to protect me — to be able to be the best physician I can be that day, to make the best diagnosis, to not make any errors, to not forget things. Sometimes I have to send a paper for a patient, I can't forget. So I entrust everything in my day to Allah.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I won't talk about my local mosque right now, because I've recently moved to the north of France — I'm just discovering my new environment, as I just got married, alhamdulillah. But if I had to describe where I was before, because I was there for at least ten years and was involved in the community — I was involved in the mosque, but also very much in an institute called [ISLAMIC STUDIES INSTITUTE 1]. I did a course in Sharia there — it was three years.

We are kind, welcoming people. A lot of non-Muslims also came to see what Islam really is, and subhanAllah, most of them later converted. We also had halaqas open to everyone — halaqas for sisters and halaqas for brothers; mothers of converted sisters came to understand. The aim of the institute was to be comfortable with our Islamic identities but also to be working citizens in France — to be comfortable with both identities and find our place with them. What I liked about this institute is that our Sharia courses were really applied to our day-to-day lives — we talked about how we behave at work, how we handle the hijab, how we deal with finances in a Western context. Our sheikh and our ustadat told us the aim of the course was to give us tools to be a benefit to the community, to the city, to French citizens, and to feel comfortable with our French citizenship, because most of the students, like me, were born in France — we lived here, we know France better than our parents' home country — but we needed to be able to live with this Islamic identity and not be ashamed of it. Here in France, until university, I was always taught to cover — to hide this Islamic side of me at work, in my studies. It was only in the last few years — I'm 30 years old now — that I became more able to talk about my faith. I couldn't before, or be openly Muslim. I had my hijab, but I had to not show my Islamic side through my words.

For example, I wore hijab at 12 years old. It's a very long story, I'll try to shorten it. I wore it when it became mandatory for me — in France, before high school, there's middle school, and it applies from middle school on; you can wear it later at university, but it depends. I had to wear it in middle school; I was in a Catholic school. Officially in France, when you're in a Catholic school — a faith school — you can normally do what you want and wear hijab, because it's not a public school. When I wore it, it was very difficult at the first school — I was bullied by the professors, it was very, very difficult. I didn't wear it fully — I wore a kind of cap that I had to take off when I entered the room, and the professors were really hard on me, and the director too. I later moved to another school, an institute with middle school and high school, and there Allah was very kind to me — I was able to wear the hijab, not fully, but with something showing my ears, because the director asked me to show my ears so it would be less shocking for people. It was very difficult — there was a lot of uproar in the school at that time. Parents asked the director to ban me from the school. They organized a big gathering in front of the school to get me out, because I was wearing this — it wasn't even a full hijab, it was a cap, like the under-cap of the hijab, showing my hair. At that time I liked dresses, I felt good in them, I felt feminine in them.

My parents thought well for me — they wanted the best for me, they didn't want me to have problems — but they always told me not to wear dresses, because it felt too Islamic. For example, I was wearing this under-cap — everyone knew I was Muslim because I covered my hair every day, so it was no secret — but I didn't wear dresses until very late in university, because I was taught to show the least possible of my Islamic side. What I wore was acceptable for a Muslim woman, not revealing, but always trousers with something long. I didn't wear dresses until really recently — I think I began around 23 or 24, when I became a little less concerned about what people think of me, about being "too Islamic." So that's an example. And I didn't speak at all about my faith — in middle school, high school, and even later at university, until very recently. I'm 30 years old, and I think I began talking about my faith at work only about two years ago, and I still don't feel completely at ease when I talk about it, because of the fear that stayed with me. Part of the problem, when the parents gathered to get me out of the second school — the one that accepted the under-cap — was that on the first day everyone came and asked me questions, and because I answered honestly, it came back on me. So I kept this kind of trauma: if I talk about my faith, they will get to me. I was really young, twelve years old, so it was a big shock to have a group of adults attack me — a big group of parents trying to get me out of the school. I was a very good student — one of the top two — so it wasn't about my attitude or my grades, it was really about the way I dressed, the kind of hijab, and it wasn't even a full hijab at that time.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I grew up in a very Muslim household, alhamdulillah — a very practicing household. My father is the local imam, my mother is also a Quran teacher — she's always at the mosque, we never see her at home, and if she's not at the mosque she's on Zoom teaching classes, mashallah. So I grew up in this environment, and I was taught Islam from a very young age. What I liked is that I was very lucky to learn Islam through logic, not just halal and haram — for example, we don't do zina, not just because it's forbidden, but because it doesn't protect the girl or the boy: feelings develop, they get attached, and if they separate, hearts get broken, and if a child is born from it, that child won't be protected. It wasn't just halal and haram, there were always reasons given, and I was lucky to learn it that way.

As for my identity — at the end of elementary school, when I was ten or eleven, I had a full year where I was really thinking. I was always convinced there was a God — that was never a question for me, I couldn't imagine existence without a God, it wasn't logical to me. But I asked myself that year, is Islam really the right religion? Because I'd only been taught this, I didn't know anything else, and in France most people are atheist. So it was a year where, every week, I took a book from the library to study a religion — I studied Christianity, Judaism, Buddhism, everything, one each week. I was ten or eleven, at the end of elementary school, just before middle school, and it was my way of choosing my religion myself. At the end of that year of research, I was even more convinced that this was the true religion, and I came to Islam knowingly.

I think that was the first step in my journey toward my identity as a Muslim. The second important step was the hijab. I wore it because it was mandatory for me — not because my parents asked me to, but because I had my menses. As you may know, when a woman has her menses, we become — I'm searching for the word in English — I became baligha. My mother didn't tell me to wear hijab or not; she called me by my name and told me, now you are baligha, I'm not responsible for your actions anymore, so know that everything you do from now on is between you and Allah. I will cover you, I will be there for you, I will support you, I will advise you, I will be there for you for everything, but I'm not responsible for your actions — I've given you everything, and now you make your own choices.

The hijab, for me — I didn't even question myself, I was very naive, I didn't know what would happen when I wore it. It was just self-evident: I'm Muslim, so I will wear it. I already wore it before, just for going out shopping, or at the mosque. It wasn't even a question — it's mandatory, I'm Muslim, I became baligha, now it's mandatory for me. I wore it without even thinking about the consequences — I didn't know what kind of consequences it would have, that it would permanently impact my life, that I would suffer a great deal because of it. Not because of the hijab itself — I think it's a ni'mah, a gift Allah gave me.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s03 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

It's a ni'mah because I think it protected me from a lot of things. What I like about the hijab is that when I talked with someone — especially during my medical studies — I was never seen as a woman who could be used or disrespected, someone people could not speak to properly. I was always seen as an equal, seen for my qualities, my personality, my place as a physician. I remember sisters, great sisters, some of whom knew more and did more than me in their day-to-day life. I don't know how it is in America, but in France, medical students have a reputation for being flirtatious, not very respectful — not with patients, but with each other, with the nurses. There are a lot of stories, not very proper, between students. Even the parties thrown by medical students are known for being the wildest. Students are known for talking a certain way — dirty jokes and so on. But with my hijab, because they knew I was Muslim, even without knowing the principles of Islam, they never made dirty jokes with me, they never talked that way around me — I'm not talking about the racist side, there was some discrimination there too, I'm talking about the misogynistic side. When I came into a room where there was a dirty joke going on, in the staff room or between medical students, they would go silent, change the subject — they never talked that way in front of me. There were sisters who weren't veiled who told me, it's unjust, I'm Muslim too, why don't they respect me the way they respect you? It protected me from bad things, from bad places. When there were parties, they understood I wouldn't go, so they didn't invite me, they didn't even approach me about it. If it was something respectable, they'd call me; if it was something wild and not right, they knew there was a limit they shouldn't cross with me.

I think it's really great. Even when someone was interested in me — before I was married, of course, now I'm married, so this doesn't apply — but even then, they knew they couldn't approach me casually; they had to come respectfully, ask for my father's number, or whatever, but not just talk to me like that, because I'm not available in that way. Anyone interested had to come with respect and dignity and serious intent. So I think it's a great ni'mah, and I feel bad for my sisters who don't see this side of the hijab.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s04 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

We have to adapt constantly — it's difficult. Prayer, for example, was always a big question. At first, when I was younger, I never even thought of praying at school or university, because, like I said, I had a lot of problems. I was scrutinized all through middle school and high school, and everyone was waiting for me to make the mistake that would get me out, so I didn't have a normal, peaceful childhood — I was always on my toes, always wary of what I said and did. I wasn't like other teenagers during high school, so I didn't even think about praying or finding a place to pray, even though it was a Catholic institution. During university, I always went back home to pray every prayer of the day, which wasn't always easy, especially in winter. Then during university I met a friend who was very confident in her faith, and she had a big impact on me — that's when I began to change, because I followed her to halaqas. We formed a little group of sisters and started being together every day, with other sisters who had different outlooks — some of them hadn't practiced in middle school and high school, so when they came to university there was a little more freedom. They didn't think like me — I was always wary of what I did, while they were freer in their minds, more at peace with practicing Islam publicly. So we were always searching for places to pray in secret — when we went somewhere, one sister would keep watch while the other prayed, to make sure no one was coming. We always had to hide. I remember a sister who'd just arrived from the UK, on her first day in France — she saw us wearing hijab and came up to us and asked, excuse me, where is the prayer hall? There was no prayer hall.

Officially it isn't prohibited — it's only recently that they added it to the university's internal rules, not the French law itself. Before that, officially you could pray everywhere, because unlike high school and middle school, you just have to respect the public peace — meaning you can't disturb the people around you, you don't pray somewhere people are working and you're in their way. So officially we could pray, but in reality — for example, there was a place where they caught us; it wasn't used for anything else, just old archives, books no one read, no one went there — and they came and told us we couldn't pray there anymore. If they found you praying even in an empty classroom, they wouldn't let you, they'd get you out, and you could even get a sanction. Because of the problems with the university, we were hiding — that's why we were hiding. No, there was no room reserved for Muslim students.

We could pray, and when I was at university we could wear hijab, but when you go to what we call a "stage" — a residency, where you go to the hospital and work, learning to be a doctor — it was totally forbidden. Every time I had to go to a new place, I was afraid, because I didn't know if they would accept me, so I couldn't wear the hijab. I never wore the hijab, or what we call the medical under-cap — I bought a surgical cap made of fabric, so it was less see-through, and I wore that. Even with that, sometimes there were problems. I was doing my residency in a city in the south — it wasn't the worst one, they let us wear it, and since I was a good student I was lucky, I didn't have a lot of problems. But in other cities, for example [CITY 1], they banned even surgical caps — students there couldn't wear one at all. Of course, if you're French with no ancestry from the other side of the Mediterranean, they let you wear it. But now, in the hospital rules in [CITY 1], they've added that you can't wear a surgical cap unless you're in surgery. I have friends for whom it was a nightmare in [CITY 1], and they didn't wear the hijab. Most of my friends who are physicians didn't wear the hijab during their studies, at least not at the hospital — in school they let us, but not in the hospital. Same with middle school and high school — most of them couldn't wear it at all. I'm one of the few who could, because Allah allowed it for me: the first day I went to my new middle school, the director told me he felt the new law banning hijab in France was unjust, that everyone should be able to practice their faith — he wasn't Muslim, he was Catholic — and he told me, don't worry, I will defend you until the end. He was true to his promise; he defended me even when parents threatened to pull their children out of the school, which was private. He told them, if you want to take your child out, that's your choice — I will only reconsider if I ban all the students who cause problems at school, since most of the problematic children's parents were the ones leading the group against me. So they backed down. But during middle school and high school I was extremely careful, because I knew if they found even the smallest thing on me, they would get me out of the school.

Unfortunately, no — I don't know many people like that director. Most of my friends and sisters, even from Catholic school, I've never met one, even though I've met a lot of sisters — I was very active in the community, in French Muslim student organizations — I've never met a sister who had the same experience as me, who was able to wear it in middle school or high school. Never, until now.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Most of them think we are oppressed — that we have uncles, a father, a husband forcing the hijab on us, or that we don't understand and are supporting a system that oppresses women. Once I was carpooling — we call it BlaBlaCar, a system where you travel with someone else you meet online — and I was with an older woman, in her sixties. Most people, when they meet me for the first time, think I'm not educated because I'm wearing hijab, so when I speak with them they're shocked — oh, you're a physician, you did studies? They're very surprised. When they see a Muslim woman wearing hijab, they assume she's at home, has ten kids, has a husband oppressing her, no education. Now it happens less, because I'm a doctor and finished my studies, so they have more respect for me, but before, when I'd talk, they'd act as if I were unusually intelligent for a "real" Muslim woman, which made them uneasy.

With this woman, for example — I'd recently gotten married, so we talked a little about the wedding — she asked about Islamic weddings, in my case an Algerian wedding, the traditions, because she'd never been to a wedding that wasn't French. I showed her pictures of traditional Algerian dresses, and since she was a woman I had no problem showing her the pictures without my hijab. She was surprised and said, but you're beautiful — why do you cover yourself, as if you had to be ugly to cover. I explained that as a Muslim woman, my beauty and my body are precious, and I don't give them to just anyone — you have to have a contract and rights to see my hair. If you're my father or my brother, you're responsible for me until I marry, and even after, if I have a problem with my husband, you're still responsible and accountable for me — that's why you have the right to see my hair. If you're my husband, we've made a contract, and I have rights over you just as you have rights over me — you've proven yourself worthy of seeing my hair, of touching my body. If you don't have a contract or a blood bond with me, you can't see my hair. I don't see anything more empowering than that — is there a woman more empowered than me? We have so much value, we're elevated, not everyone can touch us or see us or talk to us; if they want to talk to us it has to be for a professional reason, in a professional setting, or things have to be done properly. She was really surprised, and she told me, but I see many Muslim women who are oppressed by their families, who don't have money, who can't work. I told her, the first time a woman could open a bank account in France was in the 1970s — as a Muslim woman, I've had financial independence for 1,400 years, so that's what you're talking about. I told her I do work like my husband's, and he has a good-paying job, alhamdulillah, but he's responsible for it — what he earns in a month, I might earn in a week, but he's the one responsible for the house, he's the one paying for everything. That doesn't mean I don't help him — if I want to help, I do it out of goodness of heart, and sometimes I just don't want to, and I don't pay. That's how it is in Islam. So where are my "oppressive" words? Whereas a French woman has to be perfect at everything — she has the male responsibilities, she has to go to work, bring home money, pay everything fifty-fifty, but she also carries the mental load, because in the end, when the kid is sick, who stays with the child? The mother. Who thinks about the appointments, who handles everything? The mother. In France they'll tell you it's fifty-fifty, but it's not — in the end the woman does the cleaning, the dishes, everything, and carries what they call the "mental load" here — thinking about everything that needs to happen.

Because in the end she has the role of the woman — I think we have that caring side in us — but she also has the role of the man, she's doing both, and in the end she doesn't find herself, she's unwell, unhappy, because she's carrying both roles. On my side, if I decide tomorrow that I don't want to work, I just don't work — he'll have to provide for everything, like he's already doing. I don't pay for a lot of things; I only pay when I want to, because he's a good husband, he treats me very well, so I do it out of goodness of heart, but there's no obligation. What I do pay for — like when we go to restaurants, sometimes I'll treat him, or I'll buy him something out of goodness of heart — but I don't pay the bills, I don't pay for the house. I handle the household errands, and he helps me with that too, because we help each other, but I don't carry the mental load he carries for the bills. If I get sick tomorrow, if I have a difficult pregnancy, I don't have to think about (providing). I've seen my French friends who aren't Muslim — especially the ones who are physicians, because we have a good pace of life, alhamdulillah — if she's married to someone who doesn't have a high-paying job, the household is built on her income, so she knows if she burns out, if she has a difficult pregnancy, she can't stop, because they've built their life — the car, the credit — around her income. So she's in a dilemma, with a workload and a mental load that's monstrous. I don't have that, as a Muslim woman. So who is really the oppressed one?

For them, these laws are liberating me — liberating Muslim women, taking off the veil. I think most French people believe they're liberating women. When I first put on the veil, in middle school, it wasn't accepted by the professors, because at that time I was still a very brilliant student, involved in a lot of things — I was in the theater club, I did embroidery, I was in charge of things in my class, I had a lot of extracurriculars, I wasn't just a good student. For them, if I wore the hijab, I would become oppressed — before, I was the liberated Muslim woman who'd come out of oppression, who'd succeeded in life, who wasn't privileged — my parents are both doctors, alhamdulillah, so we didn't have to fight to be educated, but from their point of view I was getting away from the "Algerian way of thinking," the "Islamic way of thinking," I was liberated. So for them to see me put on the veil was like, what's happening? We thought she was liberated, educated — what happened? And that's when things fell apart completely with them. I don't think most of them were racist, or bad people with bad intentions — I think most of them, by bullying me to take off the hijab, thought they were liberating me, doing me good, because for them hijab is oppression, patriarchy, having no rights as a woman, having no liberty, no freedom — if you wear it, you can't do anything. I remember the mother of one of my childhood friends — I was really shocked by the words she used. She said that with the hijab I was going to become a "menagère" — in French, that means a woman who's only at home, cooking and cleaning and nothing else. Just because I put on the hijab, even though I was the same person, still in the clubs I mentioned, still focused on my studies, still had big dreams — it didn't change who I was — but they saw it as if I'd been oppressed by my parents, as if I'd destroyed my own brilliant future, as if I wouldn't have any future. That's how they saw it.

## HAS WEARING HIJAB CREATED BARRIERS IN EDUCATION OR EMPLOYMENT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s06 | question_id: followup__has_wearing_hijab_created_barriers_in_education_or -->

It depends — officially, no, but in my case I'm not really interested in hospital work, I don't like hospitals. I was more drawn to clinics. In small hospitals it's not too difficult, but in the big hospitals in big towns it's difficult to work with hijab. In the smaller hospitals in the suburbs, though, they don't have enough doctors — they're always searching — so sometimes sisters work there with the hijab because the hospital doesn't have a choice: either they accept the sister with her hijab, or they don't have a doctor. Most of the time, though, they don't work with a full hijab — they wear what we call a turban.

In public hospitals it's still really difficult, if not impossible. I think it was this year that a nurse got kicked out of a [CITY 1] hospital — not because of hijab, but because she was always wearing a surgical hat. She was a very liked, very good nurse — her colleagues supported her, did petitions, went on strike — but she still got pushed out. So it wasn't even a hijab. It's easier for doctors than for nurses, because a nurse is under the supervision of what we call the "cadre," the head nurse, and head nurses are usually harsher, harder on their colleagues. Doctors are under the supervision of the chief of service, who's a doctor, and he has so much else to do that he often doesn't even notice — if you're doing your work well, people tend to turn a blind eye to a surgical hat. But head nurses are much stricter about the hospital's rules — they always have something to prove.

During a training placement in the hospital, as a nursing trainee, I was under the supervision of the nurses, and that's when I had a problem with the cadre, the head nurse. I never wore the hijab in the hospital — I wore a cap. What helped, subhanAllah, was that I had two weeks of training and she was away on holiday the first week — Allah made it so. When she came back, there was only one week left, and everyone was already praising me, telling her how good a student I was, how hard I worked. When she asked me to take off my under-cap, she didn't know I'd had years of preparing for this kind of situation — I told her what a great nurse she was, talked about everything except the hijab, and she got so pleased with the praise that she forgot about it entirely. I only had a few days left, so it was fine.

The second time, I was doing a residency in gynecology, working with a doctor. I didn't feel right about him — he didn't do anything to me, but something felt off. He kept talking about religion in front of the patient, and I never answered. In the end I was right to be wary — he complained about me to the chief doctor. It was humiliating: I was in the middle of my rounds with the other students when they called me in, in a tone that made me think I'd done something wrong. I was afraid — I thought, did I make a mistake with a patient? But I knew I hadn't. The chief doctor put me in the corner during a staff meeting, in front of everyone, and told me to wait, that I couldn't go back to work. Then he told me: we received a complaint that you wore the hijab during work, and that's not permitted here. Then he looked at me and said, but you're not wearing a hijab, you're wearing a surgical cap. He turned to the other doctor and asked what he was talking about. The doctor tried to backtrack, saying, but last week she was wearing a black hijab. I told him it wasn't true, and that we could ask all the nurses and students if anyone had ever seen me in a black hijab in the hospital. He kept insisting, saying, no, she was wearing something like a niqab — completely contradicting himself. I told the chief doctor, if you want, I bought this surgical cap at a medical shop, I can give you the name so you can verify it's a medical device. He apologized, said it was ridiculous to have made me come in, that he didn't understand what was going on. All of it happened because the other doctor had seen me outside the hospital wearing my hijab. It was a ridiculous scene — he even claimed I always wore a black hijab, and I don't even like the color black, so it wasn't even true.

Yes, he wanted to make a scene, he wanted me out of the hospital, he wanted me to have problems — and if a training placement isn't validated, you have to redo the whole semester, so it's a big deal. There was also a lot of discrimination — for example, when I wanted to enroll in a class only open to high-level students, I went to the secretary, and she told me she wasn't sure my grades were good enough, even though I was among the best. I told her, enroll me anyway. I came out of her office, and as another student — with grades well below mine — went in, I heard her tell him, oh, you have great grades, don't worry, you'll get into this class. I went back and asked her, didn't you just tell me something different? She got flustered, uneasy, and said, no, no problem, I'll talk to the professor directly. I had a lot of scenes like that, because I was wearing the hijab and they didn't like it. I think for the secretaries it was hard to see a woman who'd studied more than them, who might become their superior one day, wearing the hijab. Here in France, if you want to work in cleaning, there's no problem with hijab anywhere, even though it's the same law — you can work cleaning with hijab everywhere, no problem. But when you climb the ladder, when you have a high-responsibility, high-paying job, then it becomes a problem, because I think it defies what they believe — you're supposed to be an oppressed woman, so why are you in this high-paying job? It means you're not oppressed, and that defies everything they believe. And I think there's also a colonial dimension — my parents are Algerian, and France colonized Algeria for many years, considering them beneath them. Seeing someone with Algerian roots reach a high-achieving position reverses that. As a doctor, I don't see myself as above anyone — tomorrow, if the housekeepers stop doing their job, I can't do mine; if the nurse doesn't do her job, I can't do anything, I'm useless. I don't think anyone is above anyone else, but from their point of view — having a high job means you're not oppressed, you're not an idiot, and we're no longer in a position of power over you as colonizers — I think that history is still on their minds, and that's why it's hard for them to see women wearing the veil in high positions. Everywhere in France, in airports, in hospitals, you'll see women in hijab doing cleaning work, and there's no problem.

## WHAT EMOTIONAL IMPACT HAS THIS HAD ON YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s07 | question_id: followup__what_emotional_impact_has_this_had_on_you -->

It did a lot to me. For many years I wasn't able to talk about what happened to me — I haven't told you everything about middle school and high school — because I couldn't stop myself from crying. To be honest, I had to see a psychologist, a therapist, about it, about a year ago, because I had trauma from that part of my childhood. It impacted my work, my psychological well-being — there's a whole way of thinking, a way of working, a way of living that was shaped by it. It left a huge imprint on my world, my well-being, my way of thinking. For years I couldn't have ease in my friendships, even outside my Muslim friends — I was always wary, because I'd learned at twelve years old that talking freely to my classmates was what got me into trouble.

After that, I was very wary for a long time — I couldn't speak freely with my fellow students, because I was in a position where I had to protect myself, and being at ease with others felt like it meant they could attack me. I was very worried about showing any weakness — I wasn't in a normal position like other students, I was, in a sense, at war, protecting myself, and I held that position for years. I had to unlearn it, and that took work with a therapist, because I also went through a period of having nightmares about that time. In the moment it was hard — I cried at home, though I never cried at school — but it had an impact long after, and my therapist told me it was trauma that I had to work through. Alhamdulillah, I'm better now, but it took a lot of work, because it affected me in many ways. During one of my residency placements, I found myself in a situation that made me feel like I was that little student being attacked by everyone again — it was very difficult. It was affecting my work, because I didn't feel well going to work, I was very wary — there was a supervisor at the time whose tone and way of speaking unconsciously reminded me of my professors back then. That's when I had to seek help from a therapist and understood that I had trauma to work through, to be able to leave behind the little girl who was bullied by her professors and become the woman I am today — proud of herself, proud of her Muslim identity, who won't be attacked again, inshallah, because now I have the tools. I understand now that at that time I was very young — twelve years old is still a child — and the adults attacking me were two or three times my age. Now that I'm thirty, I think, how could I attack a twelve- or thirteen-year-old child, say harmful things to them? It's impossible, I can't even imagine it. I had to work through a lot with the therapist — it took six months, and there are still things I'm working on. It's better now, but it was a lot of work. And in all of that, I wasn't oppressed by my religion at all — on the contrary, my religion protected me and made me feel good. It was when I learned about Islam, did my Islamic studies, my Sharia studies, that I felt confident in my Muslim identity again. It was the French system that oppressed me — they were the ones oppressing me, really.

## DID YOU CHOOSE MEDICINE TO BE FREE TO WEAR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s08 | question_id: followup__did_you_choose_medicine_to_be_free_to -->

To be honest, at first I didn't study medicine to become a doctor — my dream was to be a researcher in genetics. But here in France, it's not like in the USA, where researchers have more recognition and more standing. Here, if you stay in France — I'm not talking about going abroad — even if you do biology studies and reach a doctorate, the diploma doesn't carry much weight, because there are so many other students like you and not enough positions. So I chose medical studies thinking I'd become a doctor, and then, when I chose my specialty, I could become a biologist or a geneticist and go back to research, but with the weight of a medical diploma behind me. I did what we call a "double cursus" — I did the physician training, and in my second or third year I also worked toward a master's in biology and genetics.

At that point you have to do a placement in a research lab, and that's when I tried it and didn't like it at all — it was interesting, but I didn't see myself working in that field. When I went to the hospital, I loved being with patients, so that's why I changed direction. But I saw, during that lab placement, that people with a biology diploma — even when they're doctors, at the highest level, known for being very good in their field, like genetics — are always viewed as less than physicians. We had a doctoral student everyone called "the encyclopedia," because you could ask him anything and he'd answer with names of studies and everything — he was incredible — but he had a lesser standing than another student who was a surgeon and had done only six months in that specialty. The "encyclopedia" had fewer students under him because he was in a less prestigious field, but the surgeon was seen as higher up the ladder — he had his own office, while the "encyclopedia" shared an office with everyone. Here in France, biology just doesn't carry the same weight as a medical diploma, and that's why, as a hijabi, I thought I needed a diploma that would carry weight, that would give me power and choices — which is what I have now: I have a big diploma, and physicians are in demand everywhere. If I'd done biology like some of my friends, I don't think I'd have had the same opportunities. So in the end, alhamdulillah, there was khair in that choice, because I didn't end up working in research — it turned out well. In the way I chose my studies, whether I'd be able to wear the hijab or not was actually a big factor.

## HOW DID YOUR FAITH HELP YOU MAKE SENSE OF ALL THIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s09 | question_id: followup__how_did_your_faith_help_you_make_sense -->

During the hard times, my faith had a great impact on me — it was my rock. I was always doing istikhara, trying to make good choices, always telling myself, rely on Allah, there's not much else you can do — rely on Allah, and He will make a way for you. Alhamdulillah, that happened — I saw miracles happen, opportunities that even privileged French kids don't get, and I still can't fully understand how I had them. The doors Allah opened for me are incredible. My mother used to say to me when I was younger, "yahfadh Allah yahfadhka" — keep the limits of Allah and He will keep you — and I've always tried to live by that. I sacrificed so much during middle school, high school, and the beginning of my studies, but after all of that, Allah gave me so much — I felt the barakah.

From Allah, for the sacrifices, the difficulty, the sabr I had back then, when I was little and didn't want to take off the hijab. I remember my father telling me once, during my residency, when I had that problem I told you about — if it's too difficult, you can take it off for this residency, it's only six months, and then you can put it back on, inshallah. But I told him, that's how it always starts — that's why some can't take it back off, or can't put it back on. I said, I will do what I have to do to keep wearing it, even if it means sacrificing my studies, redoing the whole semester — I won't take it off. My faith carried me through that, because Allah was with me — I was able to live through it and keep going. When I think about the little me, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen years old, a teenager enduring what I endured, I don't know how I was able to get through it without shedding a tear in front of them — I think it's because Allah gave me that strength. I always thought about the reward that would come from Allah, that even if everyone was against me, if Allah allowed something, it would happen, and if everyone was with me, if Allah didn't allow it, it wouldn't happen. SubhanAllah, so many times during my studies I saw doors open for me — opportunities that people with connections, with wealth, with everything, didn't have. When people hear about some of the opportunities I had, they don't believe a woman could have had them. I learned that Allah is the Provider — we're not doing anything ourselves — and I still try to hold onto that. Ya ghulam, ihfadh Allah yahfadhka. I hope, inshallah, if I have children, I can pass that on to them.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s10 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

When I was a medical student, it mattered a lot — now it's less the case, because I don't care as much anymore, it's take it or leave it: I'm comfortable with my identity as a Muslim, and if you take me as I am, fine, and if not, it's your loss, I'll find somewhere else. But that's not the case for most of my sisters — I'm privileged. It's not the case for most Muslim sisters who wear the veil, because even without a veil, just from your name, you already face problems — you're not the first choice for recruiters. I don't have sisters, only brothers, and they're in computer engineering, and they have trouble finding jobs, finding placements, because of their names — and they're not even openly Muslim, they don't have beards, they don't wear anything visibly Islamic. Nowadays I'm in a position of power — there's a shortage of doctors in France, everyone is searching for doctors, so now they're the ones searching for me, asking me to come work for them. So my choices are freer now, and I don't think as much about what will happen because of this or that — I won't come to work in a niqab, I won't wear colors that would shock them, I'm not going to go all black just to avoid upsetting them too much, but if I want to wear a turban, or wear dresses — I wear dresses now, which wasn't the case before. But during my medical studies, before I had my diploma, yes, I was always thinking about what the professors would think, what the chief doctor would think, what my fellow students would think — it was clearly part of my choices back then, totally.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s11 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

It's a bit better now, but it's difficult. I feel French — I grew up here, I have the codes of here, I understand the culture, the humor — French humor is something else, and if you're not French from birth you don't understand it all, but I have the cultural references of here, so I feel French. Unfortunately, though, because I present myself with the veil, it's complicated — they treat me like I'm not 100% French. Especially the older generation — the first question they ask is, where are you from? I tell them, I'm from France. They say, no, no, where are you really from? But it's not the same for me in Algeria either — when I go there, they call me an expression Algerians use for people who grew up in France — I'm not Algerian enough for them either, there's a cultural gap, things I don't understand without them explaining. So I'm not Algerian, but I'm not French enough for some, and I've had to choose for myself: I feel French.

The younger generation is easier, because they grew up around Muslims, so they're less worried, less afraid of us — they've seen veiled women living comfortably, just like them, going shopping, doing sports and activities, each with her own personality; every veiled woman is different. So with younger people, it's easier.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s12 | question_id: akhirah -->

I try, as much as I can, when I do something, to do it with the intention to please Allah, to make good use of what He gave me — the opportunities, the knowledge — because if He wanted to, He could take all of it away, remove it all from my mind. It's not mine, it's a ni'mah from Allah. In everything I do, I try, as much as possible, to make it something that will help me in the Akhirah, not something that will count against me there. ## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s13 | question_id: qadr_control -->

I told myself it's a test from Allah — Allah told us we won't be able to just say "I believe" without being tested. When I compare it to the tests the Sahaba faced — they were harmed physically, killed, martyred — what I've lived through is nothing next to that. So I knew, as a Muslim, I was going to be tested one way or another; this was how it was tested for me. And it was also a big ni'mah, because being constantly tested, constantly criticized about my religion, pushed me to really learn about it, to really know it and feel it — if I'd been shaky in my faith, I wouldn't have been able to withstand those tests. It pushed me to get closer to Allah, to learn, so that I could answer people's questions, so I'd be reliable, not shaky on my feet. I had to be sturdy — I had to know my direction, to be able to answer, to be confident in myself, and for that you have to learn; without learning you can't be confident. It pushed me in that direction, because I had no real choice: either I took off the hijab and left the Islamic path, or I became confident in my faith, and for that I had to get closer to Allah and know my religion better. In a way it was hard, because I was forced early on to be very reliable, very sturdy — of course we're not perfect — while I saw the difference with my brothers, who were more relaxed, who had time to discover themselves, find their own way, even stray a little, they had that time. I didn't have that choice — I had to hold on with my teeth and nails, or I would have been destroyed. There were many experiences I couldn't have, places I couldn't go, because I was raised wearing hijab and had that awareness that I couldn't set a bad example. I see it with my brothers, who had the same upbringing as me — alhamdulillah, they're good practicing Muslims — but they didn't have that early pressure to cling to Islam the way I did.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s14 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

I don't want them — I'm thinking particularly about my future daughters, inshallah, more than sons, because I think it's easier for boys here in France. When I was twelve and those problems happened, I took an oath — I was still young, but I promised myself I'd do everything I could to give the girls who come after me the opportunity to not live through what I went through. Since I was twelve, everything I did, I wanted to open opportunities for the sisters who'd come after me, including, inshallah, my future daughters — so they wouldn't have to always justify themselves, wouldn't feel out of place, wouldn't limit their dreams because they're Muslim. So what matters to me now is giving them that, raising them in Islam, and also maybe finding an environment where they don't face the same difficulties I did. For example, I got married far from where my parents live, which is difficult for me, but the region I'm in now, the north, is much better for Muslims — there are more Muslims here, and they've built more, they've built Islamic schools, there's an Islamic middle school and high school here, even though the French government is making trouble for them right now, so I pray they stay open. There are more opportunities here — you can go to a shopping mall and see a cashier wearing hijab, which would be impossible where I'm from, in the south.

There are more Muslims here, and they've done a lot — built mosques, educated people — so people in the north are less Islamophobic, more open, more welcoming, and I see that girls and women with hijab have more opportunities, and they're more confident in their faith. My husband's nieces, for example — I don't have nieces or nephews of my own — are very confident, outgoing, well-spoken, not afraid to share their opinions, because I think they were raised in an environment where they didn't have to hide their faith like I did, didn't have to be discreet, keep quiet. So I hope it will be the same for my kids — that I'll raise children who are proud of their Muslim identity, who have strength, who are comfortable in their environment.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s15 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

Laïcité — it's the source of all my problems, and it shouldn't be, because laïcité in France means the separation between the state and religion. It guarantees that every French citizen can practice their faith without problem — the people who represent the state officially can't display religion publicly, though in reality that's not always the case; in the Assemblée Nationale, sometimes you see people wearing a cross or a kippah, and there's no problem — it's only a problem with the veil. But officially, laïcité should protect us, and the same for other people of faith — Jews, Christians, everyone — it should guarantee that you can practice your faith freely. But if you're representing the state, you can't openly display your religion. That's what I told you earlier — in university, officially we can pray, but in reality we have to hide. Officially we can pray, because laïcité allows it — it says we have "la liberté de culte," as long as we don't disturb the public order. It means we have the liberty to practice our faith as long as we don't disturb others — if you pray in a corner discreetly, where you're not in anyone's way, officially you can pray anywhere. One time, a friend of mine, a law student who knows the law better than me, was praying in a corner of the university, and someone came up to her — she continued her prayer, finished it, and then calmly explained the French law to him. He couldn't say anything, because it was true, and they know it's true — that's why they added a new internal rule at our university, in [CITY 2], saying you can't pray at the university, even though that's against French law, against laïcité itself. So laïcité here — most people don't understand it as it's meant to be, as a freedom of worship — they use it against faith, and especially, though not only, against Muslims — they use it against Muslims being publicly Muslim.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

Submission to Allah, what does it mean? It means accepting and obeying whatever God has chosen for me, even when it means sacrificing, or not always understanding why — that's what it means to me. It means accepting everything He commands, and trying — I'm not a perfect Muslim — trying to obey Him as much as possible, even when I don't see the reason behind it. Alhamdulillah, we see a lot, especially nowadays, when the world is completely out of control and not working according to an Islamic framework — we see the problems that arise from that — but it means accepting the rulings of Islam, even in situations I don't fully understand.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-015_s18 | question_id: liberation -->

I feel most liberated when I wear my hijab and go out as a Muslim woman — when I can do my work, live my faith, without problem. Like I told you, I wore the surgical cap in the hospital, but when I wasn't in the hospital, when I was at the clinic — we had training in private clinics too — the doctor running the clinic could accept me with the turban or not; laïcité isn't an issue there, because you're not representing the state. And subhanAllah, it's not even that much more coverage — I haven't changed, I still pray, I still behave the same way, with the same modesty, there's no big change in how I act or believe — but that little more coverage I have, with my hijab, highlights the freedom. It's really special, and now I really feel the difference.

# Interview MWI-016

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

About an ordinary day in my life, like today: I wake up around 4:30 in the morning to do my Fajr prayer. I had a good intention to stay up and be productive, but I just went back to bed. Then I woke up around seven, got ready for work, had to choose what I wanted to wear, went to work, visited some clients, went back to the office around 2 p.m., did my second prayer at the office, went to visit the last client at 4 p.m., and from there I came home. I commute about 45 minutes to an hour, so I got home — I'm fasting, so I'm a bit tired — and I was exercising with my daughter just before you called me. No, that's okay, it's a regular practice — I fast Monday and Thursday. And that's it. So now my daughter has already made dinner for me, so I'm free until it's time to pray and eat again. That's kind of how my Mondays are.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s01 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

I think there are two obstacles for me to pray at work. One is my work schedule — sometimes I'm not at the office but doing home visits with clients, and if that happens, I don't pray; I just hold my prayers and wait until I either reach the office or get home to pray. I know it's not the most ideal thing, but if I'm interviewing people or doing visits, I can't stop what I'm doing to pray. Most of the time, though, there's a space available for me to pray at work — I usually use the conference room, and if not, there's a room designated for prayer on the floor above us. That works except when there are a lot of people in the office and everybody's using all the spaces. Maybe once every three months it's hard for me to find a place to pray, but otherwise, most of the time people aren't in the office and I'm free to use the space.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s02 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

That just happened recently, because somebody who'd known me from before — I'm actually a new Muslim, I reverted two and a half years ago — asked me, you dress differently, are you Muslim? I said, yes, I am Muslim. So then obviously the question comes: why do you dress like that? I said the hijab, covering the arms and legs, is a way for me to connect with God. It's a commandment that I choose to accept and comply with. Then we talk about what it means to be a Muslim here, and always the comparison comes up — well, women are oppressed overseas. And I say, well, women are oppressed here too, it's just a different type of oppression. (They) have to go through the struggle — like the volleyball team that didn't want to wear the skimpy uniforms, they wanted shorts, and they were penalized for it. So I explained to my co-workers: the best way to know about somebody or something is to not go by what you see on social media, because it's misleading and inaccurate. But if you're interested, you can visit the mosque — everybody's welcome, you can come and see people coming and going, see what people do there. It's not a cult, there's nothing weird — it's just people coming to worship God in a house of God, just like people go to church. That was the beginning of a four-hour-long conversation.

My community — I tell people that we are just people, just like everybody else. We have the same struggles, the same tests in life. The difference is that we choose to submit to the will of God, to follow the teachings of the Prophet, peace be upon him, and we know with certainty that this book, the Quran, is the truth. We try to comply as much as we can with all the guidance, all the commandments, all the requests — using the Quran as a guide for our daily life.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO ISLAM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s03 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_come_to_islam -->

It's a long story — do we have time? Well, two things, I guess. When I was a little girl, I wanted to be — not a minister, but a missionary. Somebody told me not long ago, and I said, well, you came full circle, you're actually doing that now, but not how you expected it, how God ordained it. But I went — I was raised Catholic. Because of doubts with my faith, I left the church and became an Episcopalian, which is kind of like a more bland form of Catholicism. Then in 2009, I had the opportunity to take a sabbatical from work, and I chose to — I had just lost my husband two years before, so I decided to take a solo trip to Morocco.

And I heard the Adhan for the first time, and it just moved me, moved me to the core. But I always say I'm not an impulsive person, because if I was, I would have immediately asked what this was, gone to the mosque, and become a Muslim. But it took fifteen-plus years for me to come around. That was the first time I experienced the call from God — the direct call from God. And I really felt at home. Moroccan people are very hospitable, kind, generous — I got to see people sharing water with strangers, just because they're at the same bus stop and one is thirsty. I said, this really feels like home to me. So I came back and decided to go back to school. During my bachelor's, I took an introduction to Islam class, and I wanted to learn Arabic — I thought maybe if I learned Arabic I could decipher what it was that had moved me. I was blessed to study Arabic in Jordan in 2015.

I did that, and I always thought, oh, my interest is just the language, I want to have the knowledge, I want to be knowledgeable, I want to educate people, because even the misconceptions about Islam were rampant in my family — we were very ignorant, we didn't know anything, we didn't know any Muslims until I went. So I started that process, and in 2016 I took another class on Islamic sciences with [IMAM 1], the imam of the mosque where I attend. He invited us, the students, to visit a mosque and come back with feedback. So I visited the mosque because he'd given us that assignment, and we stayed in touch. A couple of years later I was trying to create a group to better the neighborhood, to help people of different faiths come together, so I invited him — he came, the group didn't take off, but we stayed in touch. Before the pandemic, in 2018 I think, he invited me to a certificate program in Islamic studies at the mosque, saying he knew I loved to study and wanted to research Islam, so I took two classes. Then the pandemic happened and everything shut down, and I went on with my life. In 2023 I was going through some personal things, and [IMAM 1] called me out of the blue — I hadn't heard from him in about a year — and said they were starting the certificate program again, I should come back. So I took a class, starting in August, running through December. I think it was October that my life was unraveling, and I said, let me go back to church, because that's what I know and what brings me comfort. I went to church very conscious that I wanted to participate, to be connected to God again. Then the lessons from class came back to me, and I said, I honestly cannot say this prayer — not just out of habit, but because now I know the truth. I said, okay, now I have all this knowledge, so I can't deny what I already know. I finished the service, went to the parking lot, and cried and cried. Then I said, I know what I need to do. So I went home and did my Shahada.

I didn't tell anybody. My daughter said, oh, mom, you were a closeted Muslim. I said, yeah, because I'd still go to the mosque, take my classes at night — everybody would leave for prayer and I'd stay behind because I didn't know how to pray in congregation. By December, [IMAM 1] gave us an assignment to reflect on what we'd learned from the class. I wrote and told him I'd actually been a Muslim for two months, mashallah, and that I'd decided — because I had a trip planned to take my parents to Morocco — to go do my Shahada in the place where God first called me, and bring my parents so they could witness it. He convinced me otherwise — I was supposed to travel in April 2024, which was Ramadan, so he convinced me to do the Shahada before leaving. I did my Shahada at the beginning of Ramadan 2024. I've been very blessed — my family is supportive, in that they're not against it. After that, I had the opportunity to take my parents to Morocco — we celebrated Eid together, participated in the prayers at the mosque, and they kept me company. I continued taking the classes and finished the certification last December. Now I'm working with the group that supports new Muslims at the mosque. And that's kind of where I am right now.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s05 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

How do I see myself as a Muslim now compared to two years ago? I still see myself as a new Muslim — I don't know when I'll stop using that label, because there's so much to learn, I'm amazed at how much knowledge is required and how little we know. But I think these two years have made me more connected. I started wearing the hijab full-time about four months ago, so that's what's changed in the meantime. I continue to take classes, I try to build friendships and associations within the mosque so that becomes my social group, and I try to help now with the group for new Muslims. I don't lead it — we have an imam designated for it — but I'm a volunteer working as the administrative assistant, taking people's information when they contact us, guiding them to where they need to go, inviting them to our classes on Wednesdays. Now I'm contemplating enrolling in [ISLAMIC SEMINARY 1], in [STATE 1]. It's a constant (journey).

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s06 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

I attend classes at [ISLAMIC CENTER 1], and I also signed up for various groups online — there's a group for new Muslims based in Spain, and I took a class with them for Quranic recitation. Then somebody suggested a sister from Egypt for Quranic recitation, and I took that class too. I just finished the certificate program with [ISLAMIC CENTER 1] in December — a two-and-a-half-year program. Right now I'm still taking a class on Aqeedah at the mosque, and that's it, because I promised my kids I'd take a break from school. It also interferes with family life, and I'm trying to balance it, but those two years at [ISLAMIC CENTER 1] were intensive — I was taking classes Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays, so my kids didn't see me as much. So this year I said I wasn't going to take classes, but I did end up taking one — they said, we already knew you'd take another class. I'm trying to keep my promise not to enroll in school, but I'll still be held accountable for homework and assignments until next year or so. That's where I am right now.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s07 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

Because I'm a stubborn person — I've always been stubborn — so that part of submission to God was the hardest to accept. But I kept hearing, I read myself, the ayahs where God commands women to cover, and I thought, do I have to cover the whole way, or can I wear it very loosely? That's what I did — I said I'd wear it when I go to the mosque, and that's what I did for the first two years. Then I came to the realization that the real reason I wasn't submitting completely was just my nafs — pride, and I guess vanity. I like my hair, and I always thought I didn't want to cover my head. I said, that's really the reason I'm not doing this, and that's exactly why I should, because I need to go against my nafs. I made excuses — it would be weird at work, I don't know how my clients would see me. I didn't really worry about my supervisor because we have a good relationship, and he knew — I'd always talked about the classes I take at the mosque, and he knew when I became Muslim. Recently, during Ramadan, I told him I wanted to ask for a religious accommodation, to be off on Fridays and work four ten-hour days, and he was very flexible with me. When the realization came that I wasn't wearing it just because of my vanity, I said, let me just put it on.

I went to work thinking it would be so weird, but almost nobody noticed — well, one person noticed. She said, oh, you look different today, more elegant. I said, oh, thank you. As the weeks went by, people did notice: you're wearing a head covering all the time, are you Muslim? I said, yeah. That started conversations. One of my clients, who's known me for two years, finally asked me today — can I ask you something? I said, yeah. He said, are you wearing a head covering because you're Muslim? I said yes. He said he was just curious, because he'd seen me always dressed covered but hadn't put it together until now, after four months. I said, yep. So now it's just become part of me.

What it does for me — it's funny, because I never wanted to be identified as a Muslim. I didn't want to be like a token, or I was afraid that if something happens, my Latina side is going to come out and people are going to say, oh, see that Muslim lady with the hijab. My daughter calls me spicy, so that could come out. I didn't want to be identified as a representation of Islam if I did something bad, but actually it's the same thing, just in a positive light now, because now people see me — like my co-workers said, seeing someone wear the hijab reminds them of God, because that person is doing it out of that affiliation, that desire to do something for God.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s08 | question_id: misconceptions -->

During this time wearing the hijab, I get very respectful treatment from men, but I get dirty looks from women. I think it's the misconception that if you're wearing the hijab, you're somehow ignorant, or oppressed, or somebody made you do it. I had to go through that too — I have a big family, six siblings. When I told them I was going to be Muslim, my youngest sister, who's very close to me, was okay with me taking all the classes, but she asked me, please don't wear that thing on your head. At that point I said, well, I don't think I'm going to wear it, because I don't think it's compulsory — I don't have to wear it to be a Muslim. But time came around, and I said, well, actually, yes, I do have to wear it, because God asked me to, not because the mosque asked me. If the mosque had asked, I would definitely have said no. But because I read the Quran and know that He Himself asked me to, I do it.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s09 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Yes, because there's a lot of that everywhere, even within the mosque — there's the "hijabi police," the sisters who try to come fix your hijab, touching you without permission, or you see women commenting on other women's choice of color or style, and it's like, it's not my business, it's not your business. You don't know what that person is going through, how long it took them to embrace it, or the reasons why they choose to do it or not. We shouldn't just judge, but yeah, you see that a lot. I want to say (it makes me feel) sad, but most of the time I just get very passionate about it, and I want to correct people — in a nice way, because now I'm learning to be very patient, thanks to God. When people say something negative, I ask them, where did you hear that, or why do you think that, or why do you think I'm oppressed if I'm wearing this — not knowing it's a choice I made. So it could be a negative feeling, depending on the situation, but I'm trying to see it now more as an opportunity to educate people.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s10 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

Let me think — the difficulty (that) I encounter, well, we encounter it every day, in a way, because I'm trying to leave behind my previous life. My family gatherings — we're Latinos, so it's all about music and food and getting together, but there's also beer, because my dad drinks beer, and my brother. At work, for example, they were trying to build camaraderie, and they'd choose to go watch the soccer game — which I wanted to do too — but they'd watch it at a bar, so I couldn't go. It's not that I would drink, but I would feel really out of place, and I don't want someone to see me in a bar and say, oh, this person is wearing hijab but she's in a bar — it would be really incongruent, it wouldn't make sense.

I am the only Muslim in my family. I see it as part of my journey — if God wanted me to be Muslim from the beginning, He would have given me a Muslim family, but He put me in this Colombian family, and I became Muslim after my 50s. So I pray every day that my family also sees the truth and reverts. There are things I don't partake in, but I still come to family gatherings, I still come to Christmas. I explained to my family — I was the aunt who bought gifts for all my nieces and nephews, made things for everyone — and I told them, I'll give you gifts throughout the year for no reason at all, but I'm not going to give you a gift on Christmas. I was the one who'd send Christmas cards, and I told them, you're not getting Christmas cards from me anymore. My mom has been very nice — she makes sure there's no pork in the dishes, or she modifies the recipes for me. I also told my brother, if we split calls, or if you want me to get something from the store, I can get the fruit or the veggies, but I can't buy alcohol for you — okay, we get it. So for now, that's the compromise: there are some things I don't do, but I still want to be part of my family and part of the gathering.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s11 | question_id: expectations -->

Oh, yes, the patriarchy is real, even at the mosque, which I was hoping wouldn't be the case, but I have encountered it. I'll give you an example — during my first year, I took a class on Islam and marriage, and I found it very difficult to accept the advice the imam was giving. I stood up and said, I cannot agree with you, and this is why — I don't think the advice is sound for this time and age and for where we live. Things like, stick to your culture — but the Prophet himself said we're all the same and we're here to include one another. Or he'd say, if the mother-in-law doesn't approve, don't even bother pursuing a girl, and I'm thinking, but you had two marriages, so you didn't follow your own advice. What I was really upset about is he said teenagers need to marry young, girls need to marry at sixteen or seventeen — but they haven't even finished school, why would they need to marry just because the boy needs to marry? I said, that's wrong, because we both have a journey to God, and we need to stop going from being someone's daughter immediately to becoming somebody's wife. We need time to grow as a person, to grow in our faith, to grow as a human being, and then when we're ready, we choose marriage — not the other way around.

Then hearing the men make a list of what they want in a spouse, or the misconceptions that had to be cleared up during class — like, when you're having trouble with your spouse, you should put some space between you and not share the same bed, but then the next step uses a word that translates as "hit," though it's not really hit, it's more like separate — but men have taken that literally, in the wrong way, thinking it means they get to hit their wife if she doesn't obey. Those situations are still challenging. I tell myself this is why I need to educate myself, because I need to understand this for myself — I can't take the word of this man in particular, because if you think about it, who were the first people to share the knowledge of the life of the Prophet, peace be upon him? It was his wives, and men came to study from them. Aisha gave about two thousand hadiths, and then men ran with that knowledge and didn't give her credit, but started adding things. One example — they always leave out that Sahih Bukhari wanted to include, in the section on wudu, that women needed to unbraid their hair to wet it, and that wasn't included because they said it would create too much difficulty for women to do that five times a day. Things like that — you hardly hear about the forty thousand women who were part of the history of Islam and collaborated and created knowledge too, because all you hear is the history of Islam through the men's lens.

How do I understand my role? I think that as a woman in my community, but in any community, the place for women is to be nurturing, yes, but also to lead — women leading the families. Yes, definitely, that's the same role I play in my community.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOUR FAITH CONFLICTS WITH YOUR COUNTRY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s14 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_your_faith -->

How do I explain the relationship between my faith and the country that I live in? Well, everything (is) in God's hands, but I feel that this was part of the plan. I came from South America to be here and get to know Muslims, to attend the mosque, to have that experience. Growing up, there were no Muslims in my neighborhood — I didn't know any Muslims in my country, although now I know there's a mosque and a growing population of Muslims, so that's encouraging. In Colombia — so I think that was part of God's plan for me to be here. And the negative — I see that, well, I'm Latina, so I'm a minority in this country, and I think that now that I'm Muslim, that gives me a second dose of being a minority, because sadly we're seeing the rise in anti-Muslim speech and attacks and all these things.

I have a choice. When we confront problems, when we have things going on in our lives that are hard, it's like, what am I going to do with that — go back to the One who gave it to me.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s19 | question_id: submission -->

Acceptance, patience — consulting with Him, having that support that we know comes from Him. Before I was Muslim, I wouldn't really tap into that — I'd call out to God or go back to church or pray only when I was in real need, but I'd forget to do it in times of good. Now it's constant: it doesn't matter what happens in a day, a week, or an hour — if it's good, it's thanks to God, and if it's not good, it's still thanks to God. It's constantly thinking that way; that connection continues no matter what's going on in my life.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s20 | question_id: liberation -->

I feel more liberated in that I'm free to practice my religion and really immerse myself in the community — going to halaqas, going to meet friends at the mosque; that's kind of the place where we go. Part of it is also my age — I'm going to be 55 this year, and I don't have the responsibility of small children, so that plays a role too. Younger women have more constrictions from responsibilities, but for me now I'm free to spend my time doing the things I like to do — I like to spend my time at the mosque and seeking knowledge. I'm free of those mundane responsibilities toward others, so now I can spend my time doing what I want to do.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s21 | question_id: expectations -->

There's definitely (an expectation). I think there's an unspoken idea of things you need to accomplish by a certain age — by 23 you've got to be married with three kids. I see younger women struggling with not having good candidates for marriage, or this cultural idea that if you're 29 you're too old and nobody's going to want to marry you. There's a lot of baggage placed on women that isn't really religious, it's more cultural. That's true externally too — there are misconceptions, because people will also think... Well, there's a couple of things people get confused about. When they see, oh, my friend is Muslim and she doesn't wear the hijab, why do you wear the hijab? — you have to explain that. But again, I can't speak for your friend, I can only speak for my own choices, or how I want to identify, so I better not speak for her. And I'm also not going to say wearing the hijab makes me a better Muslim — she may be more knowledgeable than me, she may be more respectful, she may be more submissive to God. Wearing the hijab does not make me better.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s22 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

The internal (matters), because everything external — people can pretend. But if you don't have that sincere desire to purify your heart, to seek the pleasure of God, to stay in that state of communion with Him daily throughout the prayers, then what kind of Islam would you have if you're just faking the prayers, or covering because you think it makes you look more religious? It's not (real).

## WHAT LED YOU TO TRAVEL TO MOROCCO?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-016_s23 | question_id: followup__what_led_you_to_travel_to_morocco -->

I only got hints — I'd never even, I didn't even know where Morocco was.

But I got a bit afraid. I was at work, and I'd been preparing, saying, okay, I'm going to have two whole months off — that was my sabbatical. And one thing I did is, I didn't want my children with me — I really felt like I needed a break from my life — and I said, what's the most remote place I can go to? It just so happened that I had hired an employee who was from Morocco. He knew I was going to be gone for two months and was curious — how can I do that? I told him, you're going to have to work ten years here before they give you a sabbatical. But then I said, I'm struggling — I know I don't want to go back home, I want to go somewhere completely new and different than anything I know. And he's the one who said, you should go to my country, you should go to Morocco.

People are nice, and he put me in contact with his family — he said they could pick me up from the airport and help guide me to where I wanted to go, and since I'm a woman traveling alone, they could watch out for me too. Then another employee, who'd started just two months before, was also from Morocco, and he also said, go visit my family there. So I ended up spending one week with one family, one week with the other family, and one week by myself.

# Interview MWI-017

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Depending on when we wake up, we'll start the day with breakfast — I'll make breakfast. Before that, early in the morning, I pray Fajr and do some of my adhkar. My little one is a toddler, so after breakfast we'll do a little playtime or story time, and then he'll take his first nap. When he wakes up we'll eat lunch and either go on a walk or do some activity — each day is a little different. After our afternoon activity, whether it's a walk, playtime, or an excursion, we'll come back home, eat, and then start settling down for nighttime.

I prioritize my salah — whenever it's time to pray, that's the main priority. My day is organized around the obligatory acts of ibadah.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would say, for me personally, there's a strong sisterhood — I've built a strong sisterhood for myself. I have a lot of friends who are also like-minded, and they help in terms of reminding me and being a source of emotional support whenever there's a need. We can rely on one another. It's a very strong community, not just spiritually but emotionally — they help financially if needed. Overall there's just a really strong community and sisterhood.

I'd say it's a community of young women who are all striving towards the same thing, whether in our worldly lives or our afterlife, because that's mainly where we're striving. But we're also pretty ambitious when it comes to our worldly life — we're either in school or working.

I would say we're practicing — I can speak for my group of friends. We're all visibly Muslim, whether we're wearing niqab or hijab. We're visibly Muslim, but on the other hand we're also not too different from the rest, since we have the same goals and aspirations as everyone else.

I'm from [CITY 1]. I've lived in Canada my whole life.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO ISLAM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s02 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_come_to_islam -->

I came to Islam in 2016 — I was quite young at the time.

I've always grown up around a lot of Muslims. I'm predominantly Lebanese, Moroccan, and Somali — I'd say Somali was the more predominant community I was around. I always had them as neighbors, and they were really welcoming; there was a strong neighborly presence, if that makes sense, because now, depending on where you live, you don't really see that — neighbors don't tend to really know each other. But growing up, neighbors were always around; if they were cooking, they'd be feeding us, and we'd be feeding them. There was a strong bond there. I always had Muslim friends growing up, and then in high school I started doubting my own personal beliefs. I grew up as a Christian.

Some of my Muslim friends would tell me things, whether it was a hadith or something from the text of Islam, and it would resonate with me — I believed it. There was no doubt in my heart about what they were telling me. Eventually I started doing my own research. My friends would bring me to the masjid or to gatherings of Muslims where sheikhs would be speaking, and I believed everything I was hearing. I did delay my shahada for some years, but at the end of my high school career I decided to finally do it, due to the advice of a friend, and I just did it.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s03 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

There was definitely a lot of growth. At first I didn't really know much. At the time my friends weren't necessarily practicing — they were more cultural Muslims — and I stayed with them for maybe two years as friends. Then I realized I wanted to grow, to evolve as a Muslim; I knew I didn't want the same lifestyle I had before.

I attribute this to Allah — I think it was Him removing people from my life and introducing new people into my life. I started frequenting the masjid more often, even though I was anxious to go alone. At that time I didn't have much knowledge, and I would still consider myself new even after two years, because I wasn't wearing hijab yet, and I don't think I really knew how to pray correctly until I actually started frequenting the masjid, getting myself out there, and asking questions. Around 2018, two years after my conversion, I decided to put myself out there — I started learning more about my religion, dressing more modestly, and becoming friends with more of a practicing group of sisters. Up until now that was kind of my trajectory, going up, up, up, up, and then now I feel like I've plateaued. But I'd like to definitely increase in my iman and keep growing and learning.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s04 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Now it's mainly online, just because I'm a mother. But when I can, I try to go to the masjid or the local markaz here in my city whenever they have classes. Before, there were a lot of different resources here in the city — there was one masjid that had an Arabic program, and I went there. Even before that I was always learning Arabic, since 2018, once I decided I wanted to seek knowledge about the religion. From 2018 to about 2022 I was really immersed in seeking knowledge — I was studying Arabic, learning about Quran and tajweed, and I started memorizing the Quran. These were all programs in the city, but now it's mainly online.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

In 2016 I didn't jump into wearing hijab right away — I was doing more of a turban and dressing more modestly, long pants, long shirts, long cardigans. After two years, when I started frequenting the masjid, I noticed a lot of sisters were wearing really long hijab, really long khimar, and I liked that — I thought it was beautiful, so I started emulating them and dressing like them. For about two years I dressed like that, with long hijabs and long abayas, and then in 2020 I decided to wear niqab. I decided to wear it mainly because of the mask mandate — I wanted to take that decision into my own hands. I didn't want to be forced to wear a mask, and I thought the mask at the time was uncomfortable. I said, you know, I've always wanted to wear niqab, so why don't I use this as a gateway to wear it. Since then I've been wearing niqab, jilbab, and abaya.

I personally think it's empowering. I really feel the most feminine and the most empowered being able to take modesty into my own hands and not be objectified. The older you get, you realize certain things and you're more aware of how the world is, and I definitely feel safest being covered. When I'm engaging in conversations with people, they're not just looking at me because of how I look — they're engaging with me because of me and what I have to say, genuinely. So I definitely feel a lot safer, in the sense that I'm not being objectified.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I do feel like it's really misunderstood. Prior to the past couple of years, people were more open-minded, I find, especially in Canada — there's this stereotype of Canadians being super nice and polite. But I feel like now, maybe with the political climate, people are becoming a little less open-minded, because I've personally noticed a lot more hostility towards niqab specifically. Which is strange, because I've always lived in Canada and I've worn niqab for the past six years, and it's only become an issue in the last year. I used to be able to work with it on, to live my life wearing it, and I never really encountered any issues until recently.

I definitely think a lot of people misunderstand it and view it as something oppressive — mind you, this is a personal choice that I made as a young woman, and most young women are not deciding to fully cover. So they might view me as oppressed or extreme, but I obviously don't think so, because it was definitely my own decision, before I even got married. I think there's definitely a misunderstanding — most people don't really want to hear the reality of why a woman decides to cover or fully cover. It's more that they want to believe what the media is telling them instead of actually being open-minded and understanding why a young woman would want to fully cover.

## ENCOUNTERS WITH HOSTILITY AND HARASSMENT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s07 | question_id: followup__encounters_with_hostility_and_harassment -->

I've encountered — I can remember about three instances that were memorable, and here and there people who stare and give you dirty looks. One time I was driving from Costco, and you know how Costco is usually really busy with lines of cars trying to leave the parking lot — a pickup truck quickly swerved around me, and when he saw what I looked like, he cussed at me and said the F-word. I pulled up beside him eventually, when the traffic was light, and I rolled down my window and asked him if there was something he needed to say to me, and all of a sudden he went quiet. You notice that when you talk back and ask them, they kind of shut down, because they don't expect you to be a person under there, to defend yourself or ask them what's going on — if you have something to say, say it. You could tell he dialed back his aggressiveness, but that was just superficial, because he had this big pickup truck and I was driving a small sedan with my baby in the car. I just thought it was overly aggressive for no reason — we're just going grocery shopping on the weekend, there's no need for this.

More recently, we were at the grocery store closest to my house — my baby and I had gone on a walk and then stopped by the grocery store. Women I've noticed will more just give dirty looks or glares, or say something and walk away, but men I find are more aggressive. This guy came up to me — I had my AirPods in, and I knew he'd said something vile because of the look on his face. I took my AirPod out and asked if he needed something, and then he started with a whole verbal attack, getting really aggressive and screaming — people on the other side of the store could hear. He was getting red in the face, you could tell he was extremely angry for absolutely no reason; I guess the way I dressed aggravated him. He was talking about how I should go back to my country, that I should go blow something up, stuff along those lines, cursing and using all types of foul language. That was the most unsafe I've felt, because this person seemed like he could actually be of physical harm to me.

He eventually walked away. Obviously I was shook, just because this is a man and I'm a woman with my child — I wasn't expecting that, and this is my local store, so they know me. Eventually they got security involved and banned him from the store, and then police were involved, because I actually saw him the next day or two days later, so I realized he's someone who lives in my neighborhood. I definitely needed to safeguard myself, so I called the police and made a report — that's being taken care of.

In another instance, a woman was driving and slowing down her car — mind you, these are all within the past year, so I'm surprised it's been back to back to back. She was an older woman; she stopped her car and stared at me, and I just waved and gave her the peace sign. I guess she didn't like that, and she got really upset with me, so I just walked off — I don't have time to be interacting that way, I have my own stuff to do. There have also been little instances like people slowing their car or honking. Another instance, a white guy drove past and used an Arabic slur, a really derogatory word regarding women — I was shocked, because I'm a fully covered woman, so why would you use that word to describe me? He yelled it out of his car, which was embarrassing, humiliating. There have been a lot of weird instances like that in the past year — this is in [CITY 1].

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

When I first started practicing, around 2018, I was in university. At that time I had learned that interest-based loans are haram, so they're not permitted, and that caused me some difficulty because I was in the middle of my university and needed that financial help to pay for schooling. I ended up not taking the loan — in [PROVINCE 1] we call it [PROVINCIAL STUDENT LOAN PROGRAM], which is what a lot of people use to pay for school. I didn't want to take the loan; I was taking grants, but it became difficult to pay off the rest of the tuition because I had to work and take semesters off to work, and that became exhausting. I ended up actually leaving school because of that. It was a hardship — I wanted to finish school, but it was really difficult to manage and pay it all off. At the time I decided to work full-time, and I found a lot of fulfillment in that, because it was in a field where I was happy, working with children, and I decided to go down that path instead. I was able to go to college, and I found college a lot easier — I was able to manage working full-time and full-time studies online. I think it was a good decision for me, to be patient and eventually find something I wanted to do.

For me it was because ultimately I'm doing it for Allah's sake. I knew there was going to be no real repercussions — I'm winning at the end of the day regardless of what I'm doing. I knew there would be blessings in whatever I do moving forward, because I sacrificed one thing for Allah, and He opened more doors — He eventually did.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

Oh, I definitely think so. There's a lot of expectations placed on women, and I'll speak specifically about marriage. I find that a lot of Muslim women are expected — it depends on who you're marrying and who you're getting to know — but expected to leave off what they've built in order to start a family, to stay home. That can be difficult; I'm not speaking from my own case specifically, but I know some women who may have completed their PhD, and that's a lot of school, a lot of energy, a lot of work, and to be expected to, for example, be a stay-at-home wife — I think that's difficult to do. That's a huge sacrifice, and I feel like men aren't expected to sacrifice in the same way women are.

Not in all cases, but from my own experience, I feel like there's meant to be a lot of sacrifice from a woman's end.

## HOW DID YOUR FAMILY RESPOND TO YOU BECOMING MUSLIM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s13 | question_id: followup__how_did_your_family_respond_to_you_becoming -->

I'm grateful — their response was pretty calm. It wasn't a big dramatic change; they just accepted it and we moved on, even with my transitioning from being a turban-wearing Muslim to a more modest Muslim to full niqab. Everything's been pretty seamless — the transition. I'm grateful for that.

Yes, I've stayed in contact and received family support.

I think it's cultural, because where we come from, we're not from a culture where Islam is foreign — it's pretty integrated into the culture. It wasn't something alien to them; we grew up around Muslims even before they immigrated to Canada. They were always surrounded by Muslims and knew about Islam. So I attribute it to that.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s14 | question_id: belonging -->

Belonging — that's a good question. Honestly, I feel like as long as you're a visible minority and not of European descent, you don't really belong here. Unless you're truly assimilated and view things how they view things, you're going to be othered, if that makes sense. Especially now more than ever, I don't really feel that belonging, honestly speaking.

Or even other immigrants who assimilate well to the Canadian culture, quote unquote — maybe it's easier for them, they can easily integrate. But if you're like me, someone visibly Muslim who fully covers, I think that's kind of out the door. I may have gotten away with it in my teen years, but even then, I think it depends — they're more open to certain cultures than others.

I'll speak my mind on this — I feel like East Asian cultures are more accepted, because East Asians assimilate really well into Canadian society and culture, they can really integrate. But a lot of us who come from other Asian cultures or African cultures tend to hold on to a cultural identity a bit more without leaving it behind. If you hold on to a lot of your cultural background or identity, it might be harder to assimilate and be accepted by white Canadians.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND ASSIMILATION?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s15 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_assimilation -->

I think there's a way to be balanced, but in my opinion, the way they expect assimilation is like ignoring everything and becoming fully Canadian, if that makes sense. That's how I view assimilation here in Canada specifically. I think melting pot versus multicultural is a bit different — I think Canada is more multicultural than a melting pot, whereas in the States you can hold on to your cultural identity a bit more easily without it being such a huge problem. This is based on my own assumption and experience, but I feel like here you're kind of expected to forgo your cultural identity.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s16 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Previously it was really easy — I found it easy to be a practicing Muslim here, and I didn't find any issues. But now, especially given that I'm a lot more wary of going outside and doing my daily, mundane things, that's actually pushing me to consider moving to a more accepting environment elsewhere. I definitely feel a push to move away, for my own safety and for my children's.

Just the experiences — the hostility I'm noticing this past year only, it's becoming a bit too much to deal with. You don't want to go outside and have to deal with people staring at you and potentially harming you, and now you're seeing more instances of people being bold enough to actually harm people.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s21 | question_id: followup__hopes_for_her_children_s_religious_experience_in -->

Honestly, I do plan on relocating to a predominantly Muslim nation, for the kids' sake, because I want them to have more of a conservative upbringing, if that makes sense. ## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s22 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

Obviously with the hijab, because it's mundane, you do it every day and you kind of forget why you're doing it. But when I do remember, I try to remember that I'm doing this for Allah's sake — I'm not just covering my whole body and receiving hostility for no reason. I try to remember that as much as I can, just for Allah. Certain things do become mundane, because when you do things repetitively, it can become part of your routine, but I think it also depends on the state of your iman — if you're in a lower state of iman, you might not appreciate these acts of worship, but when you're in a higher state, you're more appreciative, more spiritual, more connected to Allah, closer to Him, your heart feels more full, and you feel that gratitude. It depends on the state you're in at that moment.

I'd say I try to be intentional about my acts of worship, because I want to be rewarded ultimately for what I'm doing — I don't want it to just be a wasted effort.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s23 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah means listening to His commandments and obeying. I don't think there should be arguments about what we hear from the Quran or the Sunnah — it might be difficult for some, but I feel we should try our best to obey the commandments that have been sent to us. Especially with things like the example of the student loan — it might be difficult to pay off your tuition, but I think it's better to do it slowly instead of putting yourself in debt with the sin of riba. I definitely think we should avoid major sins as much as we can.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LIBERATION?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s24 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_liberation -->

Liberation in terms of what? It depends on the context. As a Muslim woman, my ideas are based on my belief system, so I think the only way to truly be liberated is by obeying what Allah has commanded us to do, because He created us and knows what's best for us. If He's telling us to cover ourselves, it's for a reason, and you should be liberated that way. Personally, I don't feel objectified wearing niqab — that's how I see it as liberating. What's funny is that even when I'm fully covered I'm still approached by men, but I feel better knowing that I'm not actually seen by them, that they don't have access to me. That's definitely liberating, and I see the wisdom in why we should be covered. Men are wired a certain way, and maybe they can't always control how they think or feel — maybe they shouldn't act on certain things, but we can't control how people act, so it's better to keep ourselves safe. I definitely think hijab is a safety net for us women.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-017_s25 | question_id: liberation -->

I would say, in the fact that I'm a niqabi, I do feel liberated. I feel free — I feel like I don't have to conform to how society wants me to be. Women get so much pressure to look a certain way, to have their bodies look a certain way, to always get manicures, pedicures, their hair done — wasting all this money. If you want to do it, go ahead, but if there's pressure to do it and you feel insecure without it, that's a huge weight that nobody should really be carrying, unless you genuinely want to do it, not because of pressure.

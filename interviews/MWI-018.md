# Interview MWI-018

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

An ordinary day in my life isn't very exciting. My life in Canada — I'm a mom of three, so it's mostly getting them ready, getting them off to school, their activities, whatever they need to do. I have multiple roles: the mother, the taxi driver, the cook, the cleaner, the event coordinator, everything. At the same time, I'm doing my PhD, so pretty much I'm home twenty-four seven — once the kids are out of the house, I'm working, either on my PhD, another research project, or teaching part-time at the university: preparing lessons, meeting with students, responding to emails, serving on committees. That's pretty much my life. I don't really have a social life right now outside of that.

Yeah, that's a good question. Faith kind of structures the day. Alhamdulillah, I pray five times a day — getting up on time for Fajr, and on the times my alarm's gone unheard, I pray when I get up a bit later. The breaks I take throughout the day, how I schedule my routine and meetings, I try to schedule those around the prayer times. Sometimes it doesn't always work — I'm stuck praying in random places if I have to leave the house for meetings. But the prayer kind of guides most of the activities happening. And obviously during Ramadan that leads to a whole new schedule — alhamdulillah, given my work at the university, my schedule switches a little so I can sleep in a bit in the morning and do more work in the evening, because I get tired during Ramadan and fasting. Other than that, in Canada I wasn't really involved in a lot of religious-based community groups, just because I was so busy with my PhD and kids. So I'd say the salah and the fasting during Ramadan are the biggest ones, and then opening up the Quran and reading here and there, or reading a religious book — but I wouldn't say that's an organized, structured part of my daily routine.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I recently met someone — this was early spring. He worked for the property management company that managed the house I was renting; they do yearly inspections to check things like smoke detectors. He'd seen the pro-Palestinian sign in my window, since I'm a pro-Palestinian supporter, and he commented on it, and that led to us discussing Islam. He was saying there's going to be a civil war in Canada because Muslims want to take over, and I was like, what? No, I don't think they do — as a very visibly Muslim woman, I'm telling you they don't want to take over Canada and impose their own culture. One of the things I said was, even within Muslims, we come from so many different cultures — which culture would you even impose? We're very diverse. I guess he's just thinking broadly of Islam and Sharia law. So that's my tangent — I feel like lately there's just a rising fear of Muslims, and it's media-led, especially social media, where they have these ideas that Muslims want to take over and impose our own ideas.

To more specifically answer the question, I'd say that we are everyday people — we believe there's just one God and that the last messenger was Muhammad, peace be upon him, and we live according to the main principles of Islam, the Sharia. But it's not this evil thing where we want to take over the world and dominate — we're everyday people, we have everyday lives, our jobs, our kids, we want good for them, we want them to be successful, to do the things that are good and lawful, and stay away from the haram. We just want to lead positive, successful lives. That's the frustrating thing — what social media and the news try to present as Islam and Muslims, and what we actually want and do.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO ISLAM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s02 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_come_to_islam -->

I was actually thirteen years old, in a school that was predominantly white Catholic. I'd met some friends outside of school, and it was Christmas time — I asked one of the boys what he was doing for Christmas, and he said, I'm Muslim, I don't celebrate Christmas. It was a huge shock to me — I was like, what do you mean you don't celebrate Christmas, everybody celebrates Christmas, this is normal. He explained, no, I'm Muslim, this is what we believe. I was just fascinated, and I asked my mother, who's now deceased — I asked her, okay, what's the proof that Jesus is the son of God, that Christmas is normal and everybody should celebrate it? And it's actually her response that led me to learning more about Islam and eventually becoming Muslim. She said, there's no proof, it's just something you have to believe. I was like, what do you mean there's no proof, there has to be proof. And that's when I started learning about Islam, and I actually wanted to become Muslim from that age, but when you're young and scared — I knew those guys, but they weren't close friends I'd see every day at school, just from the neighborhood — so I felt like I couldn't do it on my own, and I only actually became Muslim in my early twenties. It took me quite a while to get there. I always had the intention, but to actually do it, I think I was just scared and needed someone to hold my hand.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s03 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I think I've changed a lot. When I first became Muslim, I believed the basics — the articles of faith, the pillars of Islam, all the fundamental parts of the religion. To wear hijab was a big no — I said, I'll fast, I'll pray, but I'm not going to wear hijab. But subhanAllah, I think it was within less than six months I started wearing hijab. I did focus on praying first — that was a big struggle in itself, to build that routine and discipline. Having discipline is something that has greatly improved since I became Muslim, and choosing to be a practicing Muslim, because you have to have that discipline to make sure you're praying five times a day, on time.

My sense of understanding the world, and the things happening around me — although we don't always have specific reasons and can't rationalize why things happen — it's ultimately having the understanding that everything is in the power and control of Allah. We are the creation, not the creator, whereas before I think I was more controlling. Now it's, okay, I'm not actually in control — yes, I'll put effort into what I'm doing, but ultimately, whatever happens is under the command and control of Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala. That gives me a lot of peace, in a way, and whatever the outcome is, I know it's for the best, even if it's something we don't always like — we accept that's what Allah decreed. I don't spend so much time mulling over it or being angry about an outcome that wasn't in my favor. I think it's just really putting that trust and faith in God that things will work out in the best way, however it's meant to happen.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s04 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

No. When I was younger, not married, no kids, I did — I would attend classes every week; I even managed a Quran center for a while. It was really nice to be in that environment, where women and children were coming to learn and memorize Quran. But especially since I had my youngest — even with my eldest, we used to attend a lot of halaqas and go to the mosque — my youngest was more challenging, so that made it difficult to attend anything regularly. Some of the halaqas I used to attend were online, so if I wasn't completely exhausted, I'd log in and listen to the lectures, or once in a while I'd download lectures onto a USB and play them in my car while driving. So I can't say I regularly sought knowledge, but at the same time I didn't completely abandon it.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

My hijab story — I can't even put a finger on what really led me to accept it and decide to do it. It was just a feeling, I guess — when Allah puts something in your heart, it changes the heart, and you go in that direction. I've been wearing it for almost twenty-three years now, so it's been a while. In the initial days, when you first become Muslim and you're so excited and want everyone to know — when you don't wear hijab, nobody knows, and everybody thinks you're just a regular white girl. But I was like, no, I want people to know I'm Muslim. So that could be one thing that motivated me — I wanted to be visibly Muslim. I was in college at the time; I converted at the end of January, and I think I started having that feeling toward the end of that winter semester, around April. But I was still scared, and over the summer I'd put it on here and there, and eventually I made the choice that when I went back to work, I would wear it.

I was working with immigration at the time, and I told security, on the Friday, with no hijab, listen, I'm going to be wearing hijab on Monday, so I need a new picture — it was just a weird announcement. He was just kind of looking at me, like, okay. So I showed up on Monday, and I even emailed my co-workers, telling them there was going to be a change — I think I sent it on a Wednesday, so they had a few days to get used to it before Monday. I don't always think things through very well, but anyway, it happened.

My level of modesty and conservativeness has fluctuated. At first, when I became Muslim, I'd wear jeans with a tunic, long sleeve, a regular scarf — a bit tighter. Then I became extremely modest, wearing only big abayas and big, oversized scarves. Then I calmed it down a little and went to wide-leg pants with long, three-quarter-length tunics, still quite loose. Now, honestly, it's more out of laziness — I just wear abayas, the same three abayas and one hijab, twenty-four seven. So it's definitely fluctuated. One thing I've noticed with sisters — and I'm not trying to be judgmental, because everybody's doing their best with the knowledge they have, and different personalities align with different ideologies — is that I can't say I'm the super conservative type who has to cover my face and can only wear abaya. For me, as long as you're trying to be modest, that's what matters.

Going back to what it does in my life — I still very much feel that I want to be visibly Muslim. I'm proud to wear it. It's second nature now; I just put it on, and I forget I'm even wearing a hijab among all these other people who aren't. But at the same time, I do very much want to be known as a Muslim, a proud Muslim — I have no fear in that. I've noticed that a lot of men became more respectful once I started wearing hijab — it sets a boundary for how they need to interact with me and the limits of those interactions. I used to experience a lot of sexual harassment, and that stopped once I started wearing hijab. So identifying as a Muslim woman is important, and it establishes those boundaries with the men I interact with. That comes from my appreciation and love for my faith, and wanting to visibly represent what I believe it means to be a Muslim woman. And also, it's a commandment from Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala — a commandment I'm happy to abide by, and I don't feel any negativity about it, or that it's a burden.

That understanding — that it's a commandment of Allah — came from when I started learning, at thirteen. I read a lot of books about Islam, the basics, the translation of the Quran, listened to lectures. I learned a lot in the early years, and even though I knew intellectually that I had to do it, I felt like it was something I'd never actually do. But as I progressed — after I said the shahada, started praying — I think my iman grew stronger, and then I was like, yes, I definitely want to do this.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

There's definitely the misunderstanding that women are forced to wear hijab, that we have no choices and aren't able to think or make decisions for ourselves. I can say that I have many friends who wear niqab, and it was them who made the choice on their own to cover — they're the ones who told their husbands and family it's something they wanted to do, which is the complete opposite of what most people think, that it's the men who tell them they have to. From my own experience and the people I know, it's been the women who made these choices themselves and informed their male family members about it. We're not male-dominated, unable to make choices for ourselves — the women I know are pretty headstrong, and we make the choices for ourselves.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s07 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

Going back to that example of the man who was saying that Muslims want to take over Canada, that there's going to be a civil war — it's frustrating, because no, we're just everyday people, we're not trying to take over the world and impose Sharia law. Even though I was telling him, Muslims are everyday people, we just want a good education, good work, to raise good kids, go to nice restaurants — he was just like, no, no, no, Muslims want to take over and take control. No matter what you're saying, they're not listening — they just have this idea in their mind and don't care about anything you're saying.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

Alhamdulillah, Allah has tested me a lot. Going back to what I said about Allah being in control — the situations we face that we feel are negative, that we don't like, they're difficult, but there's a reason behind it, and no matter what hardship we face, we'll be rewarded for it — better days will come, whether in this life or the next. That's really what gets me through it — having that certainty that Allah is not going to abandon us, and that good will come, no matter how long it takes.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

That's a really hard question to answer, because the community is so diverse, and I think we have little cliques even within the community — groups that are maybe more practicing, the Salafi, the Ikhwani, this and that — we're very much divided and diverse in many ways. Within those different groups, maybe there are certain expectations. The Salafis, for example, are more conservative, and generally the women will be wearing the abaya, the niqab — though obviously it's not the same for everyone. Whereas other groups, niqab isn't even something that's discussed; some might say wide-leg pants are fine, others say you have to wear abaya. It really depends on the group and their perspective.

The weird thing is I feel like I'm just a floater — I don't fit into any one group. These are the commandments Allah put for us, but how, and whether, we implement them is really a personal relationship between the person and Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala. It's not my business if somebody is wearing an abaya and black niqab, or wearing skinny jeans and no hijab. Personally, I don't even think about that — I just deal with people as they are. And, on that note, I've seen women who don't wear hijab who are more religious, more serious about praying on time, than women wearing niqab. So the dress itself doesn't always represent the level of religiosity or implementation of the essential pillars. That's something I think is important to know — I recognized years back that wearing more conservative attire doesn't necessarily mean you're a better Muslim.

Dress aside, it really depends from community to community. Some communities are more conservative, prefer women to stay home and become housewives, and education might not be so important, whereas other communities expect Muslim women to get PhDs and be successful in certain careers. And in terms of how they behave — having friends of the opposite gender, who they go out with, spend time with, smoking, those kinds of things — it really depends.

## INTERNAL AND EXTERNAL STRUGGLES AFTER ADOPTING HIJAB

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s10 | question_id: followup__internal_and_external_struggles_after_adopting_hijab -->

The biggest struggle from the beginning was internal. I don't know if it's just a convert thing, but it was a whole lifestyle change — not having the discipline, having to pray five times a day, and changing the way I saw the world and the things happening around me. My level of understanding all changed, so it was a huge internal struggle at the beginning. It did take some time, and I'm glad I took the time to go through it, because I think sometimes people rush through it — they're like, I'm going to be this perfect Muslim overnight, and then they can't handle it and they regress. Alhamdulillah, I took my time, focused on specific important parts and slowly implemented them before moving on to others — that helped ease the struggle, and I was able to mentally deal with everything.

The external struggle came along with the conversion too, with my non-Muslim family — they weren't very happy that I became Muslim, they were worried about what would happen to me, they thought I'd change and become this radical Muslim terrorist. Not just them — I lost friends too, especially once I wore the hijab, because then I was visibly Muslim; before that, it wasn't visible, so they could deal with it. Once I was physically wearing the hijab, the friends who weren't happy with Muslims and Islam, I lost those friendships. Externally, family members and friends really changed, even the places I went — I stopped going to places where alcohol was being served, and within my family, a lot of them aren't Muslim, so they drink alcohol, and I wasn't going to sit with them while they drank anymore. A lot of my family members didn't really know how to deal with me, weren't sure what to expect or how I'd change — it was uncomfortable at first, a lot of growing pains, but with time they saw there were some differences, I looked different, but I was still [INTERVIEWEE], and eventually they became more comfortable with that.

I'm still the only Muslim in my family, just myself and my children. Initially everybody was scared, they didn't know what to expect. I remember, maybe five years ago now, my grandmother made a comment — she said, we thought it was just a phase, that you'd go through this phase of wanting to be Muslim. They really expected that, and I think a lot of them thought I'd become this hardcore, conservative terrorist, and when they saw I was still the same person, they calmed down and became more comfortable. We settled into an understanding with each other — okay, this is how it is now: [INTERVIEWEE] doesn't come to gatherings where there's alcohol, [INTERVIEWEE] covers in front of men, [INTERVIEWEE] doesn't shake hands. It was just an understanding that [INTERVIEWEE]'s still [INTERVIEWEE] overall — she'll still sit with you, talk with you, joke with you, but there are certain limitations.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s11 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala, first and foremost — in terms of fulfilling the obligations of the salah, the praying, the fasting, all of that. After that, it might sound weird, but to myself — to be making the right choices.

A lot of my close family members are deceased. My father is still alive; we have a decent relationship in terms of communicating, but even when I was in Canada he lived in a different country, so there's always been that distance. I definitely help him when he needs it, but he's still very much independent, and it's more that personal, social communication rather than spending time together. That's why I didn't put my parents closer to the top of the list.

In terms of my relationship with Allah, it's making sure I'm fulfilling the requirements — praying, fasting. On a personal level, it's having balance between my religion and my long-term career goals and aspirations, and interpersonal relationships, with the opposite gender and with my own friends. And with my children — I guess maybe because I'm a convert and my family isn't Muslim to be there to support, I tried to raise them as best I could, when they were younger, as Muslims, especially my older son and daughter, who are eighteen and twenty now.

At the same time, I'm not the type of person to stand over them, saying you have to pray at a certain time. As they got older, I told them, you have to make your own personal choice about your relationship with Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala and your level of implementation of the religion — if you do it out of fear of me, you're not doing it for the right reasons. So although I'm not a hundred percent happy with the choices they make, I know inshallah the foundation is there, and everybody is going to stand before Allah on the Day of Judgment. I've tried my best, and the rest is for them to make their choices. My younger son, I'm spending a lot more time on now, because he didn't have those foundational classes when he was younger, so I'm trying to provide the same foundation, inshallah, that my older two had — and he'll have to make his own choice once he's older about how he practices his religion. At the same time, I'm their mother, they're my children — even if I'm not happy with the choices they make, it doesn't change our relationship.

## DOES LACK OF FAMILY SUPPORT AFFECT HOW YOU RAISE YOUR CHILDREN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s12 | question_id: followup__does_lack_of_family_support_affect_how_you -->

Yes and no. I don't know how it is in the United States, but in Canada these days, you see even children from Muslim families who aren't quite practicing at all, unfortunately making really bad life choices — and some of them have family members, or families, with close relationships with other community members. The Yemeni community, I'd say, and the Somali community as well, are quite tight-knit, where families spend nights and evenings together, so much time that the kids basically become like cousins. But even in situations like that, I've seen the boys or girls, as they get older, go in different directions, even though that support and foundation is there. So although I initially thought I was at a disadvantage because I didn't have Muslim family support, and my ex-in-laws weren't really involved either, when I saw other families struggling with even worse things than what I was experiencing with my kids, I changed my mind on that. And the solution for that, I don't know.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s13 | question_id: role_family_community -->

That's a good question. Within my family, especially since I'm a single mom and there are no other close Muslim family members, it's definitely my duty to keep reminding my children about Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala and their religion, and to encourage them to do good, both in terms of religion and their worldly life, and stay away from the bad.

In terms of my community, I'd say I overextended myself serving my community. I did a lot of community work, and a lot of times I really went beyond my limits, and it took away from my time with my children. Unfortunately I see this a lot with women who try to be involved in community work — just a few days ago I was talking with the woman who took over from me running the Quran center, and she's completely burned out, managing the job of two people, and nobody else seems interested in stepping up, even though it's something very much needed. There's a very small number of people who are motivated to create these community spaces and opportunities for kids and women to come learn, be with each other, support each other, have that sense of community, but unfortunately those numbers are the minority and they end up burning out from overextending themselves to keep those resources and services running.

So I'd say I'm slightly — I wouldn't say fully — recovering from overextending myself for the community. I'm trying to step back and focus on myself and my kids more, because I can't do it all, but at the same time I'm frustrated that not more people are stepping forward to work together.

I'd say it's the love of religion, and wanting that community — because life is hard, and it's easier when you have that community support, but the problem is that the people providing it are limited, and the people wanting to take advantage of it are many, so it becomes overwhelming. I know another sister who did the same thing — she'd organize weekly potluck dinners for women to gather, bring the kids, have that space to spend time and support each other, and the kids could be among other Muslim kids. But in the end she was the one doing all the cooking, so she burned out and had to stop.

I think I have a different answer now, especially because of that burnout, from over-investing myself in trying to do so much for the community. When I decided to do my master's, I stepped back a lot; when I decided to do the PhD, I stepped back even more. So I don't even really feel like I fully belong to the Muslim community anymore — don't get me wrong, I definitely do, but I'm just not as involved in community events and activities as before, and I'm okay with that, because of my own confidence in myself as a Muslim woman and my own religion — I know that my religion is really between me and God, not anybody else. It's nice to be around other Muslims sometimes, but because of that burnout, I'm like, I just need to focus on myself and my kids now. At the same time, I think it would have benefited my children more if I'd stayed more involved in the community, but I wasn't comfortable continuing at that level after the burnout.

That's a tough question — because I'm feeling more and more like, wearing hijab and being Muslim, I'm labeled as not Canadian. I've had people just walking down the street tell me to go back to my country, and it's like, I'm Canadian, I'm white. Obviously, Canadians originally come from many different European backgrounds, but that's something I've struggled with, I'd say, the past couple of years — feeling not as acknowledged, wanted, and accepted as a Canadian, because I'm a Muslim, more than at any time in the past.

It's still wearing the hijab — being a visibly Muslim woman — that means you're automatically labeled as, I guess, no longer white. I'm not the type of white Caucasian that they want. It's the religion and being visibly Muslim, that's the problem.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s15 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

That one's also hard to answer, because we have the masajid, there are Quran classes, Islamic lectures happening — to a certain extent you're still very freely able to practice your religion however you want. If you want to go to lectures and Quran classes, wear the abaya, everything, you definitely can. The only thing is that you do somewhat feel that rising tension, that you're different, and many people no longer want you around or accept you being around. So it's this weird tightrope — you can still practice as a Muslim, but you're not quite wanted or accepted anymore.

## HAVE YOU BEEN MADE TO FEEL YOU'RE A THREAT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s16 | question_id: followup__have_you_been_made_to_feel_you_re -->

Yeah, just walking down the street and being told to go back to my country. The biggest one — like I told you, the man who said Muslims want to take over, so he's thinking we're a threat, and I'm like, no, we're not, we just want to go to good restaurants, raise kids, have a good life — but no matter what I said, he still saw us as this aggressive group of people.

Another time, there was a big storm, and a non-Muslim couple came to my house because they didn't have electricity. I had the Al Jazeera news app on my smart TV; they made themselves comfortable, had the remote, going through everything. I was in the kitchen cooking, serving them, and I heard the man comment, Al Jazeera, this is the channel of the enemy — and his wife just kind of shushed him. I didn't even know what to say or how to respond, but at the same time I was so upset — I'm a Muslim, and I'm treating you so nicely, so kindly, but you still see Islam as the enemy. By default, am I the enemy as well?

## HOW DO WE CREATE CHANGE OR BETTER COEXISTENCE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s17 | question_id: followup__how_do_we_create_change_or_better_coexistence -->

That's going to be very hard to do, honestly, because what's fueling that is a whole media campaign, a social media campaign — that's a hundred percent what I believe. It's not something Muslims in particular are doing, it's that social media campaign, and it's been going on for generations, from the movies to the news to everything, basically creating that fear in people. Not everybody buys into it — I know a lot of people who have Muslim neighbors, spend time with them, realize Muslims aren't like that.

Some people have open houses, potluck dinners, sharing meals, trying to show people we're happy-go-lucky Muslims — maybe some will show up and be interested to learn, but the people just sitting at home watching TV, watching the news, those are the ones who will never show up and will keep spreading the same thoughts, no matter what we do. It's hard to say. After 9/11, Muslims were totally apologetic, everybody was condemning what happened, but did that change anything for Muslims? Did things improve? We were so focused on being apologetic and trying to distance ourselves that some people even took off their hijab to fit in, to seem like, we're fine, we're regular, normal people. I don't have a good answer — on one hand I feel like no matter what we do, for the most part they're not going to listen to us, and at the same time I'm frustrated given the social media campaigns and how we're portrayed.

I think part of the best thing we can do is just keep trying to succeed in public spaces, keep showing up. I attended an awards ceremony earlier this year, for students who received scholarships at the university, and alhamdulillah, many of the top scholarships went to Muslim students, Muslim women, hijab-wearing students — I could see the non-Muslim women were so amazed and impressed at these Muslim women who, for all accounts on social media, are these oppressed people who have no rights and can't do anything, but they see us showing up in these spaces, succeeding and doing well. Even some of my undergraduate students have commented on how happy they were to see me in the classroom, as a very visibly practicing Muslim woman, being the professor, teaching them, doing research, getting a PhD — they were so happy and excited to see me there.

One time, during Ramadan, I was fasting, and the course was during the daytime, so my mouth was getting dry — it's hard to keep talking. I'm pretty easygoing, so I just said, sorry guys, I'm fasting and getting so thirsty, just give me a minute, and made a joke about leaving my kids at home sleeping while I'm here with you guys. After class, one student messaged me and said she was so happy that I was comfortable openly talking about it — it was a class of two hundred students — that it normalized it for the non-Muslims too, showing them, yes, we fast, we pray, we continue our lives and do good things, and that's it. So with all my rants and tangents, my final answer is just to keep showing up and being successful.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s19 | question_id: akhirah -->

Oh, yeah, it actually plays a huge role, because I know that everything I do, I'm going to be standing before Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala on the Day of Judgment and held accountable for my actions — in terms of harming somebody, even to my own detriment, financially, physically, emotionally, I never want to be in a situation where I'm harming somebody else, where somebody can complain about me to Allah on the Day of Judgment. Honestly, in hindsight, I've even done too much, been walked over, in those scenarios, but at the same time you're trying to get the good deeds and not infringe on anybody else's rights, to make things easy for other people. Definitely knowing that I'm going to be standing before Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala and judged for my actions, and where I end up in the next life is heavily dependent on it — it plays an overwhelmingly huge role in the choices I make every day.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s20 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Beyond my control — I definitely take a step back and leave a choice for my qadar. I used to try to be very controlling, push things to go a certain way if I decided it had to be that way, but with maybe maturity and understanding in my religion, I accept that I try my best in whatever I'm trying to accomplish, but if things aren't working out, or it just seems like it's not meant to be, I'm okay stepping back and saying, it wasn't meant to be like that. That goes even with the death of non-Muslims in my family — some people dwell on it, and it's hard for them when a non-Muslim family member dies. For me, I don't know, maybe I'm just built differently, but the death itself is difficult, obviously, but in terms of their own afterlife, it's under the control of Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala — I have zero control over that.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s21 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

It's hard to say, because on one side I very much believe that religion is a personal choice and a personal implementation. I'm a woman, so I don't have to go to the masjid five times a day — I just pray at home, or wherever I need to pray, I find a place. I pray outside, in hallways, stairwells, changing rooms — I find a place to pray. And that's in the West — I've generally never had any issues finding a place to pray and praying. Same with fasting during Ramadan — it's a personal thing, nobody can force you or control when you can and can't fast.

Even if it came to a point where there were no Quran classes, no Islamic lectures, alhamdulillah, everything is online — the resources are always there for people to find and practice and implement.

What I really miss, and didn't like, is not hearing the Adhan, not having a wider community always remind you of Allah SWT. Now that I'm here in [CITY 1] — a few weeks ago I was in a taxi, and the driver was asking me questions because he knew I was a foreigner, and he asked, are you Muslim? He said, your answer is wrong — I said, what do you mean my answer is wrong, your question is silly — and he said, no, you should follow it with alhamdulillah. I just stopped and said, you're right, thank you for that. So sometimes it's those little interactions with fellow Muslims that remind you of Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala — that's something you miss if you're living in the West and not among a lot of Muslims. Obviously you see a lot of bad behaviors too, but it's those little things, those reminders, that make a difference. So my answer is that living in the West, you're still able to practice Islam, because nobody can force it on you — you can still pray at home or other random places, learn as much as you want, it's your personal choice. At the same time, I personally felt like something was missing.

It's a hard question for me to answer. After 9/11, I was in college for most of my college years and wasn't Muslim, and didn't spend time with Muslims. ## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s23 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

It's hard to answer, because of the layeredness — where do I even start. I've always had it at the back of my mind, the desire to be in a Muslim country — this was from years ago, but it was always delayed, because I felt I needed more education, needed to finish this or that. My intention was, I'll get a good job and come here with a job, which is basically not what I've done. But it was always delayed because of needing to accomplish something else first. At the same time, I strongly believe in what was written to happen — it was just meant that I didn't come in the past, and I'm here now. Even coming here, I had that layered intention — I was going to wait until after my PhD, but then I thought, it's taking me forever to finish, am I going to keep delaying things? So I decided to take the jump, throw myself in the deep end and just do it, whatever happens happens. I have somewhat of a plan, savings and so on — not no plan at all.

In terms of how I feel in my intentions, there's nothing better than hearing the Adhan — I love hearing the Adhan. My first apartment here was right beside the mosque; if you missed Fajr in the morning it's because you were dead asleep, it was so loud. Here you can hear the Adhan, but if you have the TV on or other things, you won't — which actually upset me, because I really wanted to be able to hear it loudly. At the same time, I can't say I'm spiritually different between Canada and here — I still feel the same. I can't say being in a Muslim country has increased my religiosity; I'd say it's still the same, whether I'm in Canada or in Egypt — praying, ibadah, everything is the same. Although, fasting the days of Ashura here, I was so thirsty — fasting in Canada is so much easier than fasting in Egypt — but that increases the reward. From a religious perspective, I don't feel different.

Fasting Ashura is something from the religion that's recommended to do, a commemoration of what Prophet Musa went through with his companions and how Allah saved them, and it's recommended to fast the day itself as well as the day before or after. As for the intention behind it, it's generally my practice, even in Canada, to try to do as many of the recommended days as possible — a regular practice, though I don't generally do the white days, I'll be honest, but the special days that come a few times a year, I make an effort to do those. That's nothing new — I'd do it whether I'm in Canada or here in Egypt. It's what I want for myself, what will remain no matter where I am.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s24 | question_id: submission -->

Submission to Allah means that I'm trying to abide by the commandments of Allah as much as possible, and do my best to fulfill them as they've been commanded. Obviously we all have weaknesses and shortcomings, but that means praying Fajr on time, not just Fajr, all the salah, praying them on time, trying to do any additional salah, fasting during Ramadan, trying to do any additional recommended fasts, giving charity, going for Umrah, Hajj, helping the community, helping the poor — the things Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala has asked us to do, commanded us to do, and recommended for us, again with the Akhirah in mind, that you want your scale of good deeds to be heavy when you meet Allah.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-018_s25 | question_id: liberation -->

It's a hard question — I don't feel that I'm not liberated. When you ask that question, I go back to the whole stereotypical standard that we're oppressed, we're not free, we're forced to wear the hijab and do all these things — and I just reject that, that I'm not even liberated to begin with. I'm a free woman, and even being a woman, I don't feel like it stops me. I know sometimes people see these patriarchal societies, how women need a man to do everything, but I don't feel that way — there's no man who's ever made me feel that I can't do something on my own, that I need a man to do anything. So in terms of where and how I feel most liberated — the whole world.

As a woman, Allah created us free. I know there's that stereotype that we need a man to do everything, that we're behind the men, but I don't feel that way at all — I've never been made to feel that way, no matter where I am, in Saudi Arabia, in Canada, in Egypt, or anywhere else I've traveled. I've never felt that I'm a second-class citizen, or that I'm not free. I guess that's just how I see it — maybe I don't have the cultural baggage; people with more of the cultural norms and expectations from quote-unquote Muslim countries might feel differently. Just as a convert, I've never felt that way.

I'd say it's the same — the only thing that's changed is my mindset, my way of viewing the world and processing what's happening around me. But in terms of feeling liberated, I'd say it's the same, whether before or after I became Muslim.

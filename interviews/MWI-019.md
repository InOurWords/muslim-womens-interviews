# Interview MWI-019

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Now I don't work — I'm on maternity leave, so it's a little different. When I was working, I was finishing my studies, so I worked in a public hospital where the hijab isn't authorized. I would wake up, put my hijab on over a surgical cap, and go to the hospital. Before arriving, I would take off my hijab and go into the wards wearing just the surgical cap. It's been an issue in almost every ward I've worked in, except one. I'd live my day normally, and at the end of the day I'd put my hijab back on and go home. Now that I'm on maternity leave, I don't think my hijab disturbs my daily life — sometimes I ask myself more questions about it, but now I wake up, take care of my baby, and go out and do whatever I want whenever I want. I don't have a lot of limits because of my hijab.

Faith is the center of my life. I make decisions based on my faith, I educate myself with Islamic content, and I pray wherever I am — I always find a place, especially in stores, like in the changing room of a clothing store. There are very few places to pray, even a mosque, in [CITY 1], especially for women. There are some for men, but except for the big mosque, the smaller mosques don't let women in. They say there's only one room and no one to supervise it, so they give it to the men and say it's obligatory for men (to pray there) — maybe women should pray at home instead. I don't know, but this is something that bothers me. So I pray in shopping malls, in little clothes shops. A few times I've prayed in a public park, but I was so scared that it's really only happened a few times. I think wearing the hijab is a demonstration of our faith in the street, especially in France. I'm in [CITY 1], or near [CITY 1].

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would say I'm French, I feel French, and that I worship only God and pray five times a day. I think if you haven't seen a Muslim woman before, there's nothing that special about us — maybe the hijab, but that's it. I would say we are a community that always tries to do good and avoid the bad, and something I really like is that people do things for Allah's sake. That's really different from the French people I know, who are mostly atheist — they tend to only do something if there's something in it for them. You will rarely see someone do something good out of nowhere. So I'd say we try to do good actions just to please our God and to have good deeds in the balance.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s02 | question_id: belonging -->

Of course, I was born here, and I feel a strong belonging — I don't know if that's the right way to put it, but I feel I belong to Western culture. It's a mixed feeling, unfortunately, because I also feel that my society is rejecting me, especially politically. Our politicians say so many things against us, it's crazy. Our Prime Minister recently talked about forbidding hijabi women from doing sports in high-level competition while wearing the hijab, and on TV, at the end of his remarks, he said "à bas le voile" — "down with the veil" — the way you'd say "down with the king" during a revolution. I was so shocked. Around the same time — it's quite recent that I started wearing the hijab, in 2023 — our education minister said it was forbidden to go to school wearing abayas. It was at that time that I was traveling for six months, and I felt so free. I couldn't stop thinking — the question wasn't when or why I would wear my hijab, it was why I wasn't wearing it, when I felt so free. So I decided to wear my hijab while traveling, and when I came back it was really hard, because I didn't know if I'd be able to stick with it. The abaya ban happened while I was traveling, and it made me even more attached to my hijab. I think that's one of the effects of events like that: people who are in the middle either become more attached to Islam or become more discreet about it. That's what happened for me.

It's not only about religion, because here in France we have this saying that religion isn't a hundred percent about the hijab — you can't say someone who doesn't wear the hijab isn't religious. You can have great faith, do many good deeds, and be very close to Allah, but not wear the hijab because it feels impossible. That was the case for me, because I was in medical studies, and it's forbidden in the hospital. It felt impossible. Every year it stayed on my inshallah list, and it seemed impossible until I traveled, especially to Malaysia and Indonesia, where I saw everybody wearing it and everybody was fine with it. I thought, so it is possible. It's so carefree and lightweight there — you see Muslim women in hijab everywhere, on buses, in hospitals, doing whatever they want.

It's forbidden for people who represent the state, like doctors, to wear hijab, but it's fine for patients — as someone representing a public service, it's forbidden for me. It shouldn't apply in private hospitals, but a lot of private hospitals make their own rules against it too; there, it's the head of the private institution who decides whether he wants to allow it or not.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

It's really difficult — you can't talk about faith in France, that's my feeling. If you say "I believe in God," people look at you like you're dumb, like something out of the 1950s, like a hippie or someone crazy. So I only talk about my faith with Muslims; with non-Muslims, it's one word and the conversation is over. It's taboo. For example, I was recently looking for activities for my son, and near my home there was a place called [FAMILY ACTIVITY CENTRE 1] that offered psychomotor activities for kids. It was free, so I went, and everyone was very friendly — even nicer than usual. Someone asked how I'd heard about them, and I said Google Maps. The next time I went, I noticed a cross in the building — it was a Christian organization. I wasn't sure if it was okay for me to be there, but I told myself it was fine. Someone asked me what my son's name, [SON'S NAME], meant, and I said it was from the Qur'an, and after that it was like "okay," and no more conversation. I've had the same experience in other places — at a mother's coffee morning, I was speaking French to my son while my husband spoke Arabic to him, and a French woman married to an American asked why I was teaching him Arabic, as if I should just be teaching him values. I said it's so he learns about (his) religion, and she just said, "okay."

I think it's the same with all religions — people are very faith-free here, especially in [CITY 1]. There's a lot of atheism. There are some Jews who sometimes talk about their religion. There are Christians who don't go to church and don't practice — they're Christian by culture, not really by faith. I even met someone who had her child baptized, not because she believed in it, but because she wanted him to get into a good Catholic school. And yet, in that church, the people were strangely warm and friendly — I think because they were practicing Christians, welcoming and polite. So I think people of faith understand each other more than we understand atheists.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s04 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yes, I try to. I'm learning Tajweed at a mosque, and I try to read — I read books quite frequently, I listen to podcasts, and I watch YouTube videos, like Omar Suleiman's.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I always knew it was something obligatory — although at one point someone told me it wasn't obligatory, it was just about control, and that suited me at the time because it was difficult. So I just lived like that. Then I traveled, and I felt so free. I went to Malaysia or Indonesia — I don't remember which — and I was in the countryside. We met some local people, and I told them I was Muslim too, and one woman said, "Muslim? Where's your hijab?" I thought, she's right — that's something you'd never hear in France. That's how I began to think about it. I was learning a lot at that time, listening to a lot of podcasts, and I was also so grateful, because traveling the whole world for six months had been my dream. Out of that gratitude, my way of saying thank you was to wear the hijab.

I felt free while traveling, so I decided to put on the hijab because I didn't feel the pressure I felt in France. Before that, I thought I felt free in France, because I didn't realize how much pressure I was under. It was only when I went to other countries that I understood what it meant to be free of that pressure. In South America, for example, we were always trying to find places to pray. Once, at a hotel where we hadn't even slept yet — we'd just left our luggage there — we asked if we could pray somewhere, and they said, "yeah, of course," like it was nothing. In France, if people say okay to that kind of thing, you feel like you have to thank them five times over. It will never feel as easy there. That's one thing that made me realize how much pressure there is in France, from society.

It's pressure to hide, of course, because here in France, on television and in political speech, they say the hijab is a tool of Islamization — that we're trying to Islamize people, that it's political, that we're trying to prove ourselves in public space. In France they say faith should be private — you can have your faith in private, that's fine — but the French idea of religion is that you're not supposed to show your faith. You're supposed to keep it private.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s06 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

At times I've prayed in the toilet, but I asked and was told that wasn't okay, so I've prayed in the shower — in the surgical area, not the changing room, there's always a shower — or in a patient's exam room, not where the patient sleeps, just where you examine them. When there's no patient, I pray there, behind the door, and I lock it if I can. If I can't, I pray just behind the door, so if someone comes in on me I can just stop. Some hospitals even have chapels, but they forbid staff from using them — they say it's only for patients, and practicing (Muslim) staff aren't allowed in.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s07 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I think it protects me in a lot of ways — you become more aware of your behavior. I'm proud to be a representation of Islam in the streets; I'm trying to be a doctor while wearing my hijab. I feel like I can be an example for young girls, and even for racists — there's this big idea in France that Arabs are just here to take public money, to have children and live off the state, so I'm proud to show something different. I also think it's good protection against men's looks, even if sometimes it doesn't stop them.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s08 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I think they need someone to blame because of poor economic policy. Maybe they also want to preserve some idea of French identity. Mainly, I think it's a way to cover up political mistakes — it's easier for people to look at Islam than to look at what's really happening in politics.

## HOW DOES YOUR EXPERIENCE DIFFER FROM A MUSLIM MAN'S?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s09 | question_id: followup__how_does_your_experience_differ_from_a_muslim -->

It's so different, because you often can't tell a Muslim man is Muslim just by looking at him — he could be Jewish, Portuguese, Spanish. So we, (women,) are the main target, for sure. And it's not only about the political representation of Islam — they hate the hijab because, to them, it represents submission: submission to men, submission to Islam. Their vision of freedom is that to be free you have to be free of your clothes, so to speak, and by wearing the hijab we're seen as submitting to men. That's seen as really against French feminism.

## WHAT DOES FRENCH FEMINISM MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s10 | question_id: followup__what_does_french_feminism_mean_to_you -->

I think the French woman (ideal) is someone who doesn't care about anything, who can live the life she wants — any job, any lover. Marrying, for example, is seen as really old-fashioned by my French friends and even my co-workers. I remember being at work, talking with my boss, who was just a few years older than me, and I mentioned I was married. She asked at what age. I said twenty-five, and she said that was so young — she couldn't imagine being married before thirty, that it was crazy. At the beginning, I didn't tell people I was married; I said "boyfriend" instead, because I knew it would lead to the same reaction: married, at what age, that's crazy. It's easier to just not say.

Another thing that happened: I'm a hospital specialist, and at the first meeting of my cohort, there was a buffet, and I was talking with someone a few years older than me. A waiter came around with food, and I asked if there was meat in it. When the waiter said yes, I said no thank you, and the older man I was talking to asked, "Oh, you're vegetarian?" I said yes, and then he asked why, since when. I thought, okay, I actually eat ham — I'll just stop the lie right now. It's just so much easier to say I'm vegetarian, but even that didn't work. It shows you it's easier to talk about nothing with them.

Another thing that happened — a doctor at a well-known center in [CITY 1], who's Tunisian, was talking about his upcoming marriage, which was arranged. People around him reacted like, "Oh my god, arranged marriage, that's crazy," and he ended up not getting the job because of it — they said they couldn't work with someone like that. So, the less you say, the better, I think.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s11 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I think it's part of the difficulties I have to face, but I think it's okay — it makes sense because I'm representing Islam. If I left France, who would stay to represent (us)? I'll have, inshallah, the means to manage, and I feel like if I left, I'd just be leaving people who are less educated or poorer to face Islamophobia on their own, whereas I can be an example of good social integration — a hijabi who works as a doctor. I think that's a good image for people, so I see it as my contribution.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s12 | question_id: expectations -->

Not that much, I think — I don't know, because I'm not so involved in the community; I mostly have Muslim female friends, so maybe I'm just not close enough to it to feel it. It is quite sad that there's often no space for women to pray at small mosques. I don't go to the mosque often, just for classes or for Eid — for Jumu'ah you have to check beforehand whether there's a place for women. I think there are some women who go regularly.

I see it on social media, but not so much in my daily life. If anything, I see a lot of people trying to ease into the hijab by wearing a turban first, especially in the Algerian community. In France, that community has a difficult history — we were under colonization for a long time, a hundred and forty years, and destroying our culture, especially the hijab, was part of French policy. After decolonization there was a civil war against Islamists — Islamists against the Algerian liberation movement — so there's a lot of trauma around this in Algerian families. When I wear my hijab, I see it in my parents' generation — not my own parents, but for example my friend's father looked at me like, "whoa, that's so radical, that's too much." They're frightened, I think, because of the civil war — they saw so much, and in the 1980s a lot of girls in Algeria were forced to wear it just for their own safety, and that's stayed in the minds of that generation. I'm also thinking of a friend's mother who is starting to wear the hijab, but very slowly — just covering the neck at first — because she has a whole social circle to navigate, and when you wear the hijab you're always worried people will say you're becoming radicalized.

Muslims and non-Muslims (both say this). Personally, I was afraid of being seen as radicalizing — we hear so much about "radicalization" on TV, like it's the worst thing on earth. They don't say it outright, but what's implied is that when you radicalize, you're against people, against the state, a danger to others, closing yourself off, not wanting to be seen. I saw a clip from a French TV show where a man said, "Do you realize how violent it is for me to see a woman in hijab? She's telling me I'll never be with her" — as if she's telling the whole world it's impossible to have her. It's crazy. I've also seen, on social media, people say that hijab isn't part of Amazigh culture, as if Amazigh women don't wear it — I can't imagine it's even harder for them.

I think the real issue isn't either of those things — it's that I felt like I had to say goodbye to a part of my identity before the hijab. When you put it on, you know a lot will change. When I came back to France wearing my hijab, I went to ask for information at a desk in the métro, and I was spoken to so rudely — before, I had a kind of "beauty privilege," where everyone was nice and easy with me, and with the hijab I never felt that again. But on the other hand, I felt like Muslims would come up to me, like they recognized a sister. Another time, cycling, I didn't stop at a red light — which is really common here, cyclists never stop — and a man got angry and called me "sale hijabi," dirty hijabi, meaning it as an insult. Another time, passing a café, a man who was drinking alcohol said, "Hey you, why do you cover your hair?" and I told him, "Isn't it your dream to see my hair? It's your dream." Then I just walked away. But there are things like that. On the other hand, when I'm cycling in [CITY 1], sometimes Muslim men will see me and say it's good to see a hijabi cycling.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s13 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

Of course, because in Malaysia I'm a nobody — I don't have social connections there the way I do here. At work, for example, I was talking about the hijab, and a young woman I thought was fairly educated asked me, "Oh, so did you start wearing the hijab when you got married?" I said no, and you have to explain, again and again. I just finished my studies this year — six years as a student, then six years as a resident, so you're a full-time specialist, but still a resident. I need one more year in the public hospital, inshallah.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s14 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

First, Allah. In the work environment, of course, I'm always afraid of my superiors, of people reporting to the administration that I wear a hijab, so I modify my behavior. In the last ward I was in, I never ate with my co-workers at the hospital, because you might run into someone from the administration, and if they see you in the surgical cap, they'll keep track of you until you can't wear even that anymore — because even the surgical cap is forbidden.

I hide when I can and try to be as discreet as possible. For example, when you start in a new ward, they take a photo of you for the hospital website — I let them take it, but then I asked the photographer not to post it anywhere, because I knew it could lead to me having to take off the surgical cap. I also don't let myself speak at conferences, because I don't want the extra attention — I don't want extra work for myself, I just want to finish my studies. I'd wear the hijab, but I don't want to speak publicly, because there's this idea in the public sector that the hijab is political — I don't want them saying, "you see, she's showing off her religion." I don't want the extra problems. It's worth it (to stay quiet).

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s15 | question_id: role_family_community -->

In my family, I try to be as good as I can — I try to educate my son, who's still very young, but I take it seriously, trying to give him love for Allah and for practicing the religion. In my community, I try to act mainly in an educational way. I've participated in meetings where I talk about my job and help medical students study, and I take part in mock exams — I sit on the jury and see the students. I think that's my way of helping the community.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s16 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

I'm not sure at all. One of the men in my family married a French woman, and when she met me she asked if I was born here. So I'm not sure — I don't think they see me as French. When they see the hijab, I don't think they do. Just recently I took my son to the doctor, and it was an Arab woman like me, but I felt like she looked down on me, like I was stupid. Since I'm also a doctor, I felt I shouldn't have to ask questions, but she was like, "no questions, just listen." It was for a BCG vaccination, and I hadn't had it done yet because we'd traveled to Algeria — it's actually best to get that vaccination before traveling there — and she said, "oh, you took him to Algeria, that's not good, not good to go without the vaccination." I wasn't even surprised. I don't know if she's Muslim, but probably. I've also had French people talk to me really slowly so I can understand, when maybe I speak (French) better than they do.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s17 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

Honestly, I have very little hope that things will change for the better. I'll teach my son as much as I can, especially prayer — I want that to be the most important thing, praying on time, even if that's not the easiest thing here. I don't think it can get better, honestly; I'd be happy if it just stayed like this.

What I fear is — there were social reasons why my generation didn't learn Arabic; it was treated as shameful to speak Arabic, and our parents wanted so much to protect us from being mocked, so a lot of people in my generation never learned the language. I'm glad my husband can teach our son. I fear him learning to hate himself — in French, détestation de soi, self-hatred, I hope that's a good translation — because we see so much negative talk about Muslims on television, and I don't want him to feel that. For example, I know of a boy at school who didn't want to wear nail polish, and the school called his mother in to explain why she was "forbidding" it, as if the boy should be free to wear it. Another story: a boy saw someone fall at school and said, "Allahu Akbar," and his parents were called in — "you said Allahu Akbar, that's crazy, radicalization," and so on. I also know a ten- or twelve-year-old girl, Arab but from a non-practicing family, who heard "Allahu Akbar" during prayer and asked why (Muslims) say it "like terrorists." I think it's not as intense now as it was after Bataclan and the big terrorist attacks in France, when "Allahu Akbar" really did mean "terrorist" to people. I feel that's slowly changing — maybe people are starting to understand that we say it in prayer.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s18 | question_id: akhirah -->

I wish it shaped it even more, but it makes me try to learn Qur'an, try to do good deeds, and make a lot of du'a. I don't know that it shapes it beyond that.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s19 | question_id: qadr_control -->

For the moment, it's still okay to wear the hijab in public space, so I can still do the things that are obligatory in our religion. As for the other difficulties, I think they're meant for us, and that they become good deeds for us — when you have difficulties, it's only so you have fewer bad things weighing on your balance. So I try to take it in a good way, to tell myself it will benefit me in the end, inshallah.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s20 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

It's a big word here. It's everywhere in the hospital — every time you start in a new ward, you get a PowerPoint about laïcité. It's crazy, really — laïcité, laïcité, you can't wear a surgical cap, it's forbidden, laïcité. But you'll see people wearing a cross necklace, and that's not a problem. Legally, laïcité just means a public agent has the obligation to be neutral — to show no sign of belonging, of religious affiliation. But in French politics they want to expand it to any individual in public space, and even ordinary French people who aren't politicians tend to think that's what laïcité means.

I know laïcité is really just a pretext for Islamophobia — it's very clear. Co-workers will report you to the administration if they see a surgical cap, but if they see a cross, they won't call anyone. Now hospitals even have people whose job is to be the "laïcité police" — they're literally called the chief of laïcité, and they go looking for surgical caps on our heads.

There's a story from my hospital. For a while it was okay for me to wear the surgical cap, but they always made it clear they were doing me a favor. Then one day, someone wrote a letter saying an employee wasn't respecting laïcité — it wasn't even about me, it was about someone at the front desk who wore her hijab visibly, while I just wore the surgical cap in the back. But because of that letter, it became a huge issue. They called me into the office and told me, in no uncertain terms, that I couldn't wear it anymore. I was so desperate. I went to another office, to the ward administrator, and she said she was sorry, and suggested I go to occupational health to see if a doctor could give me a note saying I had a medical need for the surgical cap. I didn't want to lie, but I went anyway, crying, and the doctor asked me what my husband thought about it. I thought, why does it matter what he thinks?

I went back to the administration, still crying — crying with rage — and she asked the same thing, what does your husband think? I said, it's not possible, leave me alone, I'm thirty years old, I'm a doctor, what does my husband have to do with this? I had even more rage after that. I called in sick for a week because I was so desperate.

Then I came back and took it off. It felt like taking off my dignity. It was terrible, I hated it. The administrator told me I was so brave, but it wasn't bravery — it was humiliation, an obligation, there was no bravery in it at all. I thought about it for weeks, and I couldn't accept it, so I put it back on. The head of the ward called me again, surprised, and I told him I'd gotten legal advice, so I was putting it back on — even though the legal advice I'd actually gotten was that there was nothing I could do, that I should just take it off. But I tried anyway, and they let me keep it for the rest of my internship, because it was only one more year, and I'm a surgeon (so they needed me). They wouldn't give me a research contract though — I think because they knew I'd be leaving anyway. I emailed to ask about a position for this year and never got an answer. During that search, I had an appointment with another ward chief where I wore just a turban, styled to look neat, but that felt undignifying too, I hated it. He seemed to like me — he'd already written to HR saying they had a doctor — but at the end of the appointment he told me there was no way I could keep anything on my head, because he was the chief of laïcité at his hospital too. So I didn't get that job either. I did find something else, but I don't know yet how they'll respond to the turban or the surgical cap — I had one appointment with the chief there and he didn't say anything, so it will be a surprise once I actually start.

It's okay for a hijabi woman to be a cleaner, but not to be a doctor — cleaning staff can wear a cap, even if it's not technically legal, because no one minds. I'm sure that's also post-colonial — it feels comfortable to people to see someone in a hijab in a position "lower" than them. That's just how it is.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s21 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_oppressed_by_the_state -->

Yes, of course. Especially around political symbols — it's crazy what they say.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s22 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

I'm always worried someone will find out — that's for sure — but I do it because I have to, and in a sense I can be proud that I'm doing it. Even if I have to pray in the changing room, I'm doing what I need to do, so I feel happy about that.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s23 | question_id: submission -->

I think it's believing in what He gave us. I think it's trying to be near Him, trying to have tawakkul, (trust in Allah). That's mostly how I'd put it.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-019_s24 | question_id: liberation -->

In my case, I felt liberated by wearing the hijab, I think. When I go to the hospital I feel oppressed, because I have to take it off, but when I don't have to take it off, I feel liberated, because I'm doing what I want in order to please Allah — I think that's what makes me happy.

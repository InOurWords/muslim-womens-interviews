# Interview MWI-020

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

I wake up in the morning, I do my worship, say my prayers, and go about my daily business — making breakfast and so on. I do a lot of volunteer work, I help people, and I do all the other things a regular person does, except I just dress differently.

I live my faith. I say my regular prayers, I follow the guidance in the Qur'an, and I feel that my religion doesn't interfere with my daily life — it is my daily life. I had a successful business running with my husband, and I was out meeting people and so on, and I always had a very good reception.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s01 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

Yes, I am. I just excuse myself for five minutes — it doesn't take more than that. I go to the washroom, wash up, go to a corner, take my five minutes, and that's about it, then come back to my meetings and my work.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s02 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I would tell them we're no different from you — we just believe differently, but we worship the same God.

You'll be surprised: I think the people around me are my community. It doesn't matter if they're French Canadian, Christian, Jewish, Bengali, Pakistani, or Indian — it doesn't matter to me. My community is the people around me. I don't associate only with a certain community, because I believe everybody is a human being and everybody's creator is one creator. For me it's been very good — up until now I've had a very good experience here, except for a couple of times in the fifty years I've been here.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s03 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've lived here almost fifty years — this September it will be fifty years. I came here in September 1976. I've had good experiences, except when I've heard horror stories about the government making laws to discriminate against Muslim women. On a personal note, I have French Canadian friends and English-speaking friends, and I don't see a difference. I'm perfectly comfortable, except with people dealing in areas where the education level is a little lower than where I move around, because the government creates an atmosphere of fear against "the other," and then the other looks at me as the other and is scared. For instance, after 9/11, I was wearing the scarf and working in an office building on the seventh floor, and I noticed that as soon as I got into the elevator there was this barrier of cold air around me, like people were scared of me. So what I used to do was start talking to them with a big smile — "oh, that's a nice bag, where did you buy it," "those shoes look nice on you, are they comfortable" — and once you start a conversation, I could literally feel the ice melt away. They'd have a smile on their face and chit-chat with me until I got to my floor.

I think everybody should do that — I don't think it's only because I wear this scarf and I'm visibly Muslim that I do it, but I make a point of it because I know the other person is scared of me, because fear-mongering has taken its toll. I want to break down their fears, show them I'm just another human being like them — I go about my business, I eat my food, I do my exercise, I go for walks, just like them. I only believe differently, I only dress differently. It's part of my wardrobe, part of my practice — it's not something to be scared of.

I didn't wear the hijab until 1997, after I did my Hajj. As I understand my Islam, I have to dress modestly — I don't have to cover my head, I don't have to flaunt my hairstyle, but if I'm modestly dressed and covered, I don't have to wear this. But since I went for my Hajj, I understood it differently: when I stand in prayer I cover my head, so I understood that maybe I should always cover my head. I also found it easier for me, because whenever I'm out somewhere and it's prayer time, I don't have to look for something to cover my head — I'm always ready for prayer. There were many reasons. One was that I wanted people to know it's not your dress that matters, it's what's inside you, what's inside your brain, that matters. When I communicate with others, I want them to feel I'm no different from them.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s04 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

I have to share with you that I haven't had that experience. There have been many occasions where I was the only hijabi woman among thousands of non-hijabi women, Muslim and non-Muslim, and I haven't felt uncomfortable at all. Sometimes I even forget I have it on my head — I'm driving, somebody cuts in front of me, I scream at them, and then think, "oh my god, they're going to think this is what a Muslim woman is like." For me this is just part of my dress now — I've been wearing it almost twenty years. My math isn't all that good, but I don't feel different.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s05 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Absolutely. I read a lot of books, and I've read ten to fifteen different interpretations of the Qur'an. For the last thirty years, maybe more, I've made it my mission to read a different translation every Ramadan, just for my own knowledge, because you really can't translate the Qur'an, you can only interpret it. An elderly person once told me that if you want to understand the Qur'an, you have to read different interpretations, because no two are quite the same — each gives a different perspective, because it's the translator's perspective, and the Qur'an is written in metaphors. So I've made it my mission to read as many translations as I can, though there are a few I keep going back to. I've also read good authors like Karen Armstrong and others. I want to build my own knowledge — I don't want to just listen to somebody else. I do listen to religious podcasts, but I form my own impressions and my own understanding. I don't like anyone telling me what's right or wrong. In fact, I encourage everyone to seek their own knowledge and understanding, because the Qur'an tells us that each soul is responsible for its own actions — we don't have the right to tell anyone they're practicing wrong, whether they wear a hijab or a niqab or neither. It's about how each of us understands our faith and practices it.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s06 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I started wearing the hijab when I went for Hajj — you have to cover your head there, and of course, when you pray, you cover your head. I understood it as a mark of respect to my creator, and that's why I kept it on: I said, I'm going to leave it on, it's a mark of respect to my creator. Since I have to cover my head whenever I stand in prayer, I thought, why not cover it all the time? I haven't faced any aggression, except two or three times in fifty years, as I told you, so I'm pretty comfortable with my scarf.

It gives me peace, because I know I'm respecting my creator. When we stand in prayer we're standing in front of our creator, in communication with Him. So if I'm already covering my head when I come before my creator, then by leaving it on, I feel I'm always in His presence. That's why I left it on, and it hasn't had a negative impact on me.

You could call it subconscious — that's my personal understanding, and someone else might understand it differently. For me, when I have my head covered, I feel I'm in the presence of my creator all the time, because I used to cover my head only when standing in prayer.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s07 | question_id: misconceptions -->

All the time — I'm giving interviews all the time, to the media, to PhD students like you, so many interviews in my life.

I blame my own community for a lot of this misunderstanding — we should have been more open, more willing to reach out and show people we're just the same. When I moved into this neighborhood in 1977 — I'd lived in an apartment building for a year before that, but nobody wanted to rent to us after that because we had four young children; they thought the children would destroy their apartment. So we bought a townhouse instead. When I moved in, I made a point of going out and mixing with the neighbors. My next-door neighbor, a Catholic woman, used to fast with me — when Ramadan came and she saw me fasting, she asked about it, and said she wanted to fast with me too. I asked her, don't you feel hungry, don't you feel thirsty? Because I knew that during Ramadan God gives us the strength to fast. She said no, whenever she felt hungry or thirsty she just offered it up to God, and then she was okay. On the twelfth of Rabi' al-Awwal, she baked a cake and brought it to my door — "this is for your Prophet's birthday." I've had very good experiences. She was French Canadian, and she used to come to my house for dinner, and we'd have coffee together.

There was another neighbor, a young English-speaking woman, who told me that when I first moved into the neighborhood, people were scared — "oh, Muslims are moving in, they're taught to keep their houses dirty, they don't live well." Then she said, "I come to your house and it's just the same as mine." I said, of course it is — people have these misconceptions about us, and it's up to us to remove them.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

By the grace of God, I've never really had difficulty other than financial difficulty — when we moved here with young children and my husband didn't have a job, we had a difficult time financially, but I've never had any other kind of difficulty in life. I've always had very positive responses and feedback from people. We got through it by budgeting.

I said a lot of prayers and asked for God's help, and I got it. I went back to the Qur'an, to the verses in Surat At-Talaq — I think chapter 65, from the second verse to the end of the third. That's a very good ayah for getting over financial difficulty, because God says in the Qur'an, "I will give you from whence you know not" — if you have faith in Him, if you ask Him, He will give you from whence you know not. So I prayed a lot, and I was okay — my husband found a job, we budgeted, our children went to school, and we were good, masha'Allah. Sometimes now I just think, my God, where did all that come from? I don't know, but it's exactly as Surat At-Talaq says: He will give it to you from whence you know not.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

I don't go to the mosque — I'm sorry to say that, but I feel very insulted that women are pushed into a corner, behind curtains. I've been a secretary at my mosque, and I was on the board of a masjid — the only Muslim woman on the board here in [CITY 1], actually the first Muslim woman on that board — and I saw how our Muslim men operate: my ideas were totally discounted. It's the Muslim men who need to evolve. Muslim women have evolved, but the men need to.

Yes, and it's up to us Muslim women to rise up against those biases, against those expectations. It's turning — it will take time, but it will turn, it will be okay. Women are standing up because they're getting more knowledgeable, learning their rights, saying, "no, this isn't in the Qur'an, you can't ask me to do this because it's not in the Qur'an." It's knowledge that we need more of. Things are changing because women are getting educated and learning their Islamic rights, because Islam gave women their rights more than fourteen hundred years ago, but the colonizers came and changed all that, and Muslim men took advantage of it and changed the religious knowledge, kept women uneducated — they said women don't need to go to school, just learn to read and write a little, do the household accounting, and that was it. Men needed the higher education, they said, and the women were left financially suppressed, without the means to stand on their own two feet. But now it's changing — now women are understanding that they have Islamic rights.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s10 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

My family — I'm responsible for bringing up my children. I taught them how to read the Qur'an, and I used to stand in prayer with them, because my husband traveled a lot for work, so I was alone with the children most of the time. I gave them whatever Islamic education I had, and I prayed with them. Now they're all grown and gone. My son lives in the States — he calls me every day, because I've told him not a day should pass without my hearing from him, since I worry. Every day I ask him if he's applied for anything over here. I don't like that he's there, honestly, to tell you the truth.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s11 | question_id: role_family_community -->

An educator, in every aspect of my life. I try to make life easy for others — God told us that, and that's what my father told us too, to help others. To tell you the truth, I don't make a difference between friends, family, outsiders, Muslims, non-Muslims — whoever needs my help, I'll help in whichever way I can.

Yes, absolutely — I was on the board of [COMMUNITY ADVOCACY ORGANIZATION 1], and on the round table of senior citizens of [SUBURBAN REGION 1]. I'm very involved in the community, and I feel everyone has to be, whether it's a Muslim community or not — a community is a community, and how will non-Muslims know who Muslims are if we don't get involved? In my family, I'm a leader — I lead my family toward goodness. I try my best.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

I belong to Quebec, but the government keeps telling me, over and over, that I don't belong. It's a huge — I can't find the word — the government tries its best to make me not feel I belong, although I do feel I belong. Because I've lived here — I've never lived anywhere else for this long. It's been fifty years. I was only twenty-eight when I came here.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s13 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

I have to share this with you. The government can keep on talking, but the people around me, they make me feel good. They don't discriminate against me, and life goes on as we want it to go on. The government can keep making rule after rule — who's following that rule? You will have one or two here and there, hijab-wearing women who have been discriminated against, and again I'll take you back to the reason I didn't feel discriminated against: first, I was raising my family and didn't go out to work, and then I had my own business, so I wasn't exposed to that part of society where I've heard horror stories of women being pushed down escalators because they were wearing a hijab. I have a friend who is a PhD, and when she'd just finished her PhD, she applied for a job at a university and got it, over the phone and by email — she was a hijab-wearing woman — and when she went in for her final interview and the man across the table saw her in the hijab, he completely did not hire her. All her credentials worked, her timing worked, her salary was fixed, everything was done — but when he saw her in a hijab in person, he didn't hire her. I haven't faced those situations, because I didn't go out into the world to work. I was home raising my children, because my husband and I had decided he would go out and earn the money, and I would concentrate on our family here. If I'd gone out to work, all my salary would have gone toward babysitting for four children — it wouldn't have worked out, budget-wise. So I said I'd rather give my children security and bring them up properly, and we'd make do with less.

My faith is my own faith. The province doesn't have any business in my home, and it cannot control my faith. I know Quebec has a history with religion — the Catholic Church used to be very imposing on people, and they resent religion because of that — but I don't resent my religion. My faith is my faith, and I'm a very good citizen: I've paid my taxes, I've done a lot of volunteer work, I've done a lot to help others, and they can't interfere in my faith. So I have no problem with it, although many times we've asked for a face-to-face meeting with government officials and they haven't responded.

## DO OTHER FAITH COMMUNITIES FACE THE SAME RESTRICTIONS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s14 | question_id: followup__do_other_faith_communities_face_the_same_restrictions -->

The Jewish community here is a very old, well-established community — Jewish people are involved in politics and everywhere, so they're very well established, very powerful. We can't compare ourselves to them; we're not that powerful yet. The Muslim community is very new, and it will take time to establish itself. We have to be more involved in the community, more involved in politics, more involved in education — a lot more involvement. That's how you control many aspects — you control the narrative.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s15 | question_id: akhirah -->

I live toward the Akhirah — when I follow my faith, I'm taking care of my Akhirah. I live according to how the Qur'an guides me, and that's supposed to be my Akhirah. If I'm not doing anything wrong, if I'm not cheating, if I'm following the commandments God set out in the Qur'an — I counted twelve, I don't know why — I should be fine, inshallah.

I'll share a story with you. There was a Christian woman I knew who was diagnosed with a terminal illness, and she explained everything to her pastor about her funeral arrangements — what dress to bury her in, to put her Bible in her right hand. Then she said, "oh, I forgot one thing," and asked him to also put a fork in her left hand. He asked why, and she said: at church suppers, they used to call out from the kitchen window, "hold on to your forks, there's dessert coming." So she wanted to be holding her fork, because she was expecting something good to come — that's her Akhirah, holding the fork, expecting dessert from God. I thought that was such a good idea. When she passes on, she's going to get dessert. That's what shaped my thinking — that I should do everything good, and have my fork in my hand, ready.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s16 | question_id: qadr_control -->

It's very easy, if you're a person of faith — my life is very God-centered, Alhamdulillah. If something goes wrong, I believe there must be some wisdom in it, and I ask God to show me that wisdom — that something went wrong in my life for a reason, because there must be something good in it. The Qur'an tells us not a leaf falls but that it is in His knowledge.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s17 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

No, my children don't have children yet. One of my daughters is a professional athlete. My daughters don't wear the hijab, and I've never asked them to, because hijab is something you have to be comfortable in — if you're not comfortable in it, it doesn't serve its purpose. As long as you're modestly dressed, that's between you and your Lord, your creator.

I'm holding the fork in my hand — I hope the future is good. I'm expecting God Almighty to have mercy on us and show us the way, although we're going through very dark times. I would like us — and when I say us, I mean the whole of Quebec — to understand each other: you're Christian, you're Jewish, I'm Muslim, you're Hindu, you're Buddhist, you don't believe in anything — but we are all human beings, and we should learn to respect each other's beliefs and move on as human beings doing good things for each other, who don't have to be enemies. We are all God's children.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s18 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

I don't get their version of laïcité. For me, laïcité means I'm allowed to do and believe and wear whatever I want — if I want to walk around in shorts, that's none of their business; if I want to wear a bikini, none of their business; if I want to cover and wear a niqab, that's also not their business. Laïcité means freedom of thought, to me. How is it laïcité if they're imposing something on me? That's not laïcité, to me.

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s19 | question_id: followup__have_specific_laws_bill_21_bill_94_bill -->

I have speeches on that — if you want, I can share them with you and you can take excerpts. But I'm not really the right person to ask, because, as I told you, I've never personally had problems because of these laws.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s20 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

I'm a very peaceful person — nothing really bothers me, honestly. If I have good health, food on my plate three times a day, I don't need much. Of course, three cups of coffee during the day is good too — one in the morning, one mid-morning, one at three p.m. — and I'm happy. I'm the kind of person who feels everything can be solved with dialogue: if you have a problem with me, sit down across the table, ask me questions, and I'll answer honestly. That's all.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s21 | question_id: submission -->

Everything. Submission is everything — if I'm not submitting to the will of Allah, then I'm not a Muslim.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-020_s22 | question_id: liberation -->

Liberation, to me, means doing things that make me happy, that make me comfortable. I don't really know where — I'm always liberated. I speak my mind; what I have inside comes out of my mouth. I speak my mind. I'm liberated.

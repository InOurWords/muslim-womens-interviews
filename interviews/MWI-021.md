# Interview MWI-021

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

Oh my goodness — an ordinary day in my life? There are several, I would say. I've had several stages in my life, because now I'm a very senior person, and what I've done as a routine has of course varied depending on my age. So where do you want me to start? Because if you say today, my routine today is very different from my routine fifty years ago.

Let me start from the beginning, and the beginning would be my beginning in Canada, since you're interested in the history of women who have lived in Canada. I'll start there — not that I think my life is interesting, but since you asked, I'm going to respond to the best of my knowledge. I came to Canada as what at the time was called a landed immigrant, so I came as an immigrant knowing that I would become Canadian, quote-unquote, after three years. I was here as a student. This is how I started. I had hopes of getting a PhD degree, and I came against my parents' wishes — they thought I'd be here just for a couple of months, and that it was lunacy to be alone in Canada. I wasn't married at the time, so they just let me go. I had saved enough money to pay for my ticket, and I had five hundred dollars in my pocket. That's how I started here. I worked at [UNIVERSITY 1] as a teaching assistant, and I also enrolled in courses for my degree, what I hoped would be a PhD degree — and this ensured that I was more or less financially independent.

I was twenty-eight years old at the time. I came to [CITY 1] because I knew I spoke French — I was educated in a French lycée — and I liked the fact that it was a bilingual city at the time; this was very attractive to me. I was kind of disappointed, because I thought [CITY 1] was a small city compared to where I came from. I came from Egypt, and [CITY 2] was celebrating two thousand years of history at that time, so [CITY 1] seemed quite provincial to me. But very soon I discovered how convenient it was to live in this city — the water pressure was excellent, the phone lines worked without any hindrance, and this is how I started.

Now, if you want to know about my faith: I grew up in a conservative Muslim family, and I had contacts here with the Muslim community. At that time it was basically more Asian, so Pakistani, and maybe not as many Arabs — sixty-forty percent. I can't give you exact numbers, but there was just one mosque in [CITY 1]. I used to go to the mosque, I enjoyed it, and this was my first contact with the Muslim environment here. It was a Muslim non-Arab environment, but nonetheless there was a Saturday school for kids that I participated in, and I really enjoyed it. I liked my life in [CITY 1] — I used to spend an inordinate amount of time going to the stores and seeing what there was available at the beauty counters. And politically, I thought there was more freedom, although I came here with the idea that I was fed up with politics, that I didn't want to know anything about the Arab-Israeli conflict, that I just wanted to be free and live my life. But in fact politics here took hold of me — I was interested in politics, and I did appreciate that you could be involved, that you could go out in demonstrations. Although in the first few years I wasn't politically active, I was more religiously active, because this was my main attachment to the community, to the Muslim community, through the mosque.

At that time, I must say, there was no marked Islamophobia as there is today — so we're talking about the mid-70s. People would see my brown skin and they would think, "oh, so you're Pakistani." That was the first reaction. I'd say, no, I'm not Pakistani, I come from the Middle East — but usually it was the same place for people, you know, there was no difference, and nobody would address me as Muslim or would be aware that we were so different. That was [CITY 1] in the mid-70s.

Now, things began to change after the Islamic Revolution in Iran. I can place it very distinctly, when Khomeini — the late Ayatollah Khomeini — came into power, the Islamic Revolution in '79. People now would start using the word "Ayatollah," for instance, and "Muslim revolution," and "Islam." And there was a very popular book called Not Without My Daughter, by Betty Mahmoody. That was the story of a mother who was married to an Iranian; she thought it was a repressive marriage, an unhappy marriage, and she wanted to leave Iran, but she had a daughter and her husband wouldn't allow her to leave. Betty Mahmoody wrote about her experience, which was an allegory for all the fantasies that Western women have of the Muslim world — that the husband is oppressive, and so on. Anyway, this was the novel, and it had great popularity. It was translated into French, and you would hear about it everywhere. There was also the fact of the American hostages who were taken in Tehran. So this played very vividly here in the media and in the subconscious of Quebecers, of the Canadians here. That was the first exposure, I would say, to the Muslim world, and to the beginning of Islamophobia.

Then in the 80s there were the Lebanese immigrants who came from Lebanon, from the civil war in Lebanon, so we had more Arabs here, we had more religious Islamic centers in Quebec — this was the second wave. In the 90s we had the immigrants coming from North Africa, in particular from Algeria. As the number of immigrants from Muslim countries increased, our problems also increased, because immigrants come and they settle here — they don't settle in faraway places, in the farms, where no one hears about them or sees them. They come here to [CITY 1]. This made a difference. We were seeing more halal restaurants, more young men coming either to study and then to stay. I mean, it was a good thing to see the community grow — we would hear more Arabic on the streets, more specialized grocery stores, restaurants. But at the same time, the international context was, how would you say, coloring the way local people here, local Québécois and local Canadians, were looking at us.

All this was before 9/11 — so the 90s and the turn of the century. Then, of course, there was 9/11, the war in Afghanistan, and everybody wanted to go and fight in Afghanistan to free the women, right? So the woman was a very central part of the Islamophobic context, because of the way they were depicted: they're submissive, they have no agency, they're repressed — in other words, they're covered, you don't see them, they're silent, so they have no role in life except to reproduce. That was the image that was circulated. And of course the Western world was going there to liberate them — this is a very good cause, you know, you can't argue with that.

Now, what was special about Quebec is that there's the French influence. There's influence from France, and the secularism law, what we call laïcité, has also been imported from France, and here it was taken up. With the number of Muslim immigrants doubling every decade — coming whether from North Africa or from the Middle East, and I include here Afghanistan, and Iraq, Turkey — this coincided with the rise of a nationalist view of what people should be, look like, dress, eat, think, speak. That was all taken up by the nationalist Parti Québécois as a banner, as what they thought would be defending the Quebec national values, what they call Québécois values. All this put together gives you an idea about the build-up of Islamophobic trends in Quebec, and especially — I'm speaking for [CITY 1], but this is true throughout Quebec — most of the immigrants, about eighty-nine percent of Muslim immigrants, are based in [CITY 1].

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s01 | question_id: belonging -->

Absolutely, I feel I belong. What helped me to integrate was that I married a man — my late husband was from Quebec, but he was from the English-speaking minority in Quebec. He was not French-speaking, he was English-speaking. But I felt integrated because I had in-laws who lived here, who lived in Quebec. I also worked in the English colleges, what they call CEGEPs here. I never felt like an outsider, because I was a hundred percent engaged in the community, and I never wanted to isolate myself. As I said, maybe being married to a Québécois did help me, but also I think of myself as an active member of society — when I was working, I was earning money, I was paying taxes, and I thought that politically I was very engaged as well, not only on the international level, more on the international level, but also on the Quebec provincial level, a little bit less on the municipal level, I do regret to say. I'm only becoming more involved now on the municipal level, with the problems that we have with the unhoused people, and garbage collection, and so on. I'm not happy with the services that the city is offering, but this came at a later stage.

You can't do everything — I mean, you can't be at the international level and the Quebec level and the municipal level. But maybe the municipal level is something that we as immigrants should get more interested in, because this is more immediate. From my experience as an immigrant, I'm always attached to my home country, so I follow what's happening over there. And although when I came I said, okay, I'm not going to live my life worrying about Israel and Egypt and Palestine and so on, I became increasingly involved in politics, and in Islamophobia. At the beginning, when you live normally, when nobody points at you and says that you're a Muslim, you don't pay as much attention to it — you don't have to defend it, you don't have to explain, you don't have to prove yourself, not as much, at least. But with increasing Islamophobia, yes, I think this is an added challenge. This is more of a challenge also for younger women who are in the workplace — we're very aware of that, especially now that we have laws that curtail what professions they can do, how they should be dressed, what conduct they should adopt in the public place. I'm very much aware of that. Now that I'm retired, I'm not employed, but I do have a business, I do have a rental business, so I'm still active, and I'm very much involved in the community, and I hope I'm doing something that would be of benefit to other Muslim women.

I don't think I deserve all this. I just have to be honest and say I lived my life. I was mentioning to my nephew that I haven't accomplished the things that I thought I would accomplish in Canada, and he said, do you live the life you wanted to live? So that is true, but there's nothing to gloat about.

## HOW DID FAITH SHOW UP IN A GIVEN DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s02 | question_id: followup__how_did_faith_show_up_in_a_given -->

What I found — and this was maybe in the 80s and in the 90s — was the difficulty, for instance, of praying. That is a big hurdle for Muslims, because you can't just say, well, I want to find a space, a private space, for praying. And then you have to justify it. People would say it's very well accepted to have breaks — a coffee break — but when you talk about a break for prayers, that is not so well accepted. And now there are laws that prevent schools from having a space for prayers for the young people.

Whereas when I first came to Canada, at the [UNIVERSITY 3] there was a chapel. There is still a chapel at [UNIVERSITY 2]. [UNIVERSITY 1], for instance — they refuse to apply this new law that says you cannot pray in a public space, because they have so many international students, and they are selling this as an advantage, that students have a place to pray, a musalla. So it's a room, and it's divided — the place for men, and next to it for women. That is [UNIVERSITY 1]. At [UNIVERSITY 1], at [UNIVERSITY 2], it was a yearly fight, from day one, from forever — I remember it was always difficult to have a space for prayers. There was a space given, allowed for Friday prayers, but not a permanent place. It was always a fight to get a place to pray. And now, with the new law, I'm not sure — I'm not sure if there is going to be a musalla, or even Friday prayers.

## HOW DID THE NEW LAW COME ABOUT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s03 | question_id: followup__how_did_the_new_law_come_about -->

I can tell you how the new law came about. This is part of the CAQ, the Coalition Avenir Québec, and that is invoking not neutrality but secularism, laïcité — that you cannot have prayers in public space. What triggered it was that during the demonstrations for Palestine, sometimes they coincided with the afternoon, because here in [CITY 1] in the winter it's almost Asr prayers — you pray noon at twelve, and then ten minutes after two p.m. it is Asr. So in some instances we had to pray standing in the street, just to have a space to pray. And there were also some instances where, at Eid for instance, families came into the park and prayed in the park.

This didn't go well with the members of the CAQ. So they introduced this new law that you cannot pray in a public place — but then they didn't want to put it so bluntly, so they nuanced it. They said, unless you obtain permission from the municipality and you don't hinder traffic. Which was already the case, because whenever we had demonstrations for Palestine, we did have to take permission from the police, and it was well announced, and the area was cordoned off, so there was no hindrance of traffic or any other activity. It was just that we were praying, and this was part of marking our presence, let's say, or our protest.

And so after that they did elaborate on it, and said, okay, a public place — well, what is a public place? A school. Of course schools are a public place, so you can't pray in a college, what they call here a CEGEP, and so on. There are a lot of ramifications to this law, which is Law 94, and this was an extension of another law. So they're just building it up, and going the way France is going, because in France those laws are en vigueur — in other words, they're circulated. So here we're copying France. This is the model for Quebec.

## DO THE OTHER UNIVERSITIES HAVE A CHAPEL OR A MUSALLA?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s04 | question_id: followup__do_the_other_universities_have_a_chapel_or -->

I would say musalla here, because there are four universities in [CITY 1] that I could talk about. So there is [UNIVERSITY 2] — and I think I did mention [UNIVERSITY 2]. [UNIVERSITY 1] has a specific place for prayers for Muslims, so a musalla. There is the [UNIVERSITY 4] — I don't know what the situation is there. Same thing for the [UNIVERSITY 3], I don't know. What I can tell you is that the students — and this I heard from somebody who is doing some research, a French intern, actually, who is doing research there — he told me that he and the other young men have to bring their prayer carpets with them, and they pray in a hidden place, but there is no central place for prayers.

No, no, they don't have a chapel. In fact, [UNIVERSITY 2] has a chapel, and they have a department of religious studies, so it's integrated there. And I can tell you that at the [UNIVERSITY 3] there was a chapel in the 80s — so a church, a chapel — and then they turned it into a morgue. They thought the space would be better used that way. Yes — for a morgue. That's what happened to it. Now, at [UNIVERSITY 4], I don't know what the situation is there, but for sure now they cannot have a musalla.

## IS THE LAW TARGETED AT MUSLIMS, OR APPLIED TO OTHER RELIGIONS TOO?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s05 | question_id: followup__is_the_law_targeted_at_muslims_or_applied -->

Absolutely. Well, the law doesn't say we are against Muslims. Muslims are the most affected by it. When we think of the increment in the Asian immigrant community, there is no debate about being a Buddhist, or about synagogues, for instance. It says "religious symbol," but it's not only the law, [INTERVIEWER], it's how the law is applied. And maybe we are more visible.

Although — the second group that is more impacted are the Sikhs. There was a big controversy about the kirpan, the small dagger, the ceremonial, symbolic dagger that Sikh men were wearing, and this was a big issue here at one point, in the schools, for instance. And lately, I just read recently, there was a Sikh man — and it's Sikh in English; I don't want to say a sick man, like someone who's ill. He's also an influencer, so he takes photos of himself and so on. He was working out at the gym, and he was wearing a long dagger — because the daggers that are allowed only have to be seven inches long, and his dagger was obviously longer than that; I've seen him in the photo — and he also had a turban. Apparently one of the employees came and told him, you have to leave. Why can't I stay in the gym? Because you have a weapon on you. And there was a big controversy — should he leave the gym, yes, no, why, and so on.

But these are still affecting a minority. What makes us more of a target is the numbers. Not that this should matter — I mean, one person is one person too much, that's discriminated against. But over and above the law, it's how people even would discriminate against Muslim women who are wearing the hijab, even if the law doesn't say you have to discriminate against them.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s06 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I see that there are more challenges, and this has actually encouraged me to learn more about Islam — when you're going to debate, when you're going to talk about your religion, you have to be informed. So I try to have solid arguments. I read, and I don't know whether the articles I've written are still on the website of [MUSLIM WOMEN'S ORGANIZATION 1].

What I write about now is very different from what my sister, who lives in Egypt, is interested in, in the kind of religious knowledge (she seeks). Here you have to convey more basic knowledge, and also to address the attacks and the concerns, so we would look at a different kind of source, a different kind of knowledge, if I may say. I'll give you an example. My sister is very much interested in how you read the Qur'an and the rules — what we call tajweed. I wouldn't have the luxury to just sit down and study those rules, but I would write about the history of Islam, misconceptions of Islam, because this is what I'm confronted with. Nobody will ask me how you read the Qur'an, or whether you read it correctly or not correctly. So there is a certain balance here. Although I know it's very important to read correctly, I would rely on listening to the Qur'an and reading it in the same way, rather than being knowledgeable about how to read the Qur'anic text — because you can't do everything. You have to specialize, specialize in the areas where you would be useful, useful in the sense of being accessible, acceptable, of explaining, in the sense of facilitating integration. That is the objective now, let's say, my primary interest, my contribution or participation. This is what is needed, or what I feel is needed, or what I feel I can do or contribute.

## HOW DO YOU SEEK THAT KNOWLEDGE, AND HAS THAT CHANGED WITH THE POLITICAL ENVIRONMENT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s07 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_seek_that_knowledge_and_has -->

Books. And yes, it has changed, because at the beginning there wasn't so much Islamophobia, not so many attacks directed so directly at the religion. I was also more on the political side rather than the religious side, because in the Middle East — I come from Egypt — during those fifty years it was always a kind of turmoil, whether it was repression, or seeking to support those who were repressed, political prisoners, whatever. It has been an ongoing upheaval, and that took precedence over Islamophobia. But since 9/11, I would say Islamophobia has become so marked. And we are in Quebec, where we receive blows from both sides, from south of the border and from France — so it's like somebody slapping you on both cheeks, one from the States and one from Europe. Not me particularly, but it's like we are in a sandwich.

## IS ANY OF IT DIRECTED AT MUSLIMS HERE, OR AT MUSLIMS IN GENERAL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s08 | question_id: followup__is_any_of_it_directed_at_muslims_here -->

In general, what's happening in the States is not kind to Muslims either. I get the emails of CAIR, C-A-I-R, and it's not — how do you say? I don't want to use the word pleasant. It's not reassuring, let's say. It's not reassuring.

However, the difference between Muslims in the States and Muslims here is that in the States fifty percent of them are natives. So the African American Muslims are aware, politically, socially, whether that comes with advantages or disadvantages. But here, most of the Muslim population is either first-generation immigrants — now we have a second generation — but we are not considered natives in the same sense as African Americans.

## WHAT DOES INTEGRATION MEAN TO YOU, AND WHAT DOES IT LOOK LIKE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s09 | question_id: followup__what_does_integration_mean_to_you_and_what -->

One word: integration means that you have access to the job market. This is the best way to get integrated. The immigrants who come here from Muslim countries are, of course, not seniors — they are at a stage of their lives where they are active members of society. And how do you become active, how do you become integrated? By having a place in the workforce. There is no other way, no better way to be integrated.

Well, it starts with economic integration. You can't just be — how are you going to be living? This is all you have. You have to have an income, you're not going to live on thin air, you have to have a means of living, and the economic side here is very important. I would say we are living in a society where your value is based on how much money you make. It's as simple as that.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DID IT DO FOR YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s10 | question_id: followup__how_did_you_come_to_wear_hijab_and -->

I don't wear a traditional hijab like the hijab you are wearing. My story is very different, because when I grew up — the 60s, the 70s, in Egypt — the women didn't wear it. It wasn't known as such. There was a national attire, but the word "hijab" didn't even exist in our vocabulary. It was more those who went out with a black wrap around their head — not a chador, we didn't have this.

There was also a divide in the society, where the upper class was imitating the Western world — very much influenced by colonialism. I myself went to a French school, so it was also about looking at France as the model: whether I wanted to dress like the French girls, act like them, speak like them. My whole environment was very much influenced by this colonial mentality.

That was one side of the family. What made a balance is that my father was very religious, and that he had studied in England, so he could see the colonialism, he could see the imperialism — he was more aware of it. He was in England for his studies and then came back — this was in the 40s. But Egypt had both English and French colonial influences. My mother sent me to a French school because this was thought to be an advantage — a young woman of my society should speak French, and it looked good when you sat among people in society to be able to speak French. It was a kind of refinement, if you want. Whereas boys would go more to an English school. My father, being an Arab nationalist, wanted his son to go to a national Arab school, so my older brother went to a national Arabic public school, and I and my sisters went to the lycée.

Going back to the question of hijab: the question of identity, of Islamic identity, came into play after Egypt was defeated by Israel in the 1967 war. That was the beginning of the call for more Arab Islamic values. And this is how it started — a prominent family from high society started wearing the hijab, and then it filtered down to the society, even up to the 80s. Since I was here, the hijab was not so dominant. I started wearing a kind of hijab, covering my head, after 9/11.

I did it for two reasons. For me, there were always norms of decency that were enforced. For instance, my sisters and I would never wear miniskirts, although in the 60s that was the fashion, the rage — the miniskirts. So we wouldn't wear miniskirts, we wouldn't wear sleeveless blouses. There were terms of decency that were, let's say, unwritten — we would know what would be considered osé, or not acceptable — but the hijab was not part of it. So this was introduced gradually in Egypt and became the norm, and it spread because it became — how do you say — fashionable; it became not acceptable but high class to wear the hijab, you see. So it's a religious component doubled with a societal component — it was a kind of distinction to wear the hijab.

The hijab, what we call the Egyptian hijab, which is basically what you are wearing — there is some historical precedence, in that at the time when Egypt was under the Turkish influence, and was actually a Turkish wilayat, part of the Turkish empire, then men and women used to wear the niqab, cover their face. But this again was a kind of distinction. This was the upper society: you wouldn't reveal yourself, or show your face, to somebody who's not from the immediate family. It was something for the upper class. It was not for, let's say, the farmers, the Egyptians who were working the fields, or in some other menial jobs — it was for the very upper crust. So that has a certain resonance. And so now the niqab is quite different, but it is not popular; the niqab comes from a Saudi influence. In Egypt at the time, for instance, my great-grandmother used to wear what would be called a niqab today, but she only wore it when she would meet men from outside the family, or go outside the house — for instance, her driver or her cook would never see her face. She always had this kind of makeup (underneath). So that idea of supremacy, or upper class, I would say, played a role in the adoption of the hijab in Egypt. For myself, it was more in solidarity with those who wear the hijab, after 9/11. I think it's quite complicated.

## WAS THERE MORE ISLAMOPHOBIA AFTER 9/11, AND DID PEOPLE TREAT YOU DIFFERENTLY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s11 | question_id: followup__was_there_more_islamophobia_after_9_11_and -->

Absolutely. Nobody attacks me. I rightly think that it's out of modesty, perhaps, that I'm covering my head. I don't look typical — I dress modestly, but I dress off the rack here. I buy my clothes mostly here from Winners; I don't know if you have Winners and others. And so I'm a Muslim, but perhaps they don't think that I'm a very conservative Muslim — or I'm a Muslim that passes. And when I walk in the street, I pass. Now, in the job market, it's a different story. But there isn't as much animosity (toward me) as toward somebody who is wearing a full hijab, whom they perceive as wanting to impose themselves as Muslim. You see the difference?

It's not that I'm not at ease — no, I'm totally at ease about being identified as a Muslim. I even had some cases where a woman would come and ask me, "so are you a Muslim, or a Jew?" And I would say, no, I am a Muslim, that's quite clear. But people here think, or have an idea, that Muslims who wear the hijab do it because they want to impose (their faith), or they want others to convert. They think it's more aggressive, rather than out of faith or out of conviction. So that is the perception. And so I kind of pass — especially because I speak French, and I speak good French. And I'm old also, because older women in France, at a certain time — I'm not saying now — would be more or less dressed like me.

No, I didn't notice a change — not with the way I dress. And also because I was not on the job market at the time — I was self-employed, I had my own business. So it was not like somebody who is entering the job market, or even studying to become a teacher, wearing the hijab.

## WHAT DOES THE LARGER PUBLIC IN QUEBEC GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s12 | question_id: followup__what_does_the_larger_public_in_quebec_get -->

Absolutely, (there is something they get wrong). There is a fear. Quebec has not had a good experience with the (Catholic) church, and that gets projected onto Muslims. They wanted to liberate themselves from the grasp of the church, and that is what they perceive as real liberation. So when they see Muslim women so attached to their religion, they say, you are bringing us back to the dark times, when the church had all the power over women — they were against the pill, for instance, they wanted women to have as many children as possible, and they also had a monopoly on the economy, on society, and on the education system. Once Quebecers got out of the shackles of the church, they don't want to see any going back, any being respectful or mindful of religion. It brings back bad memories, and they say, well, we don't want any religion in our life, and we don't want to be with people who seem backward, in a sense, or not liberated. So this is the problem here.

I should get statistics on that before I speak, but originally, yes, they are Catholics — mainly Catholics, the French Québécois majority. Now, with the statistics — and we can look the statistics up — you can also identify as a non-believer, as an atheist, so I don't know what the proportion is. There's also the question of being baptized: traditionally, officially, you're Catholic, but then you don't believe and you don't practice, so you're a non-Catholic (Catholic). The relationship with Catholicism here is rather, you know, not a complex one.

Yes, but even during the time of what they call the Révolution tranquille — which would translate, I would think, as not soft transition; I don't have the exact translation, but a revolution that isn't a fiery revolution — the nuns opted to wear secular clothes, not to be dressed as nuns, and to wear a small cross instead of a big cross. So that was part of the evolution, if we don't want to use the term revolution.

Yes — there was discrimination when I was applying for a job, for sure. I did feel that there was discrimination. But you know, Madame, I think at that time I brushed it off. I didn't want to dwell on it. Whereas now I see it more clearly when it happens to others.

## FEEDBACK ON THE STUDY'S QUESTIONS

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s14 | question_id: followup__feedback_on_the_study_s_questions -->

We should identify the sample. So, for instance, we should say this is geared to Muslim women, Muslims who identify themselves as Muslim, and also to women who wear the hijab — that's another aspect. Then the age group. I'm very sensitive to age now, with a mirror, since I'm a senior, so I can see that in the different phases of my life I had a different outlook on things, and the way you ask some of the questions, I think, is time related. The usual age groups, I think. And if you want to add whether you're a student, or a housewife, or a mother — how you identify yourself socially — because some of the questions are very socially oriented, I would say. Everything is projected with the idea that one day we're going to have to answer for our thoughts, for our acts, for how we're spending our money, how we're disposing of our knowledge. ## WHEN SOMETHING IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL, HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND IT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s39 | question_id: followup__when_something_is_beyond_your_control_how_do -->

This again is a philosophical question, [INTERVIEWER] — what do you want me to say? We Muslims believe that our life is predetermined; I don't want to say only a certain part of our life. That this was our nasib — is this a word familiar to you? Very much so in our Arab Muslim culture: if things happen to you, then inshallah, mā shā'a qadara, which means whatever God has decided for you. In a sense that helps you to accept what is beyond you, knowing that you're not going to be able to change it, but that you have to live with it, that this was what was prescribed — your destiny.

I even think that in Egypt especially — I'm talking about Egypt because this is what I know — people tend to be resigned to their fate, to what happens to them. So I wish we were more militant, I wish we would be more questioning of what's happening. That is my take on the question. Take patience, for instance: there's a fine line between being submissive and reacting, being active. So I don't think that as Muslims we have to bend at every turn, but there's certainly a part of it where we have to accept that you can't change everything — not everything is within our control.

## WHAT DO YOU THINK ABOUT THE NEW LAW ABOUT PRAYING OUTSIDE?

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT SIT WITH YOUR OWN LIFE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s42 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

Laïcité, in the strict sense of the word, is that the state, l'état, has to be neutral in terms of religion — not favoring one religion over the other. For instance, in the so-called Muslim countries, because they're not Muslim any more — again, I'm referring to Egypt — on the Islamic occasions the head of state would be filmed going to a mosque and praying with the others. That here wouldn't be acceptable, because there the government is not secular, whereas in a secular government, the government should not have a religion that the people in the government are following. We agree with that entirely. But it doesn't mean that it should strip citizens of their identity, which is what is aimed at here by those secularism laws — they don't want to see that you are a Muslim in a special place, in what they call places of authority, and they keep on expanding what those positions are.

## WHEN YOU SAY PLACES OF AUTHORITY, DO YOU MEAN TEACHERS IN PUBLIC SCHOOLS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s43 | question_id: followup__when_you_say_places_of_authority_do_you -->

Yes, yes. First, with Bill 21, they had designated specific people — teachers, judges, prison guards, and what else? There was a fourth category also, where they would say those are women. They didn't say women, so it would be women and men in places of authority — in effect, where you would have influence over others. But with the new law, which is Law 94, they expanded this. So now it's not only teachers who are wearing a visible religious symbol — they wouldn't say hijab, they would say religious symbol, as if it were not aimed at Muslims. Then they said anyone: a cook, for instance, in a cafeteria, wearing a hijab, would not be acceptable; a cleaning lady in a school. So essentially anybody working in the school, not only teachers, and whether they have direct contact with the kids or not. In a public school, you cannot have this.

It also included the garderies, the daycare centers, where in [CITY 1] a majority of the caretakers are Muslim women. At first they were not covered by Bill 21, but now they are covered — although I think there's a clause that says if you're already in the position, you wouldn't be banned from the position, but you cannot as a new entry be qualified to be in a garderie. So this is actually an extension of the law.

## WHAT HAVE WE GAINED FROM THE WEST?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-021_s50 | question_id: followup__what_have_we_gained_from_the_west -->

I wonder if you would be interested also in knowing what we have gained from the West. We talked at length about our concerns, but what makes us stay in the West, if it is a choice? We're not compelled to stay here, by and large — we're not refugees. Speaking about myself, I came here willingly, knowing to a certain extent what my life would be.

What have we gained here from the West? First of all, physical fitness. For women especially, there are more venues, opportunities and encouragement to be physically active here and to look after my health than I had back home — whether in terms of physical installations, or in how society looks at someone who wants to be physically active. Even beauty standards: in some poor countries, or even not so poor countries, a woman who is well, who is round — who has round forms, let's say — is deemed more attractive than somebody who is athletic and wiry. And this, of course, stems from the fact that somebody who is well-nourished is a sign of wealth — you're not skinny.

So that is one thing I gained from the West. Another thing is that I have the opportunity to voice my political opinions, which was very vital for me, very important. Back home, no, you can't do that — not fifty years ago, and unfortunately not up till today. So I feel that people are living in a cage; even if it's a golden cage, they can't go outside certain limits. And here in the West there is a price to it — let's not gloss over it and say it's something that you can do every day in every situation. No, there is a price to it, but still you have choices.

I also find that in the West there is more awareness about gender equality, which did not necessarily prevail where I came from. I find there is more awareness of the rights of women in the family, whereas in Egypt it was more — it depends. Men perhaps want to be seen as the deciders, although it may not be the case, but they wanted to be seen as such. So there are nuances, of course. Let's say that my place as a woman in a Western society pleases me, or is more in agreement with what I want to be.

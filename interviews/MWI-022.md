# Interview MWI-022

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

An ordinary day in my life: I wake up early, before dawn, to do my prayer, then go back to sleep. I wake up again when my alarm goes off at seven, have some breakfast, and sit on my balcony to look at my little flower garden, which is beautiful, and get some sunlight. Then, depending on what the plan for the day is, I usually do some writing or some kind of creative project, because that's what I enjoy doing with my time. I'm in my mid-sixties now and I live alone, so I have more free time than I've ever had, and that's wonderful. I make my midday prayer. Sometimes I go into my part-time job at [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE], three days a week. Other times I may go visit family members or other members of the community, or I might go shopping — I don't do that very much because I'm opposed to consumer culture. Cooking, chatting, following politics, watching streaming shows on my computer, reading books — I regularly download books. At the moment I'm doing some research on Fatimah al-Zahra, and that is fascinating, absolutely fascinating. I'm not quite sure what I'm going to do with it yet, but maybe I can work it into some kind of piece.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

Myself and my community — okay, I am a Western-educated Muslim convert. I came in through Islamic Sufism. I've written about my experience. I met a Sufi sheikh in Switzerland many years ago, and I converted to Islam a year later — actually in Turkish Cyprus, which wasn't part of Europe at that point — and then I married and came to Canada from Europe.

Somebody who's never met a Muslim: well, the converts from the West who went into Islam are a pretty unique demographic. I don't know if you've ever met any of them. It sort of started before then, but it became more significant in the 1960s and 70s. I converted in the mid-80s, by which point there were — I wouldn't say quite a few of them, but a significant number. Interestingly enough, many of them were from secular North American Jewish backgrounds. The man I married had converted ten years previously in [CITY 5] — it was the hand of Allah, the will of Allah. So I'm part of that community, and I do have friends who are raised Muslim — you found me through [MUSLIM WOMEN'S ORGANIZATION 1], the majority of whom are born Muslim and immigrants. So my background is a little bit different, and one of the reasons I wear hijab — I don't wear it all the time — is as a cultural signifier, because nobody, including the Muslims, believes that I'm actually a Muslim, because of my unusual cultural background. So I wear it to kind of prove that I am a Muslim. Well, because I'm white, because of the way I speak, because I was raised in England, and people don't realize that now the only people with faith in England are the Muslims. They're becoming a bit more aware of it, but the stereotype of British people is different to what I am.

I see that as a very positive thing. When I lived in England, in the mid-80s, the first wave of large-scale Muslim immigration was establishing itself, and there were the beginnings of communities in parts of the north of England where the industrial base had collapsed. I thought it was wonderful. I wasn't Muslim at that point, but I lived on a street in [CITY 3] that was full of Muslims, and the culture for women there was that they didn't really communicate with people outside their community, but we had some very pleasant interactions over the years — some lovely acts of kindness shown to me by Muslims. But I never thought that Islam was something that was viable for me, partly because of the hijab, also because of the cultural closeness at that point. I think in that first wave, Muslims coming over deliberately chose not to integrate with mainstream society because they were fearful or they didn't like it, so there wasn't much cultural exchange or dialogue, as they call it now. But that's changed radically.

Interestingly enough, I had an uncle — well, first of all, my father, before he was married, lived in [CITY 4], Iraq. He worked as a doctor there and was very fond of the people he met and his time there, and he talked about it in a very positive way — that was when I was a child growing up in England. My uncle, because they were part of the British colonialist thing (I have to deal with that too), came over from Malaysia, where he was working, and brought someone who worked for him to get treatment because this person had tuberculosis. He stayed with us, and that was the first Muslim I ever met, and the first person of color I ever interacted with. I was seven years old and had never interacted with somebody who had such a different life experience. That was wonderful, because I thought this person was absolutely amazing — stunningly good-looking, extremely kind, and with such wonderful manners. There was always this emphasis put on us growing up that we should have good manners, but this person's manners were next level, they were really good. So that was very positive and inspiring, and I never really bought into the anti-immigrant rhetoric that was around, because it was clear to me that the people coming to my country were better raised and educated.

In [CITY 1]? Well, I've grown up with the community, I raised my children with them, I'm very aware of what's going on with them. My most positive link with a community at this point is with the Turkish mosque. There is a very old friend who's Turkish, she's in her 90s now, and (her husband has) always maintained [MASJID 1] as a very safe space. He's linked with the Unitarian Church now, and it's become a kind of repository for immigrants — he's got all this stuff stored all over the place, and most of the born Muslims really don't like it, because it's a bit like going into a recycling store — in the masjid, well, the basement of the masjid, but also in the masjid itself. At one point he had all these kids' toys, and then the people praying there objected. Anyway, that is a place where I enjoy my interactions. That's where I go for Eid prayers and things.

Well, it's very varied. Now it's majority Francophone, because those are the people who are coming in. The group running that particular mosque at the moment is a little bit marginal in the Muslim community because they differ about the direction of the Qibla — they're called the Habashis, because they're related to a certain shaykh, and it's to do with the way they calculate it. It's towards the Kaaba, it's not towards Jerusalem or anything, but they calculate the direction of the Kaaba in [CITY 1] differently than everybody else does. Allahu a'lam. Yes, they are Sunni. Honestly, there's just all kinds of variety, it's incredible — practically every cultural community now has its own mosque. I've been in Canada since the mid-80s, so I've seen a lot of changes, and over the years I've negotiated and adapted to nearly all the communities — there are some exceptions. So I've become agile at what they call — I don't know, they have words for it now, like switching — anyway, I've gotten used to that, and I go into these contexts and just do my best to make alliances based on my understanding of deen. I was lucky enough to go on hajj, and one of the things I marveled at was the cross-section of humanity that you would not find anywhere else. It was wonderful, wonderful.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

Oh, yeah, that's interesting. When I first became Muslim, I was sort of trying to be Turkish, because my shaykh was Turkish, and his wife and his daughters introduced me and taught me about Islam — that's Hanafi Sunni. So when I came in and was newly married, I was forced to internalize that when I was confronted with the Muslims at that time, because their interpretation of Islam was very influenced by the Salafi mindset, and it was very rigid, I would say. Within Sufism we're more flexible, let's put it like that — we try not to judge, we try to see everybody as a person of God. So I had this sort of Turkish persona almost. I would say over the years I've internalized my faith more and more and linked it with the person I was born and raised to be — there was this discrepancy between the child who grew up in [CITY DISTRICT 1] and was raised in a certain cultural background, and the person who converted to Islam, got married, and became a sort of trad wife before that term existed, then later got divorced and had to start her own business and survive. So yes, it's definitely evolved, and it's become internalized, and it's actually become stronger. Several years ago, I met my sister-in-law on a visit to the UK, and she said, "Oh, so you've stopped the Muslim thing," and I said, "No, the Muslim thing has become even stronger." Because it's coming from in here — it's coming from my reading of the Quran, my prayers, my interaction with teaching and podcasts and all the wonderful resources that are out there now for us.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

Well, I'm a hidden Muslim. Publicly, I do not wear hijab. I do not want to go around with a target on my back. That started around 2010, when I had to go out and get a job. I had to interview for a job, because I knew perfectly well that nobody was going to hire me as a converted Muslim. The Muslim businesses themselves were not going to hire me because I wasn't part of their cultural community, and it was problematic — plus they had their wives already working there for free, so why would they? And, well, we're under national socialism now — the situation for Muslims in Quebec is the worst it's ever been. It's always been bad, but now there's so much prejudice, a lot of open, active hostility towards Muslims. The incidents of racism — Muslim women being attacked on the metro, having horrible things said to them, treated with prejudice and disrespect constantly. When I was married and wore hijab, I did experience some of that, but to a certain extent I was protected from it, because I was married and in a secure economic position. It would happen when I was driving — there would be these big black trucks behind me, kind of menacing me. That doesn't happen to me now that I'm not wearing it. It's bad, and people are not embarrassed or inhibited about doing it. In [CITY 2] it's way better — not so much [PROVINCE 3], there are far fewer visible Muslims, but in [PROVINCE 2], in [CITY 2], it's fine to wear hijab and be Muslim. Not in Quebec.

I didn't have to remove it, I chose to remove it, and now I use it as a cultural identifier — so when I go into what I'd describe as a Muslim context, I wear hijab, but when I'm in a secular context, I don't. Now I work at [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE], which is an interesting example, because it's one of the very few organizations that will not discriminate against women wearing hijab — there are hijabi women working at the federal government and at [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE]. However, I've noticed that many of them have swapped the hijab for a beanie or a hoodie. In the winter I do that too, because it's cold in the warehouse — I cover my head in various ways. A traditional hijab and an abaya, I'd wear to Eid prayer, but I certainly wouldn't wear it to the grocery store.

Prejudice and hostility — that's what was in my environment, and it's everywhere. You can have this thing about educating people about Islam, but honestly, look at Muslim men — most of the time, apart from their skin color, you can't tell that they're Muslim men. So why should I go around with a target on my back or on my head? I read a fatwa that said originally hijab was to protect women, and I agree with it — it's become the other way around, especially in Quebec. Wearing hijab is not protecting you, it's opening you up to hostility and Islamophobia and anti-immigrant, racist rhetoric.

It's gotten way worse, the situation has really deteriorated, and unfortunately the CAQ government, which is in power at the moment, has perpetuated and encouraged this. It started with 9/11. Shortly after, my husband, who is very intentional about appearing as a Muslim male — he always wears a hat and these kinds of outfits from his Turkish tailor, he looks outrageous — people used to call him Osama. We were traveling to Jordan and we were at Schiphol Airport, and a woman got on the lift and said, "Are you crazy? Are you out of your mind? What are you doing going around dressed like this? Do you realize how much they hate us?" I've never forgotten that. That was at Schiphol, in Europe, shortly after 9/11. But then it started in Canada too — everyone was like, "Who are these people? What's going on, they're terrorists, they're trying to take over, they're trying to blow us up." And unfortunately this kind of garbage has continued.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s04 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

I consider it one of my pillars of faith to listen to and watch and read Quran, listen to tafsir, learn — anything I can. I see my faith like a part of my body, if you like. I have to nourish it with good food, I have to make sure it gets exercise and water, that it flourishes and grows, because honestly, if you don't feed it, it will wither. I think it's true — I think we all have to do that.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s05 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Honestly, at this stage in my life, I don't expect people to understand. I don't place the burden on them of understanding who I am and my journey — that's one of the reasons I wrote this book, just to document it. My daughters read it and they were like, "Oh wow, we never realized," and I'm like, well, why would you? It's been a very long journey, and I'm old now — like I said, I'm in my sixties, so a lot has happened, a lot has gone on, and it continues.

## HOW DO WE CREATE CHANGE OR BETTER COEXISTENCE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s06 | question_id: followup__how_do_we_create_change_or_better_coexistence -->

Well, I think the secret lies in our humanity, in our hearts and our intentions. If our intention in our interactions with others is to nurture the faith of that person and meet their heart to the best extent that we can, then I think we're going to be fine. But if our intention is to try to force some kind of ideology on them, or to force a language or a way of life on other people, which is the direction of the CAQ, then there's going to be trouble — Islamophobia, racism, and so on. When people have positive interactions with Muslims, they think of Muslims in a positive way. You've got your typical Canadian who lives in [PROVINCE 2] in a suburban house — he has a Muslim neighbor, the kids play together, the wife brings over sweets, they talk over the fence, their reactions are very positive, people are living peacefully. When that person hears "they're trying to take over society," he's going to think, well, Zaid's okay, I know Zaid, he's fine — or the guy who works in the office, Abdulrahman, he's a good guy, he stayed late and helped me with a project, he always says hi to me in the lift and asks about my family. They've been inoculated against this. The problem is with people who have no experience of Muslims, so they can buy into the idea that "they're a threat, they're here to steal jobs" — as if they're here to speak English. Most of the Muslims are from North Africa, that's why they're here — if they came here speaking English, they'd be out of here as quickly as they could. What else is the sentiment? Well, "they're foreign, they have different values, they're not like us, they're primitive" — it's coming directly from France, what you hear in France is what you hear here — "they have to integrate, they have to be part of our society, our culture" — which leads me to ask, well, what is your culture? I could get nasty, but I'll try not to. What is this so-called culture that the Muslims are supposedly trying to take away from you? Think about it.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s07 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

As a revert — as a convert, I prefer to call myself a convert rather than a revert, because a revert sounds to me like a diagnosis — a big problem for me is the way I get treated by the mainstream community when I go to a mosque or a community gathering. There is often prejudice, condescension. I'm an old woman, but many Muslim males will not interact with me at all, out of what they think is politeness. They seriously believe that pretending I don't exist is a polite way of dealing with the problem of someone like me in their prayer space, in their club. So yeah, that's a difficulty.

A typical example: I go to a mosque, I'm told I can't go in — I'm shooed out of the main entrance, "get out of here, go around the back." So I go around to the women's space, which is filled with sad women in difficult situations, many children crying and upset because there's nothing to entertain them and their needs aren't being met, and then there's a giant TV screen on the wall broadcasting the imam talking non-stop, usually in another language — sometimes the language of the cultural community of that mosque, frequently not even that language. I don't know quite what that's serving, but it has happened. I wouldn't say frequently, but when it does, I find it disturbing, disruptive to my faith — it hurts my heart. Sometimes I just leave, it's my choice to be there. Sometimes I make a complaint, sometimes accommodations are made because I'm making a fuss, but just the fact that I have to do that — I make alliances with other women in the same situation, and we push and petition together.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s08 | question_id: expectations -->

I think there's both. Generally, Muslim women put far too much pressure on themselves — I'm not surprised there's this rate of depression, mental illness, and anxiety among Muslim women, I've witnessed that many times, and it's systemic, tied to the isolation and the kind of family structures they're expected to maintain. For some convert women, there are pockets of communities where women who were raised in the West, in [PROVINCE 2] for instance, are expected — and put it upon themselves — to wear niqab and homeschool their children, meaning they're responsible for the education of five or six children all day, every day, in addition to living life in the West and supporting their husbands. I don't think it's surprising that a lot of them struggle. Anyone would. They put far too much pressure on themselves. That's also true of working Western women generally — the plight of working women with children is far too much pressure whether you're Muslim or not, because you don't have your mother and your aunts or your sisters there to help you the way you would in a traditional context. If you're leaving the house every day, in a traditional context there's somebody there for when the school calls because your kid has thrown up in the classroom — for these women there isn't anyone, so it's on them. I think they could really lighten up, because Islam is in your heart, Islam is in your journey, Islam is in your salah and your relationship with Allah. This is your Islam. Your Islam is not in what you're wearing, how you're living, or where you're living — you should be able to practice it anywhere.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s09 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

Well, now there is a law that we're not allowed to pray in parks — it's kind of treated like taking drugs, not allowed in public places. At [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE], when I first started working there, there used to be a prayer room, but because it's Quebec, they closed it and used the pandemic as the excuse. So they just locked it. Now when Asr comes in while I'm at [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE], I go into the lunchroom, and there's a group of Maghrebi brothers who sort of acknowledge that I'm Muslim and sort of don't — and I definitely don't (make a point of it either). It is what it is, it's fine, they don't have to understand me, that's okay — Allah understands me, I understand me, it doesn't matter. There's a chair in the back corner of the room that's facing the Qibla — that's the prayer chair. I sit in it, wrap something around my head, and do my prayers. If someone else comes in while I'm sitting there doing my salat, the Western people just kind of stare at me, but the Muslim brothers politely ignore me, and that's that.

## LEARNING TO SPEAK FRENCH IN QUEBEC

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s13 | question_id: followup__learning_to_speak_french_in_quebec -->

In England we all learned French at school, and it was always my worst subject — possibly I was even worse at Latin, but that's a close call, since they let me stop doing Latin within a year. I had to take what we call the O-level three times; I think in the end they gave it to me because they felt sorry for me and I kept trying. So I'm not good at languages. But since I've been in Quebec, especially at [NATIONAL POSTAL SERVICE], I have a lot of fun with the Quebec people who work there — not so much the women, who usually hate me, but the men, and we have this banter. I've learned to speak what they call joual — there's this whole prejudice against Quebec French, but what they speak is a dialect that's actually much closer to medieval French than the French spoken today. I've learned to speak that particular dialect, and I have that accent, and I'm very proud of it, because when I speak to them, they look at me, they know I'm an English immigrant, and then I speak with this joual accent and deliberately use these little aphorisms and phrases — they think it's hilarious, they love it, and I love it too, because it's such fun.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s14 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

No. When I first came here, my husband — third generation Quebecer — and I were talking to a very nice, very respectful Quebec man, and I said, well, my husband's a Quebecer, [HUSBAND'S NAME]'s a Quebecer, because he was born here and his mother was born here, and he said, "mais il n'est pas Québécois de souche" — which means, essentially, that he's not "pure wool." There was this phrase, which they seem to have dropped now, fortunately, that if you're not one of the people who came over back in the 18th century, then you're not really a Quebecer. No — I joke with other immigrants, I say, well, we're Quebecers, and we both laugh. I've been here since 1986. I think of myself as Canadian too — I have a Canadian passport, and I say I'm more Canadian than I am British because I've been here longer. It's true, I am much more Canadian, but the British thing still comes through when people hear me talk. And no, the accent doesn't show up in my French, because I've got this joual thing going.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s15 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

I really like the traditional Catholic identity of Quebec, because it promoted belief in God in the daily life of people, so I appreciate that. I live just outside [CITY 1], in a place named after the Virgin Mary — that's Hazrat Maryam. I like that. Although the Muslim community has this thing where they say, oh, Christians believe in the Trinity, so they're wrong — I've made alliances with Christians as people of faith, very positive alliances, and we build each other up, because the darkness of secularism in Quebec is affecting everyone who believes, not just the Muslims. The Muslims are a visible target, but it's affecting the Sikhs, some of the Jews, the Christians too. Now their churches have to pay taxes, so they have to fundraise to support their buildings, and their buildings are all going to be demolished — I recently read an editorial in [PROVINCIAL NEWSPAPER 1] saying, well, wouldn't it be so much better if we could just use it all as public housing?

We have to support each other, because Christians are people of the book, despite what you might think about the way the Catholic Church has behaved over the years — they're people of the book, and the people of the book are the believers, and we treat them differently. To me, the problem isn't so-called polytheists or shirk, it's not the Hindus — the problem is consumer mentality, secularism, and liberalism, especially when it's aligned with communism or socialism. This government describes itself as national socialist, and that is the problem.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s16 | question_id: akhirah -->

Okay, well, I'm answerable. I have this understanding I received many years ago from a wonderful sheikh — not Sheikh Nazim, but another sheikh I studied with — who said you really don't have to worry about judging the actions of others. It's good to say something is wrong, it's good to speak truth to power, but you don't have to get into a terrible state about things that are happening, because everyone is going to be judged and Allah will deal with them. So one of the things I say to myself is: it's okay, Allah will fix them. I find that thought comforting, and I also believe it's true — nobody gets away with anything. People can make billions on cryptocurrency, oppressing others, supplying them with arms, this and that — but they're not going to get away with it. So you better be responsible about the way you behave, responsible for every interaction and every intention you have, because your intention shapes your actions and your actions shape who you are. If you wish to be a Muslim, you have to align your intentions and your actions to aspire to the character the teachings of Islam would want you to be.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s17 | question_id: qadr_control -->

Again, it's in the will of Allah, and it's not something that as an individual I can control, not something I have a voice in — my saying "this is terrible" isn't going to be acknowledged by governments in power. I don't believe in activism, honestly. When I was a teenager, I used to go to protests about things I didn't really know that much about, and I was just being manipulated. I actually see quite a bit of manipulation going on in the Muslim community too, by various political forces, and that concerns me. Allah is in control — it's the will of Allah, and I have a responsibility, as a servant of Allah, to bring about the best outcome I can within my limited capacity.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-022_s18 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

What matters to me most is that they have a vital and living relationship with Allah. Because I believe if they have that, then Allah will be in their hearts, and if Allah is in their hearts, their deen will follow. When I was raising my daughters, I was perhaps too liberal, and they had friends whose parents were extremely strict about how they were raised — today those girls have basically abandoned eighty percent of their parents' belief. Allah alim, I don't know about their prayer life, but they're one- or two-pillar Muslims — if you ask them, they'll say, yeah, I believe in Allah and the oneness of Allah, and I don't eat ham, but that's about it. I would aspire to something more than that — an interior experience of connection with the Creator of everything.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

Liberation — well, I guess when you die, inshallah, your spirit will be liberated from your existence in dunya, with all the struggles of dunya and all the suffering and pain and tribulation you undergo, so you'll be liberated then, inshallah.

# Interview MWI-023

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

I wake up — it depends if I have school or work or not — but I wake up, get ready for the day, and then if I have work, I go to work, if I have school, I go to school. If I have plans with my friends, I go to that. I can't really remember, because it's summer, so all my days look different, I don't really have much of a routine, but yeah, I go out, come back home.

It shows up in everything I do. Depending on your intention, anything can be an act of worship, so I think faith is everywhere in my day, but tangibly, I wear hijab. So when I get ready, I have this in mind, this act of worship — when I go out, it's part of me and my identity and how I show up in the world. I make sure to do my five prayers during the day, whether I'm inside or outside, so when I go out I'll find a place to pray, look for a mosque, or make sure that wherever I go there is a place to pray nearby. I'm studying Islamophobia mainly, I have a Muslim lens on my research. I'm also very involved in my community, so it does come out. I also have primarily Muslim friends, or even the friends I have who aren't Muslim — we have some talks about faith. So I think it shows up a lot.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I think if I were to describe myself, I wouldn't necessarily start with my faith, although it is something people would see first because I wear hijab — I would start with the basics: we believe there's only one God, and the last messenger is Muhammad, peace be upon him. That actually came up a lot — I went on vacation in Costa Rica, and I was a lot of people's first Muslim woman, so I've done this quite a bit over the summer. They would ask me a lot of questions about hijab and why I wear it, and I'd tell them it's because I'm a Muslim woman, and they'd ask the basic questions — can you take it off, when can you take it off, who can see your hair, why do you dress like this? I answer these because I think if people encounter a woman in hijab and don't know a lot about Islam, they start asking questions, because there are a lot of misconceptions, and it's nice when they ask respectfully — every single one of my interactions has been respectful, alhamdulillah. They usually ask why I wear the hijab, and I tell them I believe in one God and the last prophet. I also make sure people can make links, so I talk about the differences between Christianity and Islam, because a lot of people already know a lot about Christianity, and making those links makes Islam seem less like the "crazy religion" it's made out to be in the media. I make those links to help humanize us, because we're really dehumanized in the media.

The community I'm involved in, alhamdulillah, is very good-willed. I admit I've also been a little bit swayed by media representations of Muslims — not by the West, but by Muslims themselves, the way they represent themselves on social media, a lot of the extreme viewpoints that appear there. So when I got involved in the community I came in with some doubt, but it's honestly been such a good community. I'm especially involved with women, because that's what my research is about, and I've always been a feminist — even before, I involved myself with non-Muslim feminist groups, and now I involve myself with groups that help women's rights. It's very respectful, we try to build a community, really rely on one another. Fun-loving at times, especially the younger part of the community — the older people too, but because I'm younger I can enjoy the more fun parts of it. Very educational. I had this image that there would be a lot of misogyny, and that has not appeared — in fact, people have advocated for women in many ways within the community, which is really different from the way we are perceived in the media, whether it's how we're portrayed or how we portray ourselves.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I was born and raised where I live — always in [CITY 1]. Alhamdulillah, I don't think I've had some sort of identity crisis, I think I've had a very easy life when it comes to my faith. When I was very young, the political climate made people very Islamophobic, and I watched my mother struggle with that. That's kind of shaped who I am as a person — not necessarily my faith, but my sense of justice, and a lot of what I do now in my master's has been shaped by these early experiences. Other than that, until I got involved in the Muslim community, we weren't really involved apart from Quran school and things like that, so I don't think I really had an identity crisis when it came to my faith, because when I was young I didn't really think about it — I just knew I was Muslim, I knew what I was taught in Quran classes, I believed in one God, and I didn't bother with much more than that. In high school, I went to a school with a lot of my community, so I never really had any struggle with Islam, because a large majority of the students were Muslim — I didn't feel any of the Islamophobia. I started to really look into Islam when I was a little older, around eighteen, nineteen, and that's where I started my journey with Islam — learning about it through social media discourse at first, which was really detrimental to my faith, so I stopped, because I thought, if God is the most merciful, why are these people saying things about God that aren't merciful, that aren't just? So I started to look into Islam by myself, with the Quran — I started to really focus on my prayers, started wearing hijab, and that also came at a time when I was doing research about Islamophobia.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

I don't have anything to compare it to, because I've never lived anywhere other than Quebec. I never thought it was much of a struggle until these laws — personally, I'm not affected by the laws about secularism, so it hasn't changed a lot about how I practice, but when it comes to prayer, the laws are starting to affect everyone, because one of them closes down prayer rooms in universities and CEGEPs. Alhamdulillah, this law came in right when I started at a university that doesn't have a prayer room — my first year there, so this past year has been kind of hard on my faith when it comes to prayer. I was looking for a place to pray, afraid, trying to find places to sneak into, because I'd been spoiled by the prayer room back at my old university. Alhamdulillah I have an office now, because I'm a graduate student, so I just pray in there and I'm no longer on campus that much. In my adulthood I've held on to my community, and I think that's what truly helps and makes being Muslim anywhere very easy. Muslims who live in Muslim-majority countries have community, they're the majority, so holding on to your community, your friends, your family really helps — and it doesn't even have to be in a setting where you talk about or practice Islam, just being with people who think like you and accept you truly helps. My non-Muslim friends are also very open-minded and encouraging — because they know my faith means a lot to me, whenever we go out they make sure it's not at a bar, or if they're going to a bar they won't invite me, they make sure there's a place for me to pray, they worry about my prayers, if my hijab is slipping or my hair is showing they'll fix it for me. Being with people who truly understand and accept you, whether they're Muslim or not, really helps in being Muslim, not just in the West but anywhere.

I think a lot of women have had really bad experiences. Alhamdulillah, I haven't had many. I think the worst that's happened to me is crazy people talking to me on the metro, saying Islamophobic things or sexualizing me because I wear hijab. I'm not going to pretend my experience is the only experience, but alhamdulillah I've had a good one — I'm not very affected by the laws, though I still think they're very discriminatory, they shouldn't stay, they don't have legitimacy. I've been Muslim in Quebec, my sister has grown up Muslim in Quebec, and we've had good experiences, so I don't think it should discourage Muslim women from living in Quebec or from starting to wear hijab if that's where their journey takes them. But I do know some women — I don't know if it's a lot — have very bad experiences being visibly Muslim in Quebec, and some women don't at all.

## HAVE SPECIFIC LAWS (BILL 21, BILL 94, BILL 9, ETC.) PERSONALLY AFFECTED YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s04 | question_id: followup__have_specific_laws_bill_21_bill_94_bill -->

There are three laws. The first was Bill 21, which basically does not allow anyone who wears religious signs to work in a government job in a position of power — this affects lawyers who represent the court in Quebec, judges, police officers, and, really importantly, school teachers, because it's a female-dominated field with a lot of Muslim women teachers, in high school and elementary school — it's a very big community, and the educational system is already underfunded and understaffed. The law came into effect in May, and from that point no one could be hired into one of these positions of power representing the government of Quebec. People who already had a job could stay, protected by a grandfather clause, but they couldn't be promoted or change positions — if you're a woman teaching third grade, you'll teach third grade for the rest of your life; if you're a sergeant in the police force, you'll be a sergeant for the rest of your life. You can't switch schools even if you move far away. There's a real focus on education because that's where Muslim women are concentrated, but it's affected a lot of other fields too — anyone studying education who was a Muslim woman wearing hijab, that dream was dead to them unless they worked at a private school.

Then Bill 94 came into effect, after they found that one or two cases in the entirety of Quebec didn't follow the rules, and made this big law focused on the educational system: no one who works or volunteers in a public school in Quebec can wear a religious sign — teachers, psychotherapists, speech therapists, parent volunteers (and the Quebec educational system relies heavily on parent volunteers because it's understaffed), library volunteers, volunteers who take kids to field days, janitors, school lunch staff. It's truly erased religious signs from the view of children — supposedly to protect them, because Quebec has a long history with the Catholic Church being restrictive and oppressive, and when Quebec liberated itself from the Church it made everything secular. Quebec is secular, Canada is secular, but we had an open secularism that allowed people to be who they are and live, without laws dictated by any church. Now it's honed in specifically on Muslims, I think, because of our portrayal in the media — Quebec is openly trying to be like France, to remove religion from everything, and this shows up specifically targeting Islam because the media only ever talks about Muslim women when discussing religious signs and secularism, saying very dehumanizing things, and they have since 2012–13, when they were trying to establish the first law, the failed Charter of Values.

Bill 94 also prevents students from wearing niqab at school. And Bill 9, the newest one, prevents every student in CEGEP and university from wearing niqab, and extends the restrictions to any school that receives government funding, even religious schools, and to daycares — Muslim women who wear hijab can't wear religious signs in subsidized daycares. It closed down prayer rooms in CEGEPs and universities, and limited the halal menu at cafeterias — some schools have halal, kosher, vegetarian menus, and now you can't fully ask for one, I can't remember the exact rules on that. It made public group prayer illegal, so you can't pray in public in a group. The thing with these laws is that legislators didn't really think through how they'd be implemented, so they're still trying to figure it out — but yes, that's how they appear.

They're relying on school boards to enforce these laws — yes. There hasn't been a case where a board refused, but [ENGLISH-LANGUAGE SCHOOL BOARD 1] sued the government over Bill 21 — they still comply, but they've taken part in a lawsuit now in the Supreme Court, already debated, and we're just waiting for the verdict, it's been months. No school board has failed to comply. In cases where people haven't respected the laws, those specific professors or teachers are just fired.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I've worn hijab for almost four years now. My story was very spontaneous. I was sitting down — I was not a religious person, I was not practicing, unfortunately, almost at all. I did not dress modestly, I did not pray, though I did fast. I had a lot of struggle with religion and didn't care that much — I had some feeling of guilt, but not enough to change it. It was a Tuesday, I was writing a paper — I do my research on Islamophobia, so I was writing about Bill 21 when it was fairly new — and I couldn't pray because I was on my period. I felt this feeling that I really wanted to do an act of worship, and I was like, oh, I can't pray, I really wanted to go back to prayer in that moment. There was a Quran in front of me, and I wasn't informed enough to know you can touch the Quran on your period — I'd been told the main opinion is that you can't. So I couldn't read it, and I just thought, what if I start wearing the hijab? And that's what I did, alone in my house, and I started wearing it the next day, but the decision was made that same day. As for what it's done for me — truly, everything, because the moment I started wearing hijab is the moment I really started my religious journey of knowledge. I started to learn more about Islam because I told myself that the way wearing hijab in the West works out, it's really only materially negative — everyone assumes I'm subject to Islamophobia, dehumanization, and also assumes I'm a religious woman — so I told myself, since I'm putting myself in that position anyway, I might as well be ten toes down and start learning and practicing my religion properly. Since then, alhamdulillah, it's been going so well.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s06 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Yes, a lot. I think people have a lot of misconceptions about hijab and Muslim women — people think we are oppressed, brainwashed, that we don't have agency, that we're submissive, that all women who wear hijab are one type of woman — quiet, submissive, kind of childlike, reduced to a childlike state because we wear it. That's part Islamophobia, part misogyny, and it's not true. We're all very different people, with different opinions — even though what we share is belief in Allah and Rasulullah and wearing hijab because of it, the reasons why we wear it are different, our religious opinions are different, our personalities are different — we can be strong, loud, bubbly, we can also be quiet, creative, alternative, anything. At the end of the day, the way I see it, hijab is just an adornment you wear because you believe in it enough to wear it, but it doesn't change anything about who you are on the inside. And while it's true some women are oppressed, that's true of every woman, Muslim or not — we're all oppressed by the patriarchy. We're old enough to make our own choices and think for ourselves, and it doesn't necessarily have anything to do with men or anyone outside of God. One of the biggest misconceptions is that we wear hijab just for modesty, just to be protected from the male gaze — that's a really dangerous misconception, because it takes away from our agency and makes it sound like we can't think for ourselves. It really diminishes what hijab truly is, which is an act of worship to God — it's about you and God, about being recognized as Muslim, about altering the beauty standards. The way I see it, Islam is not just haram and halal, it's a lifestyle that gets you to detach from material things and focus on the immaterial, on the afterlife to come — kind of how Buddhist monks shave their heads and live in a monastery with very little clothing. We wear the hijab to divert away from material things, from beauty standards, to focus on what's truly important — our relationship with God, helping people, the community.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s07 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

I already had the basics — my mom put me in Quranic school, and my parents taught me religion — but I wanted to rework the basics, and I'm still on this journey. I started to wear hijab at a time when I saw a lot of people converting to Islam online, and I decided to pretend I was a revert and start truly from the basics — the five pillars, understanding the importance of the Shahada, prayer, fasting for Ramadan, and having a relationship with the Quran, because the Quran is the word of God, the one thing we know for sure is His word, so we should have a relationship with it. I had never fully read the Quran, even though I'd been Muslim my whole life — I didn't even know what religion I was truly participating in. So I started with the basic knowledge anyone curious about Islam would want — why people pray five times a day, what the Shahada means. I'm still working through it, it's a big text to digest, but it's made my first connection to God the Quran itself, and not other people's interpretations of Islam, which has really weeded out a lot of the bad things that come out within our community.

I have a Quran class, so I memorize with a class and have a teacher, but I also do a big part myself. I read from a Quran that has the Arabic, transliteration, and a lot of context — pages and pages of historical context. I started reading the Clear Quran, which is a very good resource, but I found it lacking in places — when you don't know the context, some of what you read seems a little unfair, especially since I started from the beginning, from Al-Baqarah and the surahs that came later, which are more about rules, wars, and history. As you know, Islam introduced the Meccan surahs first, to get to know God, and the rules came later, but I started the other way around, with the rules coming first. My Quran offers a lot of social and historical context — it makes links to the Bible, to Greek philosophers, to Shakespeare, so it really connects to a more contemporary reality.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

Right now I'm struggling with finding a job. I have little jobs, so I have a steady stream of income, alhamdulillah, but it's still very small. I'm used to having multiple jobs at the same time for a bigger income, and being busier — right now the job that's usually my main job, I don't have, so I'm underemployed, still struggling to find one. The way I make sense of it: alhamdulillah, I live with my parents, I still have some income, I'm never truly in a place where I'm really struggling, I feel very blessed, and I remember that whatever is meant for me will come for me — God has a way of always providing some stable income even when I don't have a stable job. It is a struggle at times, because I'd love to have some routine, something steady, some security, but I make a lot of du'as and remember that at the end of the day, I am safe in the hands of God.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

Yes, a lot — internal and external. We have expectations from our community and from outside it, and it's truly a lot of pressure, because we have everything coming at us — Islamophobia, patriarchy, and the two coming together, an intersectional experience. Within our community, depending on where you are and who you turn to, we're not fully supported when we talk about the misogyny within it, and we don't really have a space to do that — if we want to talk about Islamophobia, Islamophobes say "but you're oppressed within your own community too." So we're oppressed within our community and also oppressed outside of it as Muslims — it's a struggle to balance everything. I think the best way to fight it is by doing it ourselves, fighting both the misogyny in and out of our community and the Islamophobia that affects us, our men, and our children.

There's a lot of pressure surrounding the hijab, because it's the factor that shows you're Muslim — you get a lot of looks, and because people know you're Muslim, people feel comfortable coming to tell you what you're doing wrong, whether outside the community or within it. If someone feels you're doing something haram, whether you are or not, they feel comfortable giving you "advice" — and I put that in quotes, because it's not necessarily well-meaning or useful. We get policed on our bodies, in the community and outside it, because we're visibly Muslim, and outside the community people feel free to victimize us because we're women and obviously Muslim, and say whatever comes to mind. There are good experiences, where people ask about Islam in a nice way and you have interesting conversations. But there are also experiences where you get hate-crimed, dehumanized in a conversation you never asked for, when you're just trying to get home. The eyes on us, and people thinking they can police our bodies because we wear hijab, is very difficult, especially because it comes from inside our community and outside it both.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s10 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

It depends what my role is in that case, but generally I feel responsible towards my family. I feel like we owe something to everyone — I don't have a very individualistic mindset, I love community, I love helping out and being there for people, so I do feel a sense of responsibility for a lot of people around me. I guess that informs what I do now in my career and education, but when it comes to my own personal actions, I only feel responsible towards God. If I wanted to take off the hijab today or tomorrow, I wouldn't care as much about what people think or say, or my responsibility towards them — when it comes to my personal actions, I only feel responsible to God. But I do think we owe each other something — community, respect.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s11 | question_id: role_family_community -->

There's not a specific one, I just use my skills to help in any way I can. With my family, I'm very present, emotionally and physically as much as I can be — they're people I care about, so I want to be there for them and help them as much as they need. It comes from a place of care rather than obligation. I know I'm a very vocal, opinionated person, so my role comes out a lot in advocacy — I'm involved in a lot of it, because it really takes nothing from me except time to be vocal about the things I care about and for my community.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s12 | question_id: belonging -->

I wouldn't say I feel like I belong in the Quebec community as a Quebecer — I would say I am a Quebecer, but it depends who's asking. Seeing as we're very obviously not accepted by a lot of Quebecers, when people ask where I'm from, I give them my parents' nationality, even though I was born here. But if someone told me I'm not a Quebecer, I'd say I am, I was born here, you can't tell me what I am. I don't have much attachment to the land itself, or the culture — I just don't really care that much.

Very difficultly, in a very difficult way. I'm blessed to have had few bad experiences, but seeing people be so Islamophobic has brought me closer to my faith — that's something that happens when you feel you're not accepted, you hold on to your community. It's brought me there, but what's kept me there is Islam itself — I don't feel like I have trouble reconciling the two, because I am who I am, and you can take me or leave me. There are a lot of Muslims in Quebec, I live in a big city, so it's not like I'm the only Muslim in a small village where everyone else is Quebecois — I don't feel too much trouble reconciling them.

I don't know if they accept me as Quebecoise, and I don't care if they do. I know the Quebecois I surround myself with do. I don't even know if I come off as Québécoise — even my accent isn't very Québécois, so maybe they don't accept me because of how I talk, and maybe they do — but their acceptance doesn't change the fact that I am Québécoise.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s13 | question_id: akhirah -->

It shapes fully what I think. Whenever I'm going through something hard, I remind myself that this life is temporary and very short compared to the Akhirah — whatever trouble I'm going through, I'm going to die in a few years, inshallah, maybe ten, twenty, thirty, it doesn't matter, even if I live to be a hundred it's not a long time on this earth. That helps me get through the bad things, and it also helps me truly enjoy the good moments, because I'm not going to be here long, and life can be so beautiful and so painful. Because we have an afterlife, I can enjoy the time I have and also get over whatever bad thing I'm going through. The fact that there's a hell and a heaven really helps me navigate things — for example, the genocide in Gaza. One of the things that's helped me navigate it is knowing there is a hell for people committing this genocide, and the victims will have their justice, in this life and the next. The concept of martyrdom helps reconcile the feeling of helplessness we have, being so far away, unable to do much — I'm involved in advocacy for Palestine, but you can make all the effort in the world, and if the people in power don't listen or care, it won't happen in the time you want. It helps me to think that the moment their blood touches the ground, they're transported into the feeling of heaven, they see what heaven is like, they have their justice in the grave and will have it in the afterlife, and the people committing this will be punished for it. It really helps with the whole justice of it.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s14 | question_id: qadr_control -->

I have a lot of trouble with that, because I do love control, but I try to tell myself that everything is beyond my control, in a good way — that I just have to learn to let go, that the only thing I can control is myself, and I will do my best, and what happens, happens. You just have to believe that God truly loves you and will take care of you — you have to hold that belief and try to let go, and that's a lot easier said than done. I still struggle to implement it in my life.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s15 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

I really hope the laws are reversed, and that we can openly (practice) — because where it's going in Quebec, they're trying to erase Muslim women, to erase religion from the public eye. I feel nervous, and I really hope it doesn't get as far as it has in France, because I know that's their goal, as they say very openly.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s16 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

I do believe in laïcité, just not the way it's done in Quebec — the way it's done in the rest of Canada. I think it's good to separate religion from the state, but the laïcité present in Quebec is what we call, in the rest of Canada, "une laïcité ouverte," an open secularism, where the state and the church are separated but people are free to practice however they want, or not practice at all. The closed secularism, "laïcité fermée," seen in Quebec, is based on discrimination and Islamophobia, because the entire media discourse around why we should adopt secularism only ever talks about Muslims and Muslim women — it's discriminatory, it's really bad for society, because outside of discriminating against Muslim women, it also removes religion from education. There's a curriculum reform too — we used to have a program called Éthique et culture religieuse, ethics and religious cultures, which taught us about different religions, gave us the basics, let us know other religions and other people aren't evil. That's been changed to Culture et citoyenneté québécoise. Taking that away really takes away the knowledge part, because now, especially in regions without many Muslims, all people know about Islam is what they see on TV — that we're oppressive, terrorists, basically Orientalist views. I see this type of secularism as really bad, but otherwise I love the concept of open secularism we have in the rest of the provinces in Canada.

## WHAT IS THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN YOUR HEART, INTENTION, AND OUTWARD PRACTICE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s17 | question_id: heart_intention_practice -->

I try to connect with God while I do these practices. Sometimes it's not very easy or obvious, but when it does work, when I do feel a connection with God as I'm doing an act of worship, I feel very good inside, like my soul is elevating. (My intention is) to worship God, so it helps inwardly — when you change your intention towards anything, even something worldly like work, if you do it with the intention to worship Allah, worldly things won't affect you as much, because your intention has changed, and barakah comes out of it.

## WHAT DOES SUBMISSION TO ALLAH MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s18 | question_id: submission -->

Submitting to His will, to whatever happens — that is what happened, it's out of my control. This is primarily what I struggle with, accepting the lack of control, so submitting to Allah, to me, means releasing control, letting go, learning to let go a little. It's kind of like the secular idea of manifestation, leaving it to the universe, except I'm leaving it to God.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s19 | question_id: liberation -->

I think none of us will be liberated until all of us are liberated — from all the systems of domination, imperialism, patriarchy, capitalism, white supremacy. Until we're collectively liberated, we won't be liberated individually. In the meantime, until that happens, inshallah, detaching from the world helps you feel liberated. It's a mix of the two — individually trying to detach from worldly possessions and material things, while at the same time seeking a collective liberation from the systems of social domination.

## DO YOU FEEL OPPRESSED BY THE STATE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-023_s20 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_oppressed_by_the_state -->

Yes, they are (oppressive). The laws take away from Muslims, not just from people who wear religious signs — they take away choices in life, the ability to participate in society, to have a job. They take our rights away, so by definition it's oppressive. And paired with the media discourse, it leads to a dehumanization that's also oppressive — it's not just the government or the laws, but the discourse around them that allows these laws to be adopted, and that's dehumanizing and oppressive too.

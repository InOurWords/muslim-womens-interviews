## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I think for someone who has never met a Muslim, I would say everything we do revolves around intent. Being Muslim first and foremost means someone who has submitted their will to God, so whatever we're trying to do, it's for the will of God. If I were to go more into our religious beliefs, I'd say it revolves around being conscious of the fact that there is one Creator watching what we're doing.

I would say we're a very closely-knit community — family matters a lot, the community matters a lot, making sure we're helping each other out. Charity projects are very common, and looking out for the vulnerable — we volunteer at women's shelters a lot, homeless shelters a lot. It's something that's definitely very important, looking out for one another and making sure no one's in need. One other thing that's quite important to us is that our morals are reflected within our communities — if we think an action isn't aligned with our morals, correcting it is also important.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

I think it made me practice more vigilantly, because especially at the Christian school I felt like I was the only one — the only Muslim, the only hijabi — so I felt the need to show a good example. If I was about to do something disrespectful, or that didn't align with my values — say I'm at the metro and my classmates are there and I forget my [TRANSIT CARD], even though it's relatively common for someone to jump over the bars, I wouldn't do that, with the only fear being that people are watching and might blame all Muslims for what I'm doing. That was constantly in my head, because I was always afraid that if I did something, they'd equate it to all Muslims, which I've seen happen. So I was super vigilant of the fact that I was the only one, that I needed to showcase my religion properly — I always felt that pressure.

I became hijabi when I was eleven years old, and I was at that school until I was fifteen — my elementary and high school were the same school, so around grade six. In school, yes — I never had any particularly disrespectful incidents. Maybe later on, in the last years of high school, we had a few teachers change, so it got a bit more difficult, but in the first year I was wearing it I was very comfortable. I remember my teachers complimented me a lot, because it's also part of their faith, and even though they don't always follow it, they recognized that, and praised me for it. My first years, when I was eleven, twelve, thirteen, it was okay, I didn't face any discrimination — that came later, but by then I was very proud of my identity, so it didn't affect me mentally, I'd say — emotionally, maybe.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s05 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

So this is kind of unconventional, but I'm from Pakistan, and in Pakistan it's common to wear — not on your head, but a shawl on your shoulders. I remember in elementary school, before I started wearing hijab, I went to Pakistan and saw everyone wearing it, so I started wearing a shawl around my neck or shoulders. But at school we had a strict uniform and weren't allowed to wear shawls, and I did not feel comfortable at all without it, because I was so used to having something on my chest. I didn't cover my hair at the time, but I did not feel comfortable without my shawl, and they told me to take it off because it wasn't part of the uniform. However, I remember on the agenda — they'd give us a lot of pamphlets on rules — there was a line that said you cannot add anything to the uniform unless it's for religious obligations. So I actually took my shawl and put it around my head, because then I'd be able to cover my chest.

It was something different to them, and I had no familial influence either — no one at school, no family influence — I just didn't want to be without my shawl, so I decided to do it. That's how I started wearing it — at first only at school, because I wasn't allowed to wear my shawl otherwise, so I'd drape it over my head and cover my shoulders that way. Then slowly, as I started researching more about Islam, I became more and more attached to it, so within two years I started wearing it full-time.

I think the most important part is that it sets certain boundaries for whoever meets me. I'm happy that without even meeting who I am, someone can tell I'm Muslim, and there are boundaries they'll keep. For example, if I'm about to buy something that has gelatin in it, I've had experiences where the cashier will stop me and say, hey, just so you know, this has gelatin, are you comfortable with it? There are so many examples where someone identifying you as Muslim really helps. Another example I really appreciate is that if I see another hijabi woman on the street, she'll always smile and say salaam, and that can really make your day — you could be feeling really down, but if someone smiles and says salaam, it lifts your mood. That's how I feel, personally.

Definitely being recognized as such. I also think sometimes someone will come up to you for help just seeing that you're wearing hijab, so there's some sort of bond. At university, at college especially, that's the first way I'll make friends, or people will be friends with me — they see my hijab and they see what values I have, and in friendships it's very important to know where your values align. Maybe the most important point is also that it's a constant reminder to me of who I am, what my values are. I remember in middle school, grade seven, grade eight, we'd have picture day, and I'd see everyone with their hair out, someone would dye their hair or make new braids for it, and I'd think, oh, I wish I could do that too — but then I'd remember, hey, I don't come to school for this reason, I'm not here to show off, and I'm also not in this dunya to show off. I come to school to study, and I'm in this dunya to pass a specific test. So it was a constant reminder of why I'm here, and that's the most important part — it's a reminder of who I am, and a reminder to others of who I am too, and I think that's what it says in the Quran.

Well, this story is from about ten years ago, when I was in high school, so I don't know if they've had to change it now — it was also a private school, so I don't think the same legislation applies, so of course it's very different. I do worry, though, that if they continue making these regulations stricter and stricter, about my younger sister, for example — she's in elementary right now, in public school, so I do worry about what changes might come. But I'm happy in the sense that that one line made the whole difference for me — the fact that I could wear a certain symbol if it was religious, giving that permission really changed my life, a very important aspect of it. I'd say it was such an important part of my journey, and the fact that others might not have it — my sister might not have that same encouragement, that you're allowed to wear what you want for religious reasons — it's concerning.

Well, they don't apply in public schools, not that I'm aware of — they don't apply to students, they apply to teachers. But the way it's been going, it's getting stricter, so it is concerning that something like that could happen to my sister, for the teachers, the workers. Yes, to school, yes, but not as an employee. The rule is that if you got a job in the last two years, you're not allowed to wear your hijab in public institutions — court, the police force, schools, government positions — but if you've been working for more than two years, they're not forcing you to take it off. I have heard of stories where both happen — employees who've been wearing hijab for more than two years, working at a specific institution for more than two years, and still had to take it off because of pressure. But I've also heard stories where they let it go — my mother, for example, works in a school, she's been working for less than two years, and even though she was initially given an email saying no religious symbols would be allowed after a certain date in March, no one forced her to take it off, she was just sent an email. So I've heard both sides of the story. But one thing I'd say for sure, it's definitely becoming harder for Muslim women to get jobs. I got a teaching job at a Muslim school, so of course they weren't going to tell me to take it off, but I've had friends who've applied for jobs and found it harder to get hired — not necessarily because the employer or HR is racist, but because they're concerned, they see these laws being passed, don't know what's going to happen, so they don't hire you.

Yes, (my mother is) working in a public school. It shouldn't be (up to the school) — the government wouldn't want that, the government would want its rules followed, but I feel like they haven't come up with anything to enforce it yet. Personally, my mother has a good relationship with the school, she's done a lot for it, she's close with the administration, and she knows they would never ask her to take it off.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s07 | question_id: misconceptions -->

Oh yeah, for sure — I've had a lot of instances where people assume certain things, like that I'm oppressed in some way, or being forced to wear it. I've had this conversation with many people — my family never influenced me in any shape or form to wear it. My mother wears it now, but when I started wearing it, she wasn't. That's one thing people assume, that I was forced. Another thing people assume is that we wear it to not attract attention from the opposite gender, that they think that's the sole reason we put it on — so I've explained the misunderstanding around that, what I believe are the nuances behind wearing hijab, my own perspective.

Yes, I can give examples. One other thing I'd say is that people assume I'm Arab — I'm from South Asia, from Pakistan, but it's very common for all hijabis to be considered Arab. Perhaps another misconception.

A specific example, yes, definitely, about the part where they assume you're forced to wear it. I remember I was in a friend group and I showed them a picture of my parents — we were somewhere, I was wearing hijab, my mother wasn't, and someone said, oh, she tells you to wear it but she doesn't wear it herself, and I said, no, no one told me to wear it. They were very shocked. I think that's really changing now — a lot more people, I feel, because of all the emerging hijabi influencers online, are starting to realize not everyone is forced to wear it. Social media is really helping with that — it wasn't the same five years ago.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s08 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

It was an interview for a specific program — you have to pass several stages, examinations, grades, and so on, so getting to the interview level was very difficult, with CV checks and all that, and then I did not clear the interview. That was very difficult for me, because I really felt like I had spent the last four years of my life just preparing for this moment. I think in general I'm just not very adept at interviews. How did I come out of it? Well, religion plays a role in all my decisions, so I held on to the fact that things in life might not always go as you plan, but Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala always has a better plan — if He closes one door, He'll open another. One thing I really believe is that sometimes you want something really badly, but it's just not good for you, and you might not see why. Holding on to that belief, and reciting Quran — that's such an important part of my life, reciting it again and again really calms me down — those two things really helped. And alhamdulillah, two years later, I did realize that program just wasn't right for me, and I'm happy now, alhamdulillah.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s09 | question_id: expectations -->

Internal expectations, yeah, definitely — expectations revolving around family, family values, are placed on us in the Muslim community. Having children, having a family, I think that's an expectation placed on women. Other expectations — especially regarding hijab, maybe it's more of an issue with the younger community, but you're expected not to mess up, not to make the mistakes everyone makes, lying for example. But again, with knowledge, with social media, it's changing.

I do think so, yes — they expect us to be a certain way too. I had a conversation once where someone said, oh, you have a very bubbly personality, you're very loud, and I said, oh, okay, and she said, because I always expect a woman to be quieter and things like that. I don't know where she got that from. So there are stereotypes about Muslim women, both within the community and from outside it — people expect us to be a certain way, but it hasn't impacted me in any significant way, I'd say.

Have I resolved internal expectations? I don't know if I'd say I've resolved any. External expectations, it's kind of easier, because you can explain to them what's halal and haram, what we have to do and what we don't, so if they think you're supposed to be a certain way, you can say, no, this is what I believe. But you can't always say that to another Muslim, because they're part of your religion too, so you'd think they'd already know.

Like I said, I don't think they've impacted me, so I wouldn't say it's something I have to resolve — any expectation someone has on me, I have my own expectations for myself, and the expectations my family has, that's what matters to me. I have to follow the Quran and Sunnah to the best of my ability — after my religion, my family comes first, so it's about pleasing them, I wouldn't care much what the wider community has to say.

## HOW AND WHERE ARE YOU ABLE TO PRAY DURING YOUR DAY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-024_s15 | question_id: followup__how_and_where_are_you_able_to_pray -->

Yes. However, before, they've always let us pray — we have a prayer room, it was amazing, and they let us rent out a bigger room for Jumu'ah on Fridays. However, recently the government has tried to close down our prayer room, I think twice during the semester, and more times at other universities I've heard of. At our prayer room specifically, news reporters tried to come in and ask us questions about whether it was closing or not, and there was talk of it happening, to which the MSA, the Muslim Student Association at my university, said they'd hire lawyers if need be. There's been talk of it closing, but I haven't had any experience where I wasn't allowed to pray in the prayer room.

Not the university — the Muslim Student Association, we have our own student groups within the university. I don't think [UNIVERSITY 1] itself would hire lawyers to fight our case, it would be the MSA or [STUDENT SOCIETY 1], the student society, the group that represents students, not the university itself. There have been discussions, but I'm not part of the administration segment of the MSA — there's a hierarchy within it, and I'm not part of those who communicate with the university, but I know they've had talks, I don't know what came of it. I think [UNIVERSITY 1], universities in general, the Muslim population is so large that they won't be successful in shutting it down — people would really pull together resources and get lawyers. But the case for smaller colleges and CEGEPs, which in Quebec are the two years after high school before university, is harder.

In CEGEP it's harder, especially for smaller CEGEPs. The CEGEP I went to, [CEGEP 1], there were very few Muslim students, and we had a very small room to begin with — maximum six people could fit, for a student population of maybe fifty to seventy Muslim students. It wasn't called the Muslim prayer room, it was just called the prayer room, and we had Bibles, Torahs, all religious texts, prayer mats, Catholic rosary beads, and the misbaha that Muslims have too — we had everything, though people from other religions rarely came, I saw Christians there maybe twice in the two years I spent there. What happened was that it was called the multi-faith room, but then they changed the name to the meditation room, because there could no longer be symbols of any religion — that's the law from Quebec, you cannot show religion openly. It wasn't successful at [UNIVERSITY 1], I think because of the huge population there, but at [CEGEP 1] they tried to get it shut down and weren't successful, so instead you can pray in it, but you have to keep all the symbols away — you use the mat, but have to put it away in boxes on shelves. The religious books also have to be kept covered, they can't be on display, and the same goes for the rosary and the tasbih.

Yeah, actually, before I came to the college we had a larger room, and then they shifted us a few years later. The MSA tried to contest it, but we were unsuccessful. You could see there was a need, because we would line up outside before prayer time — in the winter especially there were long lines, so you can't say there wasn't a need. But we were still very thankful for the small space we had, because we knew that in French colleges and universities — the ones I've attended were English — there is no prayer room at all, and you're not allowed to have a Muslim Student Association.

[UNIVERSITY 3], [UNIVERSITY 4] — they don't have an MSA either. But [UNIVERSITY 2], [UNIVERSITY 1], [CEGEP 1], [CEGEP 2], [CEGEP 3] all have prayer rooms. I don't know exactly why, but I can tell you for certain it's not because Muslims aren't trying — I have friends at [UNIVERSITY 3] who tried to get a prayer room and were unsuccessful. I don't know if it's because they're more Quebecois than the English colleges — I can't give you a certain reason. The student life animators had the keys to the prayer room, and they were enforcing the fact that everything was hidden.

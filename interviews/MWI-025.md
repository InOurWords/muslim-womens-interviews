# Interview MWI-025

## ORDINARY DAY AND WHERE FAITH SHOWS UP

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s00 | question_id: ordinary_day -->

At the moment I am in summer school and I'm also working, and I have a roommate who's also a hijabi Muslim woman like myself. Usually I'll wake up around 5:30 — sometimes I'll miss Fajr, but I do my best to pray on time most days. I take Arabic classes with a teacher in Egypt, so I have my classes usually at six for an hour, and then either I'll go to work or have class. Depending on where I'm going, I dress a little differently — I've been trying to wear abayas every day when I can, but sometimes I'm not so confident, so I sort of base what I'm wearing on where I'm working that day. Right now I just finished a contract job as an educator, in a reading intervention program for children with autism and ADHD. On days I have class, I'll go to class and then study afterwards, come home, make dinner, pray, call my parents, and then I'll usually scroll or text my friends until it's time to go to bed.

## HOW WOULD YOU DESCRIBE YOURSELF AND YOUR COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s01 | question_id: describe_self_community -->

I feel like a lot of existing as a Muslim woman is really hard to put into words, because I find myself trying to prove myself to my own Muslim community and also to non-Muslims a lot of the time. I would describe myself as a student before anything, and I find that to be a very important part of my faith too — my studies, and trying to establish myself academically so I can build a career for myself that is halal and represents my values. I'm a very ambitious person, and my friends would say I'm someone who wants to change the world — I don't think that's necessarily how I see my day-to-day character, but I find that as I've gotten older, I've become a lot more insecure and struggled a lot more with my long-term goals because of the hijab and because of living in Quebec. In recent years I've also been really involved in activism around the various wars and genocides that have been taking place against Muslims globally, and that's really shaped my identity — it's the first thing I want to talk about if I get to decide where a conversation goes, and I think a lot of people associate me with that. When they get to know me, they usually get to know me based on what I'm studying and my work, which I always politicize very heavily. I think it's hard to balance being feminine and soft, the way I feel my culture often expects me to be, and also being a powerful, ambitious woman, the way I feel is often expected in the West. I don't even know where I fall on that spectrum, because it changes so much based on who I'm around and what I'm doing. But when it comes down to it, I'd say I'm just a student.

Well, I'm Arab, and I'm not necessarily speaking to the Muslim community as a whole, but within Arab culture there's a very strong expectation for women to be soft-spoken, patient, nurturing — expectations of a certain way of carrying myself that are a lot more passive, I guess, whereas in the West there isn't necessarily that same framing of how women should be, there aren't the same social scripts for women. But I'll give so much credit to my culture for the weight it gives to education, to educating yourself and equipping yourself to be productive in society and give back to your community — I don't see that as much here, and that's something I value so much as a person. I see that as the most central thing to myself, wanting to give back to my community and wanting to be educated in order to do that.

I think that we're very collectivist, which people here aren't super familiar with — we care a lot about appearance and how we treat and appear to others, more than we necessarily care about personal successes in career, etc., and we're the most generous and hospitable people ever. I think if more people got to know my community — I'm Palestinian, and I just think there's so much hate going around, assumptions about what a Palestinian is. Of course there are so many different people in every society and culture, but across the board I haven't met a Palestinian who isn't so excited to share their food and share what they have with others. So I would say we're generous.

## HOW HAS YOUR SENSE OF YOURSELF AS A MUSLIM WOMAN CHANGED OVER TIME?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s02 | question_id: self_change_over_time -->

I've lived here my whole life. My family is in Palestine, but I've grown up here — I used to visit them regularly, up until the war started; in 2023 I'd visited maybe five or six times before then. I'd say the hijab definitely changed me a lot, because it changed how other people treated me, which in turn changed the way I behaved. When I was younger, I used to do a lot of sports, I wanted to go to the Olympics, and then I wanted to be an actor — my family never pressured religion onto me at all, although there are people in my family who are very, very practicing, much more strict in their religion than I am, but it was never forced on me in any way. My brother is very different from me, we went in kind of opposite directions. But as I got older and became more religious on my own, I became a lot more intentional in what I do, more selective about who I spend time with and the hobbies and things I invest myself in, so my goals became a lot more focused. I went from wanting to change the world on a really big scale to wanting to help individual people a lot more, and I went into education and working with children because I see that as a really practical way of helping people. I think also naturally, for any woman, once we hit our teenage years we get really insecure, and that shaped me a lot too. Also, just living in Quebec in the past couple of years, I've had a lot of Islamophobic encounters that have really changed the way I behave and see others — now my assumption when I'm meeting people, when I'm out in public, is that people are hostile towards me before they do anything, and that will only change if I have a positive encounter with them, whereas when I was younger I'd always assume the best in others. But I think I've also just become a lot smarter.

## HOW DOES LIVING HERE SHAPE HOW YOU PRACTICE OR SHOW YOUR FAITH?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s03 | question_id: living_here_practice -->

It's really blatant in some ways — there are laws that prevent me from practicing my faith to a degree. I'm a student, and this coming fall, Bill 9 is about to shut down all the prayer rooms on university campuses, so that's going to be really major in terms of how I structure my days at school as a practicing Muslim who needs somewhere to pray on campus. But I think it's also really inhibited me in other ways — I wanted to wear hijab long before I actually did, because I was really nervous about how people would treat me. And even now, I feel like I could easily wear abayas every day, but depending on where I'm going I might not be totally safe like that, and I've had interactions that have proven it to me, so I definitely don't feel great. Even though living downtown, in [CITY 1], is pretty manageable because there's a lot of diversity, there are still a lot of hateful people, and I don't think there's much accountability when they act on that hate. It leaves a lot of us feeling very scared.

I don't know specifically about francophone universities, because I don't go to them, but I do know that, for example, [UNIVERSITY 2] does not have a prayer room. [UNIVERSITY 1], which is where I go, we have one of the oldest Muslim student associations. We have a massive prayer room on both of our campuses, and they also organize Jumu'ah prayers every Friday, and all of these things are being shut down. Bill 9 is basically calling for no more religious spaces on campuses, but there are exceptions for heritage sites — there's a chapel on one of our campuses that's still allowed to operate as a chapel, and the irony of that is not lost on a lot of us. These spaces are very used, people love them and rely on them, and I think it's pretty clear to see, even if it's not written directly, that these laws are intending to target Muslims disproportionately. There's also new legislation now around street prayers and prayers in public, which also has a lot to do with the protests that were happening against the genocide in Palestine, and the fact that there were prayers taking place at those protests, which pissed a lot of people off.

## HAS WEARING HIJAB CREATED BARRIERS IN EDUCATION OR EMPLOYMENT?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s04 | question_id: followup__has_wearing_hijab_created_barriers_in_education_or -->

Muslim women, I feel like we gravitate towards careers in education — I'm not a sociologist, but I see a trend, there are a lot of Muslim women in my program and in this field generally. I feel really disappointed, because the laws here now are saying that no one can wear religious symbols even into schools — even if they're a parent volunteering on a field trip, even a janitor, even a tutor representing the school from their own home, they cannot wear religious symbols. You cannot work in a public school or a subsidized private school with a hijab. And you need certain experience — childcare is one of those careers where you can have a great CV, but if you don't have substantial experience with children, you're not going to get very far, and a lot of the time that means doing a practicum during your university studies, but it also means students who've been working with kids throughout their university career are at a huge advantage once they graduate, compared to those who haven't. I think it's really disadvantaging many students. I was really lucky to get an internship at a private daycare, but it's been really complicated, and I've been speaking with and advocating for other students at my university who need to do their internships and weren't sure where to go. It ended up falling on our department chairs to coordinate exceptions, getting them into private daycares or schools, rather than the university itself. I think that's really disappointing, and it really shows that even if our university claims not to support these bills, we're not really welcome in these programs anymore, which is super sad.

From my understanding, complaints can be made to the government by basically anyone, and that could result in a fine on the institution and likely the termination of that teacher. Other times the institution just won't be comfortable taking that risk, so they'll fire the teacher outright, or won't hire them, or they'll share the government's politics in believing it's a detriment to students' education. We've been in conversations with our university administration since December of last year, asking for details on how they're going to enforce this, and they haven't given us any meaningful response, despite meeting with them almost five times, repeatedly asking, repeatedly going into meetings — we've just been told, oh, we don't know yet. And now these bills are allegedly going to be enforced next month, and there's still no details, so I'm feeling worried and disappointed about that. I don't think our university is planning to sanction students for praying, but if a complaint is made, they're not going to want to pay a fine, so they might have to take action. Nine and 94 are the ones I'm familiar with.

## HAVE YOU EXPERIENCED HOSTILITY FOR BEING VISIBLY MUSLIM?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s05 | question_id: followup__have_you_experienced_hostility_for_being_visibly_muslim -->

I've had people spit at me, I've had people call me a pedophile-worshipper randomly — at one point I was just walking down the street near my house and a man passed me and spat at my feet and said something under his breath. I've had people come at me on the metro, always a man — I had a man corner me and swear at me for three stops straight, getting really close to me, and it was a full metro and nobody did anything, and that reinforced my fear of going out, because I felt like no one was going to protect me if something violent happened. I've also had kids I've worked with say things that have been really disappointing, because I know they're hearing it from somewhere — I had an eleven-year-old with autism last week look at me and say, "I hate Muslims, especially Pakis," and I asked him, do you know that's a slur, do you know what it means, do you know that I'm Muslim? This kid had no idea, it's just something they heard someone say and are repeating, and if they're not given the opportunity to have Muslim educators or interact with Muslims, that's just going to be what they carry with them until they're adults, and that's how they'll treat people. It's beyond disappointing.

In other circumstances I've gone into a daycare for the first time and a toddler will run up to me for a hug, and the manager says, oh, her mom wears the hijab, or another kid will come up and say, I like your hijab, my mommy wears it too — that gives me so much joy, knowing there's that representation, but it's also heartbreaking that it's about to be taken away from so many of these kids, and they're undoubtedly going to have classmates whose parents have told them things. It's not just affecting educators and their careers, it's also affecting Muslim kids. A lot of times it's just a look someone gives you — I think we're all aware enough to know when a look is a look of disgust, just based on what you're wearing.

## DO YOU SEEK RELIGIOUS KNOWLEDGE, AND HOW?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s06 | question_id: seeking_knowledge -->

Yeah, I try to. I don't feel super proud of myself recently, I've kind of let that go, but I used to do halaqas at the masjid I go to, and I have some apps I follow for sort of Duolingo-style Islamic lessons. Also, in my Arabic classes, I see that as a big part of improving my ability to read Quran, because I grew up here, my Arabic isn't as strong as some of the rest of my family. And I just love talking about my deen with my friends and people I come across.

## HOW DID YOU COME TO WEAR HIJAB, AND WHAT DOES IT MEAN TO YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s07 | question_id: hijab_journey -->

I never had any pressure from my family. I wanted to for a long time, but I ended up putting it on only a couple of years ago — I was in university when I did. Funny enough, it was quite spontaneous — I was having a bad hair day, feeling really overstimulated by my hair kept getting in my face, and I was complaining about it to my friend, and she said, well, we're going to have to go pray anyway, let's go to the mosque at school, and why don't you just keep your hijab on afterwards — no one's going to see us, it's a Saturday on campus, I know you've been wanting to, so it's a good opportunity. So I put it on, and as we were leaving the musalla I ran into about twenty of my friends, and everyone was so excited and happy for me that I felt like, okay, I can't bail now, I've locked in — everyone was giving me hugs, being so excited and proud of me. That was the part I was most scared about, people's initial reactions, and I think that's what held me back for years, fear that people I was friends with might not see me the same way — so when I had that major reassurance, it helped me a lot. I just kept wearing it after that. My same friend, bless her, she's been an amazing support to me — she texted me that night and said, okay, I'm going to pick you up at your house for school tomorrow, you better be wearing the hijab when I get there. She helped a lot, because my parents live in a different city.

It makes me a lot more intentional about what I choose to do and how I choose to speak to people. I think it really encourages me to be a smarter person. I feel like there are bigger consequences to my actions and my words. It's weird, I feel this balance — I've said I feel unsafe in many ways here, but I also feel safe in the sense that I notice how it changes the way negative people avoid me, and also the increase in respect I've gotten from certain people within my community.

And my ability to enter certain spaces, to move more freely in certain political spaces because of my hijab, especially as a Palestinian — I think there's a certain ability to move and speak and do things when you're representing yourself as a Muslim woman that you lose without it, especially because I'm quite white-passing. I also just feel really proud of it. There are some days, even two years later — almost three, actually three years in a month — where I'm just sitting minding my business and I remember, oh my god, I can't believe I did that, that's awesome, because I struggled with it for years. When I remember that I'm just wearing it and not thinking about it anymore, I get so happy. It brings me a lot of pride, and I think it's unparalleled how good it feels when someone says salam to you on the street — that feels so good. I like that other Muslims recognize me as a safe space, someone who is understanding and there for them even if we don't know each other. I've never seen a Muslim sister and had a bad vibe since wearing the hijab, because I feel like we're immediately on the same team, so that's nice.

The best people in the world — if I could live anywhere, I would want to be in Gaza. My friends from Gaza, the people I know there, there's just a way they see the world, a way they measure their actions, that gives me chills. And I put the hijab partly because of the war as well — I felt like nobody knew I was Palestinian until I told them, which is ridiculous. People should look at me and see a Muslim, they should look at me and think, that person has a side in this — I couldn't just be wearing my keffiyeh every day, it needed to be more.

## WHAT DO PEOPLE GET WRONG ABOUT MUSLIM WOMEN OR HIJAB?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s08 | question_id: misconceptions -->

I think a lot of people assume that I had this big family pressure and grew into it, or got over it, but my family is so relaxed about religion, very open to different faiths and lifestyles, and I think that played a big part in how resolute I am now, because I was able to really consider every possibility growing up around the religion. I think also, as a Palestinian, there's a lot of belief about "violent Islam" and a lot of propaganda around that, and people are really surprised to know that my culture values education to such a high degree — we have one of the highest literacy rates in the world, and women are very involved in politics, education, social and community building. That's something that really shocks people. When they find out I'm so deeply connected as an adult with my culture and religion, they don't see it as congruent that I'm also very outspoken politically. I think people sometimes assume I'm hateful in ways I'm really not, based on stereotypes they might have come across. To me, Islam is such a respectful, kind religion, and sometimes people assume the hijab means I dislike certain people — and they're often surprised to know that I'm just Canadian too, living here, part of this community and culture, not carrying around every day being like, I hate this person, I hate that person — no, I'm just minding my business, wearing my hijab.

Yeah, especially working with kids, I see this so much — so many kids say, "but you're forced to wear it, right?" That's the main one I get all the time, people assuming I've been forced into the hijab and resent it deeply, and I often have to say, oh no, I'm so proud of myself, I did this, this is my biggest accomplishment. I think another thing is that a lot of people find it hard to believe a woman would want to dress this way. Our culture is also very different in that people have a hard time understanding family dynamics in Muslim culture — the idea of, well, I wouldn't put up with that from my family, I'd set this boundary or that boundary — that's just not really a concept that exists for me. Family is family, we're not setting boundaries and ultimatums, we're supporting each other, helping each other grow, and accepting each other's differences. But the biggest thing usually comes down to how we dress, because people can tolerate and understand a pretty diverse range of beliefs, but once it becomes visible, they start to feel some type of way about it.

## HOW DO YOU RESPOND TO WRONG PORTRAYALS OF MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s09 | question_id: responding_to_portrayals -->

A little bit disappointed, depending on how old the person is — if it's a kid, I usually think, oh, this is a great learning opportunity, but when it's coming from someone my age, I think, come on, you've been here long enough, you should have encountered enough Muslim women to know what's actually motivating our decisions. I don't get hurt by it, but I feel tired — genuinely, sometimes I'm just so tired of people being stupid. It doesn't make sense to me how a lot of people don't see the contradiction in claiming to advocate for women's rights, for women's ability to decide things for themselves, while contradicting our ability to decide to wear the hijab or believe certain things. I think that's really embarrassing for those people, and our governments view so much of it the same way — I don't necessarily blame them, but I think people need a little more critical thinking.

## TELL ME ABOUT A DIFFICULTY AND HOW FAITH HELPED YOU MEET IT

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s10 | question_id: difficulty_and_faith -->

I really struggled to secure an internship so I could set up my last year of studies. I had to do a lot of networking, speaking to people, sending emails to my department and different daycares, just to set up something I think a lot of other girls in my class get on the first try, just to be able to graduate. That was really hard, because I felt so unwanted the whole time, and I feel like our education system is already not doing great, and it's disappointing that we're pushing away so many educators — the same way we're doing with healthcare workers here. I also think I took a really big risk putting on the hijab, because now, the next time I want to go back to Palestine inshallah, I'm much more endangered by the Israeli military — it's a well-known fact that Muslim women are often targeted with sexual violence and general violence at Israeli checkpoints and when traveling across Palestine.

My parents definitely felt some type of way about my choice to do that, and the risk it was going to put on me. I've done research projects on the hijab in occupied Palestine, why it's worn in some places and not others, and I think it really is a big sacrifice in terms of mobility and rights there. But I haven't had to encounter that directly yet, because I haven't been back since I put it on.

I keep just feeling so shocked, because when you really simplify it, at the end of the day it's just blatant discrimination, blocking certain religious minorities from certain careers — that's really what it is. They can frame it as protecting children from radical religious fanatics, but at the end of the day, I live in a place where, because of my religion, I don't have the right to a certain harmless, in fact very beneficial, job for the community and society as a whole. It just shocks me sometimes — I can't make sense of it, because so much of Canadian culture is based on this idea that we're so free and fair and kind and inclusive, and then I can't get a job because I wear the hijab, and so many people just don't talk about it. I'm talking to my non-Muslim friends, saying, well, I can't work in a school anymore, and their immediate assumption is that I was convicted of some crime — I have to explain, no, the government decided I'm a threat to children, and so many of them have no idea, they say, that can't be right, not in Quebec. Really, yes, that's where we live. I really want to leave, but I also don't want to give them that, because I do feel like that's exactly what this is intended to do. Since my family doesn't live in Quebec, I've heavily considered just moving to another province where I'd have fair opportunities, because this is the career I want, this is what I studied for, and I'm not just going to throw it away because in a certain part of Canada it's not permitted.

I'm proud, because I think it's a good test, a test of my faith, and I certainly would not take off the hijab over something like this. I put it on before this law was even announced — Bill 9, Bill 94, I didn't know they were in the works or going to be enforced. So when I put it on, I still had every reason to believe I was going to graduate and get a job in a school, and when it became clear that wasn't a possibility anymore, I just thought, okay, their loss — it's really frustrating and a lot of extra work, but alhamdulillah for my hijab, because I wouldn't necessarily want to work in a province where I don't have the ability to dress how I want. Maybe I would change my mind later down the road and want to put the hijab on, and I couldn't without losing my job — better to know now than to find out or have it progress later. At least I'm at a stage where I can still plan my career and future. There are many women who've had the rug swept out from under them after twenty years of teaching, and I think that's much more vile than what I'm experiencing. But the hijab is one of the proudest things I've done, and I have no intention, I've never even once considered taking it off because of this — not for a second has that occurred to me, because it's just a ridiculous concept, to take off my hijab because of an Islamophobe. It doesn't do anything for me in that sense. It does make me want to leave, but that's about it.

## WHAT INTERNAL OR EXTERNAL EXPECTATIONS ARE PLACED ON MUSLIM WOMEN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s11 | question_id: expectations -->

We all have to assess Islamic teachings on our own — there's no forcing a woman to behave a certain way, and I think a lot of that comes from experiencing things and understanding them, whether through speaking to other Muslim women or not. I felt like when I put the hijab on — I used to do a lot of sports, and suddenly that was this amazing outlet, but suddenly there was so much shame, I was so ashamed to be seen playing soccer with my friends, I could not go to the gym.

Within the Muslim community. That said, many Muslim friends of mine are athletes, go to the gym, do these things, but they've also spoken about experiencing this idea that a woman should not be doing that in public, that it's not modest, and I think modesty is such a broad term, really hard to understand — there's not a specific line, it depends so much on the context of where you're doing something, what you're doing it with, who you're doing it with, what your intentions are, and that's so hard to understand as a young person, especially living around one culture where your friends here say this is normal and cool, and the other culture at home says a woman should not do this. I think many of our parents, if they're immigrants, don't necessarily understand the nuance that it's actually not as objectively bad to do something here, because the cultural context is different — for sure it'd be bad if I did it in a city in the West Bank, but here it's really different. I find myself hiding things from different people in my life depending on how I think they'll respond — with some Muslim friends I'll talk about the soccer game I played last week, or the event I went to, and with other friends or family I'll say I was at home studying, or I don't do these things. That takes a really big toll on someone. It causes so much anxiety — especially when you're growing up here and want to fit in, want to have the same experiences as your friends. I don't think it's that effective, what our community tells us, which is often just a flat no or a flat yes. There needs to be more conversation about that gray area, especially being second generation — it's so confusing. I think we assume young girls know what's modest and what's immodest, and I still don't really know.

I think we're expected within the Muslim community to be better representatives of the religion than non-hijabis, so when we do make mistakes, the social consequences are usually bigger. In terms of Canadian culture and the expectations on us, I think it's a lot more just assumptions that we're powerless, not in control of our lives, and I feel a lot of pressure to prove that wrong. I don't think that's necessarily bad, but I think it causes a lot of people to underestimate us and not give us opportunities we deserve.

## WHO DO YOU FEEL RESPONSIBLE TO, AND HOW DO THOSE BONDS SHAPE YOUR CHOICES?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s12 | question_id: responsibility_bonds -->

My parents. I feel very responsible to not disappoint them, to make sure I set myself up well so they can have a good rest of their lives, especially coming from what they've come from. It's so important to me that what they've gone through has led to an overall good in my life, and alhamdulillah, I really think it has. They've given so much for me, and I hope inshallah I can compensate for that to some degree.

It's hard, because I haven't lived with them for some time while I've been studying, but I call them many times every day, and I run everything by them — decisions about what job I'll take, where I'll live, if I'll sign a lease, if I'll buy a dress, what I'll cook for dinner — I really call them for everything, we're very, very close as a family. My parents really consult with me, they don't make me feel like they're telling me what to do, they guide me through conversations in a way that I feel like I understand the decisions I'm making, and I'm very grateful for that. Long-term, when I think about getting married, I don't want to move far from them, and I don't want to do anything that would disappoint them in a big way — that's always at the back of my mind.

## WHAT IS YOUR ROLE IN YOUR FAMILY AND COMMUNITY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s13 | question_id: role_family_community -->

I see my role as having to transfer a lot of generational information — I try to speak to my grandmother a lot, she's still in Palestine — and I put a lot of pressure on myself to help my community, sometimes to my own detriment. I sacrifice a lot of myself to protect people, a lot of labor to give them opportunities, and especially since the war, that's been such a huge part of how I live with myself here. Within my family, I see myself as a conflict solver — that's kind of the role I've always had, very much a mediator, the person always talking things out, reconciling situations, trying to make sure everyone's on good terms.

## WHAT DOES BELONGING FEEL LIKE HERE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s14 | question_id: belonging -->

In Quebec, I think it just means not feeling scared — really trusting that there's equal accountability for people who might do something hateful towards me, as if they'd done it to any other person. I don't want to feel like an exception, I don't want to feel like there's added legislation specifically for me, whether to protect me or to inhibit me from accessing certain things. I don't think Islam or hijab should be an issue in Canadian politics the way it has been, especially in Quebec. I also think sometimes people don't factor in what makes something inaccessible to a Muslim — if I'm at a job and everyone wants to go out to a bar as a social, I don't think it's talked about, like, oh, you're going to order something that's not halal for the team and not run it by anyone — I think it would be nice if that was considered more. But really, at the end of the day, it's less about stopping all hate crimes against Muslims and more about just actually treating them the way you'd treat any other act of violence. Stop it. Sometimes it really feels like there's this huge exception made for Muslims, and I've always felt there's an exception for Palestinians too, that for some reason violence against us, or laws against us, are seen as justifiable. That's really disappointing.

## DO YOU FEEL YOU BELONG HERE — ARE YOU SEEN AS FRENCH/QUEBECOIS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s15 | question_id: followup__do_you_feel_you_belong_here_are_you -->

Some people do, and then other people say, go back to your country. I think a lot of people presume, when they see someone wearing hijab or a Muslim, that we've come here from somewhere else, and they might not necessarily mean anything hateful by it, but I think even the most well-meaning person doesn't necessarily see us as part of the real Quebecois culture. We just don't really mesh.

## WHAT DO YOU MISS ABOUT PRACTICING YOUR FAITH OPENLY?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s16 | question_id: followup__what_do_you_miss_about_practicing_your_faith -->

I wish I lived somewhere where I could hear the Adhan, where people were praying on the streets, where during Ramadan shops don't close at night — sometimes it's really lonely here, especially during religious holidays, and sometimes just on regular days, I wish my religion was more visible. I get really homesick when I see videos where a masjid is playing the Adhan out loud, and I feel like everything is so closed off here — our masajid are old buildings designed to not look like mosques, because they'll get hate-crimed if they visibly look like mosques on the outside. I think that's just so sad.

I don't know the history of it, but to be fair, it would never end well if a mosque were playing the call to prayer out loud here. In terms of the architecture, the official reason I've heard is that it's in part to protect against attacks, that many mosques are openly community centers, outwardly, I should say — but I haven't done proper research on that. I feel like there's a little bit of a feeling that I'm sort of secretly a Muslim sometimes, because it's complicated to ask for accommodations to go pray or to have a meal I can eat. So sometimes it's lonely.

## HOW DOES YOUR AWARENESS OF THE AKHIRAH SHAPE THE WAY YOU LIVE?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s18 | question_id: akhirah -->

Sometimes it snaps me back into focus — I spend a lot of time scrolling, and I definitely shouldn't. I get these bursts of motivation sometimes, always at the most random time, and I feel like I need to get my life together, that I'm so far behind, doing so many things wrong, and I wish I could leave this place and live in a Muslim country. It also pushes the way I am with my family, how I want to treat them, be there for my parents — I think that's so huge. That's also, I think, the main reason I've persisted so much with my hijab, through a lot of discrimination, when people are really hateful and I've had bad experiences — I feel very confident that there is ajr in everything, that there is for sure a reward in all of the bad we encounter here as Muslims, and undoubtedly it's khair for us, that we were chosen to be here and not somewhere else. I wish I was more consistent in the emotional relationship I have with these concepts.

## HOW DO YOU MAKE SENSE OF WHAT IS BEYOND YOUR CONTROL?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s19 | question_id: qadr_control -->

That Allah is there — I actually feel really calm when things are not in my control, it's when I feel like I could have done something differently that I find it really, really difficult. But that was something my parents really hammered into me from when I was young, that you do what you can and then you leave the rest to Allah subhanahu wa ta'ala, that's it, you've done your part. A lot of that, when you're helping others too, is about your intention — I find that very reassuring. I don't think I've really struggled with it as a concept in Islam, but sometimes I wonder if the reason things are happening a certain way is because I haven't been as good as I could have been — I'm working on that, because I don't think that's the greatest outlook.

## WHAT MATTERS MOST FOR PRACTICING YOUR RELIGION IN THE LONG RUN?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s20 | question_id: long_run_practice -->

I think just maintaining as much of it as I can, not losing or sacrificing anything that Allah has commanded from us for comfort in dunya here. I really don't want to find myself in a position where I look back and realize I've compromised aspects of my faith and how I practice, just to fit in better. So for me it's less about being a representative, and not really so much about establishing myself either — it's really about maintaining. I don't see this as my long-term place, to be honest.

## HOW DO YOU UNDERSTAND LAÏCITÉ, AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT YOU?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s21 | question_id: followup__how_do_you_understand_la_cit_and_how -->

I think it's just the fake way of — I mean, Quebec is founded on the Catholic Church, we have a [PROMINENT CHRISTIAN LANDMARK] at the center of [CITY 1], and our holidays at school are based around Christmas and Easter. And somehow they think it's convincing to tell us that these laws banning Muslim women from places are about keeping religion out of society, about secularism. I just think it's funny — it cannot be real, the fact that they find it convincing. But I don't want to negate the religious trauma people have had in Quebec and say religion should be everywhere — true secularism and laïcité is about removing religion from the government and allowing people freedom of religion outside of the government, and that's the complete opposite of what they're doing. They're making laws around how people should dress and behave religiously — that's the polar opposite of what that entire concept is meant to mean. True laïcité and true secularism is something I'm not at all opposed to — absolutely, the government should have no religious affiliations, I completely agree with that and would encourage it. But what they're doing is saying nobody should be allowed to be religious unless it's a religion they're comfortable enough to have absorbed into the fabric of their community. And because Muslims are seen as outsiders, that's the issue.

## HOW ARE MUSLIM WOMEN ADAPTING THEIR CAREER PLANS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s22 | question_id: followup__how_are_muslim_women_adapting_their_career_plans -->

Most of us are just changing our career goals. A lot of girls are saying, oh, I'm not going to be a teacher then, I'll do that instead, or, I'm going to do grad school in [PROVINCE 2] because I can't stay here and be a teacher — that's the running theme, not so much people saying, how are we going to combat this, although I do know many people who are — I know some really, really smart, mashallah, girls who are lawyers working in activism against this. But for the most part, the ones I've spoken to in my field have just been saying, okay, I'm going to leave, or I'm going to switch to a slightly different sector.

## WHICH PROFESSIONS ARE AFFECTED BY THESE LAWS?

<!-- segment_id: MWI-025_s23 | question_id: followup__which_professions_are_affected_by_these_laws -->

Not at all — it's specific targeting of teachers working in public schools. I can't remember specifically where the line is drawn, but my understanding is it has to do with a balance of power — if you're in a position where you're an educator or holding authority over someone, you're not able to wear it. I don't think healthcare workers are affected. Professors — so far it's okay, I haven't had any — oh, no, I've had one, I had an Arabic professor at university — universities are fine. It was such a shock when Bill 9 came out, because that was the first time they came for public universities and our prayer rooms, but nothing so far on who's allowed to teach. I think that would be extreme for them, and I don't know how they'd garner enough public support, because universities tend to publicly oppose their stance on "secularism," which isn't secularism, and to be honest, their base of support for these bills comes from non-city centers, the less metropolitan areas of Quebec, where there are fewer large universities and student populations. Student cities are not for this.

## WHERE IN YOUR LIFE DO YOU FEEL MOST LIBERATED?
